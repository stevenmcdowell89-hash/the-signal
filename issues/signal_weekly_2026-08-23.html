BAND 02 — THE LETTER FROM THE EDITOR

The ordinary rhythms return.

After a summer bounded by the Strait of Hormuz, the fixture list is back — and the calendar we take for granted turns out to be the hardest thing in the game to make.

For a few months football went quiet and everything else got loud. This week the noise reverses. Arsenal took the Community Shield 3-0, the Premier League and Serie A both kicked off, and on the opening weekend Hull City — promoted only in June — walked into their first top-flight fixture in years and beat Manchester United 2-0. The season is a machine for making Saturdays feel inevitable again.

What that inevitability hides is the labour behind it. This week's Long Read looks at how the fixture list is actually built — some two thousand matches across nine months, solved as one enormous puzzle, where moving a single game can force forty others.

So this is an issue about the machinery of return: the season we were handed this weekend, and the invisible work that hands it over on time.

THE HIDDEN MACHINE
NOT THIS WEEK · A STANDING STORY · 1992–2026 · LATEST DEVELOPMENT AUG 2026

The impossible job

Most supporters treat the fixture list the way they treat the weather — it simply arrives, and a season gets planned around it. In fact 2,036 English league matches are assembled every year by one man and a computer, working through a lattice of rules almost nobody outside the game has ever seen.

BY THE EDITOR · THE LONG READ · A STANDING STORY

Every summer, on a single morning, the whole of English football finds out who it plays and when. The lists go up, the arguments start — a brutal opening month, a derby buried in midweek, three away trips in a row before the clocks change — and within a day the calendar has hardened into something that feels as fixed and impersonal as a tide table. It does not feel authored. Yet it is. Behind those 2,036 matches sits a compile as deliberate as a railway timetable, and for more than thirty years it has been run, in large part, by the same person.

His name is Glenn Thompson, and he works for Atos, the firm contracted to build the schedule for the Premier League and the English Football League. Thompson has been compiling the fixtures since 1992 — the year the Premier League itself began — and the scale of what he assembles is easy to underestimate. Across the top four divisions there are 2,036 league matches to place, spread over roughly nine months, each one carrying its own web of obligations about where it can go and where it cannot. The computer does the heavy arithmetic. The judgement, and a great deal of the tidying, is human.

There is something quietly strange in that continuity. A piece of national furniture that shapes the weekends of millions of people, and a large slice of the country's policing and rail planning with them, has for over three decades run through essentially one desk. It is not glamorous work and it is not meant to be visible; the mark of a good compile is that nobody notices it at all. The list is only ever discussed when it seems to have gone wrong — when a club stares at its first six games and sees a sentence rather than a schedule. The rest of the time the machinery is silent, which is exactly how a timetable is supposed to behave. That invisibility is the point, and it is also why almost no one who follows the game could explain how the thing they organise their autumn around is actually made.

FIG. 01 The top flight plotted on the map — an illustrative 2008-09 snapshot, not the current table. Geography is the first thing the compile has to reckon with: where the clubs cluster decides which of them can be at home together, and which cannot.Wikimedia Commons contributors · CC BY-SA 3.0

Geography is where the difficulty begins. Look at a map of the clubs and the problem draws itself: London holds a knot of them, Greater Manchester and Merseyside each hold two of the biggest, the West Midlands another cluster. A city cannot host every one of its clubs on the same afternoon. The police cannot staff two large home crowds a few miles apart at the same kick-off, and the trains cannot move both sets of away supporters at once. So certain clubs are "paired" — Arsenal with Tottenham, Liverpool with Everton, the two Manchester clubs — and the rule for a pair is simple and unbreakable: they are never both at home on the same weekend. When one is at home, the other is away. That single constraint, multiplied across every city with more than one team, shapes the entire grid before a ball is kicked.

On top of the pairings sits a set of principles the compilers call the golden rules, and they are about balance and rhythm. No club should play more than three home or three away games in any five; no club should ever play more than two home or two away games in a row; and no club should finish its season on back-to-back home or away fixtures. The logic is fairness of a very particular kind — not who a club plays, but the shape of when it plays them. A run of four away trips would hand a club a punishing month through no fault of its own, and a season that ends at home twice over would give one side a soft finish another side never gets. The rules exist to stop the calendar from quietly favouring anyone.

And the compile cannot even start until the divisions know who is in them. Promotion, relegation and the play-offs have to settle first — a club still fighting for its place in late spring cannot be slotted into a league it may not be playing in. Only once the four divisions are finally sealed does the real work begin, which is one reason the lists appear when they do, in the narrow window between the season ending and the next one being sold.

Before the computer is let loose, the humans mark out the immovable dates by hand. Christmas and the New Year are managed rather than randomised — the holiday programme has its own constraints about travel distances and turnaround, so nobody is sent the length of the country twice in three days. The opening weekend is chosen deliberately. The big derbies are spread across the season rather than allowed to fall together. These are the "key dates," fixed first; only then does the software generate the vast remainder, threading every other match through the gaps the rules leave open. The order matters. The machine is not deciding what is important — it is filling in around decisions people have already made.

Changing one match may require 40 other changes. GLENN THOMPSON · FIXTURE COMPILER, ATOS

That line is the whole difficulty in a sentence. The fixtures are not a list; they are a lattice, and every strand pulls on the others. Move one club's home game to accommodate a request and its pair is suddenly at home on the same day; fix that and a golden rule breaks three weeks later; fix that and the holiday travel goes wrong. This is why the schedule looks arbitrary and is anything but. The strange, counter-intuitive truth underneath it is that a twenty-team league has an astronomical number of valid arrangements — the permutations run far past anything a person could sift by hand — and yet the constraints are so tightly interlocked that finding even one list which satisfies all of them at once is genuinely hard. Abundance and scarcity at the same time: countless answers exist, and almost none of them are legal.

Nor is the computer's output the final word. A draft list is reviewed by the leagues and the Football Association, by the Football Supporters' Federation speaking for the people who actually travel, and — crucially — by the police and the British Transport Police, who look at it as a public-order and logistics document rather than a sporting one. High-risk fixtures, congested cities, rail engineering: all of it can send a match back to be moved. Then the broadcasters make their selections, and the neat Saturday-afternoon grid is pulled apart again into Friday nights, Sunday teatimes and Monday evenings. The list that finally goes up is the version that survived all of that.

THE CASE AGAINST · CAN A COMPILE BE FAIR?

A grid built by human judgement is not a neutral one.

Every season some club insists the fixtures have wronged it — a cruel opening run, an unkind Christmas, the wrong games away — and the process gives the complaint somewhere to live. The key dates, after all, are set by people before the computer runs, and the whole exercise is openly described by its own author as a compromise rather than an optimum. "You can't satisfy everyone," Thompson has said of the job. "It's a compromise across all clubs; you can't do anything to favour any one club." The honest reading is that the list is not neutral in the way a coin toss is neutral — it is a defensible balance of competing interests, arrived at by judgement, which is a different and more human thing than fair.

FIG. 02 The visible output of the invisible machine: Brentford beating Tottenham 3-0 on the Premier League's opening weekend — one cell in a grid of 2,036, placed months earlier and now simply happening.The Guardian / Getty Images · editorial

All of which is worth remembering the next time a season reopens. On this opening weekend the promoted side Hull City, who had come up through the play-offs only in the spring, beat Manchester United 2-0 — a result that felt like a small piece of sporting theatre and was, at the same time, the closing line of a very long calculation. That fixture existed on day one because someone decided it should, months earlier, once Hull's promotion was confirmed and the grid could finally be sealed. The upset was the players'. The stage was the compile's. The fixture list may feel like weather, arriving from nowhere and answerable to no one — but somewhere a man and a computer spent the off-season making sure it could rain in the right places.

BAND 09 — BOOKMARK BOOKS · ON THE NIGHTSTAND

  • The Club

    Football · reportage · Premier League money

    Robinson and Clegg, both Wall Street Journal writers, trace how a self-inflicted breakaway in 1992 turned English football into what their subtitle calls the wildest, richest and most disruptive force in sport. It is reportage rather than lament — the boardroom manoeuvres, the escalating television deals, the arrival of American, Gulf and oligarch owners all chasing a slice of a global broadcast machine that now dwarfs every rival league. The reporting is brisk and character-driven, following the executives and agents who built the thing rather than moralising over it. With the season back under way and a promotion place worth a fortune before a ball is kicked, it reads as the backstory to every table the weekly prints — and a useful corrective to the idea that any of this happened by accident.

  • Inverting the Pyramid

    Football · history · tactics

    Jonathan Wilson's history is the standard text on how the game is actually played, tracing tactics from the attacking 2-3-5 of the Victorians through Italian catenaccio and Dutch Total Football to the modern high press. It treats each formation as an argument that the next generation answers and overturns, so the story becomes a century-long conversation between coaches rather than a museum tour. That framing is why players, managers and armchair analysts keep returning to it, and why its title has slipped into the language. Dense in places but never dry, it is the closest thing football has to a grand unified theory of the pitch — and the ideal companion to a weekend spent watching shapes shift in real time.

  • Algorithms to Live By

    Science · popular maths · decision-making

    Christian and Griffiths take the hard problems of computer science — scheduling, optimal stopping, sorting, caching and constraint satisfaction — and show how the same mathematics quietly governs ordinary decisions, from when to stop looking and commit to how to order a cluttered desk. It is the intellectual cousin of the fixture computer that anchors this week's Long Read: how do you satisfy thousands of competing constraints at once and still return a workable answer before the deadline? The writing stays clear without dumbing the ideas down, pairing each concept with the researchers who formalised it. A genuinely useful read that leaves the machinery behind the season's schedule feeling a little less like magic and a little more like something you could, in principle, do yourself.

BAND 10 — THIS WEEK IN HISTORY HISTORY · DATED

On the morning of Monday 21 August 1911 — 115 years ago this week — the Mona Lisa was a well-regarded Leonardo hanging quietly in the Louvre, admired by scholars and largely overlooked by everyone else. By that evening it was gone. A museum handyman named Vincenzo Peruggia, who had worked on fitting the Louvre's pictures with protective glass, lifted the small poplar panel from its place, carried it to a service stairwell, freed it from its frame, and walked out of the world's greatest museum with the painting hidden under his smock. It was, in the end, that simple.

The astonishing part is that no one noticed for a day. The theft was not discovered until 22 August, when staff found the wall bare and at first assumed the picture had merely been taken away to be photographed. Only slowly did disbelief harden into certainty: the most valuable object in the building had vanished, and nobody had seen it leave. The Louvre closed for a week while police searched the vast galleries and questioned everyone who had access to them — including, at one point, Peruggia himself, who was interviewed and let go.

FIG.The gap the painting left: the Mona Lisa's empty place in the Louvre's Salon Carré in 1911, four bare pegs beneath a Veronese, after Peruggia carried the panel out.Unknown photographer · Wikimedia Commons · Public domain

What happened next mattered more than the crime. Newspapers across the world seized on the story, and the theft became a global media sensation of a kind no painting had ever generated. People who had never heard of Leonardo's portrait now knew its face from the front pages. And they came to the Louvre in numbers the picture had never drawn — not to see it, because it was not there, but to stand before the vacant rectangle on the wall and look at the space where it had hung. The absence proved a greater attraction than the painting itself.

For two years the trail went cold. Wild theories circulated, prominent figures fell under suspicion, and the panel stayed hidden while the investigation flailed and the legend only grew. Each fresh dead end became news in its own right, and the longer the painting stayed missing the more famous it became — the sensation feeding on the very fact that no one could find it. All the while the Mona Lisa was not far away at all — kept out of sight by the very handyman who had taken it, waiting for a buyer discreet enough not to draw the attention of the police.

The end came in 1913. Peruggia, an Italian who seems to have persuaded himself that the painting belonged in Italy, travelled to Florence and tried to sell it to a dealer there. The dealer alerted the authorities, the panel was authenticated as the genuine article, and Peruggia was arrested. The Mona Lisa was recovered, exhibited briefly to enormous crowds, and returned to Paris.

It came back a different thing entirely. The painting that returned to the Louvre in 1913 was no longer a scholar's Leonardo but the most famous artwork on earth — and it had become so not through anything Leonardo did, but through the two years it spent missing. A modestly-known Renaissance portrait had been turned into a global icon precisely because a handyman walked out with it under his smock. More than a century on, the crowds still gather in front of it. They are, in a sense, still coming to see the painting that was stolen.

BAND 11 — THE DESK SERVICE

The Session

FITNESS · HYPERTROPHY AT MAINTENANCE

The wearable market is booming — Garmin's screenless $199 Cirqa band landed in July as a straight rival to Whoop 5.0 and the Oura ring, its fitness segment grew 42% year-on-year in the first quarter, and Whoop and Strava now auto-sync activities. But almost everything these devices measure — resting heart rate, strain, sleep, recovery scores — sits to one side of what actually builds muscle. Hypertrophy is driven by mechanical tension: hard sets taken close to failure, enough weekly volume per muscle group, and enough protein to rebuild. A wrist sensor reads none of those three. So land it plainly before the nuance — the unit that grows a muscle is the working set, and the working set is the one thing on the wrist a tracker cannot count.

The reassuring part for anyone lifting without a hard calorie surplus is that a big surplus was never the main lever. For a non-advanced lifter, muscle can be built at maintenance or a slight surplus, because tension — not a pile of extra calories — is what signals growth; the surplus mostly speeds things at the margins. That reframes what is worth watching. A recovery score can say whether to push hard today, but it says nothing about whether last week's sets actually progressed. The number that does is the one written down: load, reps, and reps left in reserve, session over session. A band will happily log the run; the training log has to be kept by hand, and it is that log — not the strain graph — that proves a hypertrophy block is doing its job.

DO THIS WEEK · THE SESSION

Log every working set — load, reps, and reps-in-reserve — for one muscle group this week, then beat one of those numbers next session.

Hypertrophy tracks mechanical tension and progressive overload, not recovery scores; the written set is the only record of whether the block is genuinely adding volume, and it is the one metric no wearable captures.

From Paddock to Terraces · How the Word Travelled
1780The 12th Earl of Derby runs his new flat race for the first time over the chalk at Epsom DownsTHE TURF
MID-1800sAs organised football spreads through the industrial towns, neighbour clashes borrow the word — the local derbyTHE TERRACES

The season is barely a week old and already the fixture lists are circling the matches that carry a charge no league table can measure — the neighbour games, the ones spoken of simply as the derby. It is a word worn so smooth by use that its origins have all but vanished, and the surprise is that they lie nowhere near a football ground. The derby began as a horse race. In 1780 Edward Smith-Stanley, the 12th Earl of Derby, devised an elite flat race and ran it for the first time over the chalk at Epsom Downs, lending the event nothing more elaborate than his own title. The race prospered, and within a few decades the meaning had slipped its bridle: the Derby had grown into a byword far beyond the turf, so that any contest fought with real ferocity — any occasion where the outcome mattered and a crowd bayed for it — could be called one. An aristocrat's private amusement had, almost by accident, given the language its name for the fiercely contested event. From there the borrowing was all but inevitable. By the middle of the 19th century, as organised football spread through the industrial towns and clubs sprang up within walking distance of one another, the word was reached for to describe the clashes between near neighbours — the matches that split streets and workplaces and families cleanly down the middle. A meeting of two sides from the same city or district became a local derby, and in time the adjective was shed altogether, the derby alone left to do the work. What began as a proper noun, the private property of one race on one afternoon, had turned into a common one, portable enough to attach itself to any rivalry heated by proximity. The football version kept everything the horse race had lent it — the sense of an occasion set apart from the ordinary calendar, the certainty that reputations rather than mere points were at stake — and added the particular venom that comes only when the two sides share a postcode, a pub, or a factory floor. The line from a Georgian earl's racehorse to a Sunday grudge match is not a straight one, but it is unbroken: an idea of competition so intense it demanded a name of its own, handed down from the paddock to the terraces and kept there ever since. And so the phrase now reaching for the season's most loaded fixtures — the meetings friends have ringed in red before a ball has been kicked in anger — carries, quite invisibly, the memory of a man who never played the game, a stretch of Surrey downland, and a horse race first run a full century before the sport that borrowed its name had even been invented.
