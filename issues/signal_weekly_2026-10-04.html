BAND 02 — THE LETTER FROM THE EDITOR

A game shook hands with a battle.

One date — the first of October — carried a cost-of-living cut, a samurai saga and a 426-year-old war.

I keep a loose rule: a week has a spine when three unrelated things lean on one date. This one did. On Wednesday, VAT came off domestic electricity across Great Britain; the same morning Sony shipped Ghost of Yotei's new chapter, "Echoes of Sekigahara"; and the real Sekigahara — the battle that name is borrowed from — turns 426 on the twenty-first. A video game and a feudal battlefield, shaking hands across four centuries.

So we open not on the news but on the ground it points back to: a foggy afternoon in Mino province, 1600, and the day a country quietly changed hands. A standing story, not a breaking one — which is why it holds.

The rest of the week fans out from there. The electricity line gets its arithmetic at the Desk; the saga gets its ride-out in Pixel & Byte; and the slower stories — a tariff war hardening into a goods ban — keep grinding on in the Threads.

THE DAY THE COUNTRY CHANGED HANDS
NOT THIS WEEK · A STANDING STORY · 1598–1603 · LATEST DEVELOPMENT MAR 1603

The six hours that made a shogun

On a foggy October morning in 1600, two samurai armies of almost identical size met in a cramped valley in central Japan. Six hours later one man effectively ruled the country — and the thing that settled it was not a charge or a volley, but a single lord on a hillside deciding, at the last possible moment, which army to destroy.

BY THE EDITOR · THE LONG READ · A STANDING STORY

The morning of 21 October 1600 came down in fog over Sekigahara, a narrow basin of paddy fields and low wooded hills in Mino Province, in what is now Gifu Prefecture. Two armies had marched through the night to reach it. By dawn roughly 88,000 men under Tokugawa Ieyasu faced about 80,000 under Ishida Mitsunari across a valley barely wide enough to hold them — close enough that each side could hear the other forming up before it could see anything at all. Within six hours it was finished, perhaps 30,000 of them dead, and a question that had hung over Japan for more than a century finally had an answer. The Battle of Sekigahara was, by any measure, one of the most decisive days in the country's history.

FIG. 01 The battle as later Japan preferred to remember it — a painted folding screen, the Sekigahara Kassen Byōbu-zu, crowding both armies onto a single valley floor. Screens like this were made in the decades after the fight, as the victory hardened into legend.Sekigahara Kassen Byōbu-zu, Gifu History Museum · public domain · via Wikimedia Commons

The century behind these men was the Sengoku, the "Warring States" age — a stretch of roughly a hundred and fifty years during which Japan had no real master and its great families fought each other, province by province, in shifting alliances that nobody expected to last. The warlord who had come closest to ending it, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, died in 1598. He left a five-year-old heir and a council of five powerful lords sworn to govern on the boy's behalf until he came of age. The oath did not survive its author by long. Within two years the council had fractured into two camps, and the camps were really only one argument wearing two faces: whether the Toyotomi settlement would hold, or whether the strongest man left standing would simply take the whole thing for himself.

That strongest man was Ieyasu, lord of the eastern provinces around modern Tokyo, patient, late-middle-aged, and the richest of the five regents. Against him stood Mitsunari, a career administrator and Toyotomi loyalist who had read Ieyasu's intentions correctly and set out to stop him. Mitsunari had the better claim to legitimacy — he was defending the dead ruler's child — but far less raw power, and he knew it. His only route was to assemble a broad coalition of western and central lords large enough to overwhelm Ieyasu before Ieyasu's weight told. By the autumn of 1600 the argument had become a war, and the war had funnelled both coalitions into the same mountain corridor, the road linking the old imperial capital at Kyoto to the eastern provinces. Mitsunari reached Sekigahara first and took the high ground on three sides of the valley. On paper it was the stronger hand — larger wings looking down on an enemy who would have to climb to reach them.

On paper. The fog that smothered the valley at dawn meant neither commander could see what the other was doing, and the fighting, when it began around eight o'clock as the mist started to lift, was a blind, grinding business — spear and matchlock musket at close range, the two centres locked together on the valley floor with neither able to break the other. For several hours it was genuinely even. What Mitsunari could not see, and what mattered more than any volley, was that a large part of his own army was not fighting at all.

This is the hinge the whole day turns on. Several of the lords Mitsunari counted in his order of battle had taken up their positions on the surrounding hills and then simply sat there, waiting to see which way the morning would break before committing a single man. The largest of these was Kobayakawa Hideaki, posted on Mount Matsuo with a force big enough to decide the battle by himself — and, unknown to Mitsunari, already in secret correspondence with Ieyasu. When the moment came, Kobayakawa hesitated, paralysed between the side he had pledged to and the side he had been promised to. By the traditional account, Ieyasu lost patience and ordered his own gunners to open fire on Mount Matsuo — a volley aimed not to kill but to force a choice. Whether or not it happened quite like that, the choice was made: Kobayakawa's men poured down the slope, not into Ieyasu's flank as Mitsunari had every right to expect, but into the exposed side of Mitsunari's own army.

For all the tens of thousands of men crowded into that valley, Sekigahara was settled by one lord on a hillside deciding, at the last possible moment, which of the two armies below him to annihilate. TRANSMISSION №028 · THE LONG READ

The defection did in minutes what hours of frontal fighting had not. Kobayakawa's charge caved in the Western army's flank, and the other fence-sitters on the hills, reading the result rather than the cause, turned their coats in turn. An even contest became a rout in the space of an hour or two. By early afternoon Mitsunari's coalition had come apart, its banners scattering back up the Kyoto road, and its commander was in flight into the hills with the battle — and the country — lost.

The reckoning afterwards was swift and systematic. Mitsunari was hunted down within weeks, paraded through the streets, and beheaded in Kyoto. Then Ieyasu did what total victory allowed: he banished or stripped the lords who had stood against him and handed their confiscated domains to the men who had stood with him — including, pointedly, to the very turncoats whose defection had won the field. Redrawing the map of who held what, and binding the winners to him with land, was how he turned a single afternoon's result into a settlement that would not come undone. The battle ended the fighting; the land grants ended the argument.

FIG. 02 Tokugawa Ieyasu — patient, late-middle-aged, the richest of the five regents, and the man who walked out of Sekigahara effectively ruling Japan.Portrait of Tokugawa Ieyasu · public domain · via Wikimedia Commons

Three years later, in 1603, the emperor named Ieyasu shogun — the supreme military ruler who governed Japan in the emperor's name — and the dynasty he founded held that office for more than two and a half centuries. The Warring States age, with its endless provincial bloodletting, gave way to a long, closed, and remarkably stable peace that lasted until the 1860s. Very few single days anywhere have bought a country so much quiet for so long, which is exactly why the battle has never loosened its grip on the Japanese imagination, or on the steady line of chroniclers who keep returning to it.

The historian Chris Glenn, whose book-length study is among the most detailed accounts of the battle in English, frames its pull in a single arithmetic: a six-hour fight that created a shogunate lasting 260 years. That ratio — an afternoon against a quarter of a millennium — is the thing that keeps the story alive. It is also, on closer inspection, the thing that makes it genuinely difficult. Because for all its consequences, there is a real argument about what actually happened at Sekigahara, and whether the battle decided anything at all.

One reading — the one the legend, and the drama, keeps reaching for — is that Sekigahara was won not on the field but in the weeks before it. In this telling, the real battle was a quiet campaign of letters, promises and bribes, through which Ieyasu bought off enough of Mitsunari's lords that the morning in the fog was almost ceremonial. The outcome was arranged in advance; the fighting merely cashed the cheque. It is a seductive story, because it is a story about treachery rather than tactics, and because the most dramatic moment of the day — Kobayakawa's charge down the hill — fits it perfectly. On this account the whole thing was decided by betrayal, and the thirty thousand dead were settling a bill that had already been agreed.

THE CASE AGAINST · THE OTHER READING

The battle did not decide it — it ratified a decision Ieyasu had already made inevitable.

The honest counter-case is that Sekigahara confirmed an outcome rather than producing one. By the time the two armies reached the valley, Ieyasu had already assembled the larger, more disciplined and more cohesive coalition, and had already secured the loyalties — and the planned defections — that would carry the day. The fence-sitters sat precisely because they could see which way the wind was blowing. On this reading the morning's treachery was not the cause of the result but a symptom of it: lords abandoning a side they had calculated was going to lose anyway. Sekigahara, in other words, ratified a decision; it did not make one.

Both of these can be true at once, which is why the argument has never been settled and probably never will be. A battle can be a genuine knife-edge — one wavering lord away from the opposite result — and also the near-certain product of a man who had spent two years stacking every advantage he could reach. The fog, the fence-sitters and the volley on the hill were all real; so was the patient statecraft that made them matter. Four hundred and twenty-six years to the week after the fog lifted over Mino, the battle is being refought yet again — this time on a PlayStation, where Ghost of Yotei's "Echoes of Sekigahara" expansion drops a fictional warrior into the 1600 line and invites a new audience to pick a side. They will be choosing, without quite knowing it, between the same two stories historians have argued over for centuries: the day a country was won by a betrayal, and the day a betrayal only confirmed what a quieter kind of power had already decided.

The 2026 Formula 1 title is no longer a fight — it is a family argument. Going into next weekend's Singapore Grand Prix, Mercedes hold the top two places in the drivers' championship, and the only live contest left is between the two cars in their own garage: Kimi Antonelli on 302 points, George Russell on 236. That is a 66-point gap — more than two clear wins — with too few rounds remaining for any outside driver to reach either of them. So here is the answer the standings already give: whoever lifts the trophy will do it in silver, and a lead that size settles most of the rest. The only genuinely open question is which shade of silver, and Antonelli's cushion has made even that look close to decided.

Two drivers, one team, one trophy — the fiercest rivalry on the grid now runs through a single garage wall.

Which is the oddest thing about the run-in. The names the season began around are, for the title at least, already spent: with Mercedes locking out the top two, every other driver on the grid is now effectively racing for third. The drama has not vanished so much as moved indoors — from Mercedes against the field to Mercedes against itself, a contest no points table can quite capture because both halves of it wear the same colour. The numbers settle the destination; they leave only the house to argue over who carries it.

  • 106.0

    Verity brings Colleen Hoover to the big screen — and to the test

    Colleen Hoover has spent this decade as one of the best-selling novelists alive, and the screen has finally stopped circling her. Verity, the first major adaptation of her 2018 thriller, landed in cinemas on Friday with a cast that takes the material seriously: Anne Hathaway as the injured, possibly unreliable novelist Verity Crawford, Dakota Johnson as the struggling writer hired to finish her book series, and Josh Hartnett as the husband caught between them. The novel's engine is a hidden manuscript — an autobiography that may be a confession, or may be a lie — and the whole thing turns on whether you believe a word of it. That is a neat trick on the page, where a chapter break can hold a secret for fifty more pages. It is a much harder trick in a cinema, where a camera has to point at something. A Hoover adaptation lives or dies on one question: can a camera keep a secret as well as a page can? The early signs are that director and cast know exactly which question they are answering. Hathaway, in particular, is the kind of casting that reframes a role: an actor who can make warmth and menace share a single look, which is more or less the whole job here. Whether the twist survives contact with a medium that cannot hide a reveal behind white space is the thing worth buying a ticket to find out. Showtimes and release details.

  • The Battle of Sekigahara

    Narrative military history · Frontline Books, 2021 · 216pp

    If the Long Read sends you after the whole of Sekigahara rather than its six hours, this is where to go next. Chris Glenn walks the 1600 campaign from the manoeuvring that preceded it, through the fog-bound morning itself, to the executions and redistributed fiefs that followed — with 65 maps and illustrations and a close read of the arms and armour each side carried onto the field. Glenn does not undersell his own book, and the claim is hard to dispute: most English-language accounts give the battle a chapter, where this gives it a whole book. BAND 10 — THIS WEEK IN HISTORY HISTORY · DATED

On 1 October 1908 — 118 years ago this week — the first production Ford Model T was wheeled out of the red-brick Piquette Avenue plant in Detroit and sold for $850. It did not look like a machine that would remake the century. It was tall, plain, and built to be fixed with a spanner by whoever owned it, on roads that were still mostly mud. What it carried was not speed or style but an idea Henry Ford had been chasing for years: not a better car, a cheaper one.

FIG.A four-cylinder Ford Model T, photographed in 1908, the year the first production car left Detroit's Piquette Avenue plant.Library of Congress Prints and Photographs Division · public domain

The Model T used a light, strong vanadium-steel alloy and a simple four-cylinder engine, and Ford kept stripping cost out of it every year it stayed in production. The moving assembly line that made it legend did not arrive until 1913, at the company's Highland Park works; once it did, a car that had cost $850 at launch fell below $300 by the mid-1920s — within reach of the very people who bolted it together. More than 15 million were built before the line finally stopped in 1927, a figure no single model would beat for decades.

The count matters less than what it did. A car ordinary wage-earners could afford turned the motor car from a rich man's toy into everyday infrastructure, and pulled a whole country's worth of roads, filling stations, suburbs and jobs along behind it. The method Ford perfected to make it — the line, where the work moves and the worker stays put — became the template for twentieth-century manufacturing of almost everything else. It cut the time to assemble a single chassis from more than twelve hours to about ninety-three minutes, and the economics of that speed drove Ford's famous rule about the colour of the car: black enamel was simply the paint that dried fast enough to keep the line moving.

In 1999 a jury of 133 automotive journalists from around the world voted the Model T the most influential car of the century, ahead of the Mini and the Volkswagen Beetle. The original rollout at Piquette Avenue — now a museum — is recorded in the anniversary accounts of that first $850 car.

The opening days of October have a habit of starting things that do not stop. Forty-nine years after the Model T, on 4 October 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1 — the first artificial satellite, a polished metal sphere little bigger than a beach ball whose steady radio beep, picked up on ordinary shortwave sets, opened the space age and the superpower race that chased it. Sixty-nine years on, almost nothing a modern life touches runs without something orbiting overhead.

BAND 11 — (ROTATING)

Two synthwave records in two days — the genre rarely gets a headline weekend, but the first of October hands it a double bill: one of the scene's biggest names and one of its best-kept secrets, out within a day of each other. A neat accident of the calendar, and a reminder that a genre built on nostalgia for the 1980s now has both a crossover mainstream and a thriving underground. As good an excuse as any, then, to kill the lights and turn the neon up.

  • 100.3

    The Midnight come back for the arena

    The Los Angeles duo return with Syndicate (3 October, Ultra Records / Sony), the follow-up to a run of albums that did as much as anyone to carry synthwave out of the niche and onto festival stages. This one widens the circle: Carpenter Brut, the French darksynth heavyweight, and pop writer Bonnie McKee both guest. The singles trailed ahead of it — 'Chariot', 'Love Is an Ocean', 'Digital Dreams' and 'Shadowverse' — keep the band's signature move intact, that saxophone-over-neon melancholy that makes a Midnight song sound like a memory no one quite remembers having. For anyone who drifted away from the genre, this is the record built to pull them back. The headliner.

  • 104.5

    SIXMIXSIX and the midnight screening

    For the other half of the weekend, SIXMIXSIX release 'Videodrome' (4 October) — the one that would otherwise have slipped past unnoticed. The project of German producer Fabian 'Fabs' Schwarz with vocalist Markus 'Fludid' Hammer, it works a more cinematic seam than The Midnight: synthwave built to sound like the soundtrack to an imagined 1980s film, all widescreen synths and VHS grain rather than radio hooks. The title borrows from Cronenberg's 1983 body-horror film, and the record leans hard into that same queasy late-night glow. It is the kind of release that never troubles the charts and rarely leaves the Bandcamp orbit, which is precisely the point of flagging it here. If 'Syndicate' is the arena show, this is the repertory screening down the road — smaller, stranger, and worth the detour for anyone who already lives in the neon. The deep cut.

BAND 12 — THE DESK SERVICE

The Session

FITNESS · BLOCK 3, REST INTERVALS

The clock between sets is doing more work than it looks. For building muscle, the number that matters is two to three minutes of rest between hard sets of the big compound lifts — squat, bench, deadlift, press. A 2024 review and Schoenfeld's earlier longer-rest trial land the same way: resting at least two minutes — two to three for trained lifters — grows more muscle than cutting it short, and dropping under about one minute actively blunts hypertrophy. Block 3 is a muscle-building block, so this is exactly the setting where the long rest earns its keep.

The Rest Clock · Hard Compound Sets
2–3 MINbetween hard sets of squat, bench, deadlift, pressBUILDS
<1 MINactively blunts hypertrophy — save it for isolation workBLUNTS

The reason is almost boring: volume. Short rests feel productive — the burn, the breathlessness, a session finished faster — but they don't give the muscle enough recovery to repeat the load, so reps crater on the back sets and total hard volume, the real driver of growth, quietly falls. Schoenfeld's trial made the point directly: three minutes of rest beat one minute on both muscle thickness and the protein-synthesis signal behind it, with the gap clearest in people who already train. The short-rest efficiency is real — it just buys conditioning, not size. Save the quick turnarounds for the isolation work at the end, where the load is lighter and the cost is smaller.

DO THIS WEEK · THE SESSION

On your main compound this week, take a full two minutes between sets — watch the clock, don't guess — and count the reps you keep on the back sets.

Volume carries the hypertrophy, and volume is reps at load; the two-minute rest is the cheapest way to stop the back sets collapsing. Counting them is the proof it worked.

The one flagged as coming has landed: from 1 October, VAT on domestic electricity dropped from the reduced 5% rate to 0% across Great Britain, worth about £45 a year to a typical household. The zero rate runs until 31 March 2027, when it is set to revert to 5% — a time-limited line, not a permanent one. The cut does not apply in Northern Ireland, where the post-Brexit VAT arrangements keep domestic electricity on the reduced 5% rate.

Set that against the bigger rate picture, which isn't moving. The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% on 17 September — a fourth straight hold, on a 6–3 vote — and doesn't decide again until 5 November. And the giveaway sits three weeks ahead of its own author: Burnham's first Autumn Budget, alongside a 'Rewiring the State' white paper, lands on 28 October, close enough that the shape of this and every other household line could still shift. The saving is small and, because it is baked straight into the unit price, the kind of thing you can bank without ever noticing it arrived.

DO THIS WEEK · THE LEDGER

Note your electricity unit rate from this month's bill, so you have the before-and-after to check the 0% line actually came off.

This is the easiest saving to miss precisely because it is already in the price — no form, no switch, nothing to claim. The only way to confirm it landed is to read the rate yourself.

The tariff war becomes a goods ban

Previously: Canada hit back on 8 September with dollar-for-dollar tariffs on about $20bn of US goods, answering Washington's 50% levies in kind.

The war has now jumped a rung. On 29 September US import bans on Canadian dairy, motorcycles over 800cc and alcohol took effect — no longer a tariff paid at the border but an outright refusal to let the goods in. It is a goods ban, not a travel ban: people and most trade still cross, but these shelves go empty. Next: whether Ottawa answers a ban with a ban, and how far the list grows.

YOU MIGHT FALL INTO the word "shogun," and feudal Japan

Play Ghost of Yotei and you spend hours inside a world built around a word almost nobody stops to translate: shogun. We read it as a rank — the warlord who ran Japan — but it began as something far more specific, and far stranger. The full title is sei-i taishogun (征夷大将軍), and it unpacks, almost comically literally, as "barbarian-subduing great general." Shogun is just the worn-down clip of that mouthful.

FIG. The title in its final form: Tokugawa Ieyasu, who would claim it in 1603, rendered in armour by a later print-maker.LACMA · CC0 · via Wikimedia Commons

The barbarians were real, and so was the first general. In 794 CE the emperor granted the full honorific to a commander named Otomo no Otomaro and pointed him north. "Barbarian" was the court's word for the Emishi, the people of northern Honshu who had never accepted rule from the imperial centre; subduing them was the whole of the job. The title, then, started life as a temporary battlefield commission — closer to "commander of the northern war" than "ruler of the realm." When a campaign ended, the office was meant to end with it, and for a long stretch it did, handed out to this general or that as the frontier demanded.

What turned a frontier rank into the most powerful seat in the country was time and ambition. Only after the 12th century, once warrior houses rather than court nobles held the real power, did shogun come to mean the samurai leader who governed Japan in the emperor's name — the emperor reigning, the shogun ruling. The word carried its old military grammar the whole way. The man who built the longest-lasting version of the office claimed this exact ancient title, in 1603, to dress new power in old legitimacy. The emperor still formally granted it. On paper it still meant barbarian-subduing great general. By then there were no barbarians left to subdue.

That is the fossil worth falling into. A government that ran Japan for two and a half centuries was, in its own founding paperwork, a never-ending military expedition against a northern frontier that had long since stopped being one. The samurai world you move through in the game is quietly full of these — offices and ranks that once meant something concrete on a map and then hardened into pure authority, their original jobs forgotten. Pull the thread on this single word and you land in 794, on a cold campaign against the Emishi, watching a battlefield title take its first step toward the throne — the same throne, and the same title, the game's own "Echoes of Sekigahara" reaches back toward.

THE STRONGEST ACTION · PIN IT TO THE FRIDGE

On the evening of Wednesday 8 October, after dark, step outside, face north toward Draco, give your eyes twenty minutes to adjust, and watch.

The Draconids peak overnight into the 9th, but unlike almost every other shower they are best caught in the early evening rather than before dawn — and this year the sky is near-moonless. Expect only about five an hour, shed by comet 21P/Giacobini-Zinner, with no outburst forecast: a few slow streaks over something warm, not a spectacle.
