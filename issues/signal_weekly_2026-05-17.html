Starmer clings on as Labour splinters

  • Wes Streeting resigned as Health Secretary on 14 May and publicly declared a leadership challenge against the Prime Minister
  • Labour MPs are split 159 in favour of Starmer, 97 demanding he go, 147 uncommitted — no formal contest triggered yet
  • Labour lost approximately 1,500 council seats and 35 councils in the 2026 local elections, recording a projected 17% national vote share

The most significant rupture in Labour's internal order since the 2019 election defeat began not with a vote of no confidence, but with a resignation letter. On 14 May, Wes Streeting cleared out his Health Secretary desk and stood in front of cameras to announce what everyone in Westminster had spent a fortnight pretending was not coming: he would challenge Keir Starmer for the Labour leadership. The question is whether he can actually get there.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. To trigger a formal contest under Labour rules, a challenger needs nominations from 20% of the Parliamentary Labour Party — 81 MPs. Streeting has not reached that threshold as of this issue. The 97 MPs already publicly calling for Starmer's resignation represent the outer edge of declared anti-Starmer sentiment; the 147 uncommitted members are the battleground. Most of them are weighing a simple calculation: is the risk of backing a challenge greater than the risk of being associated with a leader the polling suggests cannot win a general election?

The local elections in May provided the collapse of confidence its own brutal grammar. Labour lost 35 councils and roughly 1,500 councillors. The projected 17% national vote share would, were it replicated at a general election, produce one of the most catastrophic defeats in the party's history. That figure is not a prediction — but it was the number that lit the fuse.

Streeting did not go alone. Jess Phillips, Miatta Fahnbulleh, and four parliamentary private secretaries resigned alongside him in the days that followed. Starmer moved quickly, appointing James Murray as the new Health Secretary — a competent but unremarkable fill for a portfolio now radioactive by association. The message from Downing Street has been consistent and deliberately mundane: the Prime Minister has a mandate from the membership and the country, he is getting on with governing, and the party will be judged by its record in office.

Andy Burnham complicates the picture further. The Greater Manchester Mayor is understood to be actively seeking a parliamentary by-election seat — without a seat at Westminster, he cannot enter a leadership contest. His popularity in the English regions and his positioning as a Labour figure untainted by the Starmer years makes him a plausible alternative to Streeting as a unity candidate. Whether he can find a seat, secure a by-election, and enter the contest before it closes is a logistical and political problem he has not yet solved.

The pressure is real, but the contest is not yet. Streeting needs to convert the angry 97 plus enough of the 147 fence-sitters to trigger the threshold. Until he does, Starmer remains Prime Minister — battered, legitimacy-depleted, but constitutionally in place. The 2026 UK government crisis is not over. It is paused at the point where arithmetic meets nerve.

The Angle

The most revealing number in this crisis is not 97 or 159 — it is 147. The uncommitted bloc is not waiting for a better argument from either side; it is waiting for a sign that the arithmetic has already moved. Leadership contests are self-fulfilling once they feel inevitable. Streeting's challenge becomes real the moment enough MPs believe it is real. That is why the public nomination count matters as much as the private one.

Bulgaria wins Vienna: Dara takes Eurovision 2026

516
points — the score that gave Bulgaria its first-ever Eurovision Song Contest victory, at the 70th contest in Vienna on Saturday 16 May

At the Wiener Stadthalle on Saturday night, with the kind of scoreboard moment that the Eurovision Song Contest saves for itself, Dara lifted the trophy for Bulgaria — the country's first win in 66 years of entering the contest. Her song "Bangaranga", a propulsive dance anthem that managed the rare trick of being simultaneously absurd and completely irresistible, took both the jury vote and the public televote. The last performer to achieve that jury-and-public double was Salvador Sobral for Portugal in 2017.

The 516-point total put her comfortably clear of the field. The contest, the 70th edition, was hosted in Vienna following Austria's 2025 victory — and the Stadthalle's 16,000-seat main hall gave the grand final a scale that matched the occasion. Bulgaria is expected to host the 2027 contest, which will create its own logistical questions for a country staging the event for the first time.

The United Kingdom finished last in the public vote. The jury scores provided some insulation against the full bottom position overall, but the pattern is now familiar enough that the ritual post-contest analysis has taken on a certain resigned quality. The UK's Eurovision problem is not a presenting problem — it is structural, a function of geopolitical relationships between countries whose populations actually pick up the phone, and no song choice changes the underlying geometry.

Did You Know?

The Eurovision Song Contest has been running since 1956 — but Bulgaria only began entering in 2005. In 21 editions before this one, the country's best result was second place, achieved in 2017 with Kristian Kostov's "Beautiful Mess". Saturday's win was therefore not just a first — it closed a nine-year wait from near-miss to triumph.

Iran ceasefire on 'massive life support'

When does a ceasefire stop being a ceasefire?

The answer, in the case of the US-Iran truce, appears to be: the moment both sides keep shooting and blame each other for it. Since the ceasefire formally took effect, US and Iranian forces have exchanged fire in the Strait of Hormuz at least twice — each incident generating competing accounts of who fired first and why. Trump, who brokered the initial truce and declared it a triumph, described its current state bluntly last week as "on massive life support."

The proximate cause of the latest strain was Iran's counter-proposal for a phased arrangement: halt Hormuz tensions first, address the nuclear question later. The Trump administration rejected it. The White House position has been that the nuclear file and the strait cannot be sequenced — conceding one without the other simply trades a military problem for a proliferation problem.

In the background, a new security architecture is quietly taking shape. A trilateral US-India-UAE defence partnership is coalescing around the Iran tensions, formalising what has been an informal alignment for the better part of a year. For India, the framework offers cover to maintain strategic distance from Moscow while deepening ties with Washington. For the UAE, it provides reassurance against the economic fallout of a prolonged Hormuz disruption. Whether this partnership functions as a deterrent or an escalation ladder is not yet clear.

Gemini 4.0 — the 2-million-token moment

The headline expected reveal is Gemini 4.0, Google's next-generation large model, and the number attached to it is the 2-million-token context window. For context: Gemini 3.5 (the current commercially deployed model) supports 1 million tokens, itself a leap over the 128k and 200k figures that defined the previous generation. Moving to 2 million is a doubling of the already-expanded window — and it arrives as OpenAI's o-series and Anthropic's Claude have been trading context-window claims since late 2025.

The context-window race is partly a marketing exercise and partly a genuinely consequential capability shift. Long context matters most when the task involves synthesis across a large corpus: reviewing a full codebase, summarising a document archive, maintaining continuity across a long agentic session. Google's bet is that the jump to 2M tokens — combined with what's expected to be improved instruction-following and agentic tool use — puts Gemini 4 in a different tier for developer workflows.

Android 17 — the AI-native phone OS

Android 17 is the expected developer-facing operating system announcement, with the consumer build likely landing later in 2026 on Pixel hardware first. The pre-conference signals point to app bubbles as the headline UI feature — persistent floating windows that persist across apps, similar to Chat Heads from a decade ago but built on a richer compositing layer. The more substantive change is under the hood: Android 17 is expected to position the phone OS explicitly as an AI substrate, with Gemini deeper into the system stack — not just as an assistant overlay but as something closer to an OS-level capability exposed to third-party apps via API. What that means practically is that developers will be able to route tasks through on-device Gemini without a round-trip to the cloud, which has performance and privacy implications worth watching. Google I/O has historically used Android OS releases as the vehicle for its deepest developer pitch; Android 17 is expected to be no different.

Google I/O, by the numbers

Google I/O has run since 2008 — eighteen years of developer keynotes, product debuts, and occasionally paradigm-shifting announcements. The 2013 event introduced Google Glass; 2016 brought the first Assistant demo; 2023 was the year Bard arrived under fire from ChatGPT. This year's Shoreline return continues a tradition that predates the smartphone as the category it now almost entirely defines.

Android XR and smart glasses — the face-worn bet

Perhaps the most speculative expected announcement is Android XR's smart glasses tier — lightweight glasses with a camera, Gemini live translation, and a Maps overlay. The pitch is distinct from Apple Vision Pro, which is a headset experience demanding your full attention. The glasses Google is expected to show are ambient and wearable: designed for a walk through a foreign city, a conversation across a language barrier, a navigation prompt without pulling out a phone. The core enabling technology is Gemini's real-time multimodal capability — the glasses need to process camera input, understand context, and surface a response in near-real-time without being distracting. That is a harder technical problem than it sounds.

Whether this category lands depends entirely on execution quality. Google has been here before — Glass, in 2013, had the right concept and the wrong product. Android XR has the advantage of arriving into a context where AI assistants are now understood and expected, and where the hardware miniaturisation has genuinely advanced. What's expected to be shown on Tuesday is a developer platform, not a consumer product — but the direction of travel is clear enough to read.

Googlebooks — premium Android laptops

The expected Googlebook reveal is the most commercially legible of the four anticipated announces. Google is expected to show premium Android laptops — working name Googlebooks — from manufacturing partners including Acer, ASUS, and Lenovo. This is a meaningful reframe from the Chromebook category, which has largely been positioned as an education and budget device. A premium Android laptop running Gemini natively, with access to the full Android app ecosystem, is a different pitch: it puts Google into direct competition with Windows AI PCs and, to a lesser extent, MacBooks for the "laptop as AI interface" positioning that Microsoft has been pushing hard with Copilot+ since late 2024. The timing, with Microsoft Build running the same week, is unlikely to be accidental.

The Angle

Most I/O announcements land as previews and arrive as products six to eighteen months later — sometimes never. The context-window expansion is the exception worth tracking: it is a genuine, measurable capability that changes what you can ask the model to do in a single session. The agentic workflow claims and the glasses are worth watching but harder to evaluate without hands-on time. For anyone who uses Gemini or is considering it: the 2M-token context is the real lever. If Google ships it as briefed, the developer case for switching from OpenAI or Anthropic gets materially stronger.

The wider picture: the AI keynote season

Google I/O doesn't exist in isolation. Microsoft Build wraps up the same week — the two companies are now in direct competition for developer mindshare in the AI assistant and agentic layer, and the timing overlap is a calculated aggression from both sides. Apple's WWDC follows in early June, where Apple Intelligence is expected to receive a significant expansion — on-device models, deeper Siri integration, and possibly a more substantive third-party API. The AI keynote cycle has become the new product season: the quarterly earnings call used to be where tech strategy was explained, but increasingly it's these developer conferences that define competitive positioning for the following twelve months.

The question worth holding onto after Tuesday: not which announcements land loudest, but which ones ship on a realistic timeline. Google's history at I/O includes some of the best product demos and some of the longest gaps between demo and availability. The 2-million-token Gemini 4 is compelling on paper. The test is whether it's in developers' hands by September.

Microsoft Build this week too

Microsoft Build runs 19–22 May — overlapping directly with Google I/O. Microsoft is expected to expand Copilot+ capabilities and announce new Windows AI PC integrations. The AI developer conference calendar has never been more compressed: in one week, the two largest enterprise-to-consumer AI ecosystems are simultaneously pitching developers.

Switch 2 Exclusive 21 MAY

Nintendo's first significant first-party Switch 2 release of the year lands this Thursday at $59.99, and Yoshi and the Mysterious Book is doing something visually distinct from anything else on the platform. The art direction is the most immediately striking thing about what Nintendo has shown. As a system-seller test, the game's profile is interesting. Revolut becomes a real bank — now what?

  • 11 March 2026 — Revolut granted full UK banking licence by the PRA and FCA, exiting the two-year "mobilisation" phase it had been stuck in since 2024.
  • Around 13 million UK customers are being migrated from Revolut Ltd (e-money licence) to Revolut Bank UK, the new PRA-regulated entity.
  • FSCS protection up to £85,000 per depositor now applies — the same statutory guarantee that covers deposits at Barclays, Lloyds, HSBC, and Monzo.
13
million UK customers

What actually changed on 11 March

Until that date, Revolut operated as an e-money institution — a fundamentally different beast from a bank. E-money licences require customer funds to be "safeguarded" in ring-fenced accounts, but they do not carry FSCS deposit protection. In practice this meant that if Revolut had failed, customers would have been unsecured creditors in an administration, not protected depositors. For a multi-currency spending card that most people treat as a secondary account, that was an acceptable trade-off. For anyone parking meaningful savings there, it was a real gap.

The full banking licence closes it. From the point of migration — Revolut Bank UK is rolling accounts across through 2026 — deposits sit inside the FSCS scheme in exactly the same way as a high-street bank. Direct debits, salary credits, savings accounts: all qualify for protection up to £85,000. Note: the FSCS also provides a temporary high balance provision of up to £1 million for qualifying life events (property sale proceeds, inheritance, redundancy payments) held for up to six months — this applies to Revolut Bank UK as it does to any other covered institution.

What the licence does not immediately deliver: Revolut still has not rolled out a full mortgage or personal loan product in the way Monzo has begun to. Its customer service model remains thin relative to Monzo's — no in-app chat escalation to a phone line, no branch network (though to be clear: Monzo has no branches either). The app's reliability has improved considerably since 2023, but both of those gaps are worth noting before treating the two institutions as interchangeable.

What the licence genuinely unlocks beyond FSCS: the ability to hold savings interest under deposit protection; the ability to build a UK-resident credit history through a licensed UK bank (e-money institutions cannot report to credit bureaus in the same way); and eligibility for UK government savings schemes — including Cash ISAs and Help to Buy ISAs — that require a licensed UK deposit-taker as the account holder.

Should a Monzo-primary account holder split?

The question is worth framing precisely: not "should I switch to Revolut" but "does Revolut's FSCS coverage finally justify running it as a second protected account alongside Monzo?" The answer depends on four factors.

  • Deposits above £85,000 in a single bank. Anyone with more than £85,000 sitting in a single deposit account should consider splitting across two FSCS-covered institutions regardless of which two — this is basic protection hygiene, and FSCS only covers £85,000 per person per institution. Revolut Bank UK is now a legitimate second home for those funds.
  • Frequent FX or international transfers. Revolut remains the strongest consumer tool for this. The banking licence makes no difference to the FX engine; it only makes the underlying deposits safer. For anyone spending in multiple currencies or sending money abroad regularly, keeping Revolut active as a travel and FX account — now with FSCS protection on the balance — is a straightforward call.
  • Long-term primary banking — overdrafts, loans, mortgage. Monzo's UK product roadmap is further along. If credit products matter now or in the near future, Monzo is the better primary relationship until Revolut closes that gap.
  • Savings rates. Both Revolut and Monzo offer competitive flexible savings pots; the rates leapfrog each other on a quarterly basis. Check both at the point of deposit rather than assuming one leads permanently.

The practical verdict for a Monzo-primary, UK-resident account holder: keep Monzo primary — its credit product roadmap and customer service model make it the stronger everyday bank today. Open or migrate Revolut to a "secondary holdings plus FX plus perks" role, with the assurance that FSCS now covers it. Splitting savings across two protected accounts is no longer a workaround — it is simply sensible deposit management.

Did You Know?

The FSCS was established by the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 and paid out its first major claim during the 2008 financial crisis — covering depositors at Bradford & Bingley and Icesave after those institutions failed. The £85,000 limit aligns with the EU's Deposit Guarantee Schemes Directive equivalent (€100,000 at ECB rates), which the UK retained post-Brexit. Before the scheme existed, retail depositors in a failed bank had no statutory guarantee at all.

Auditing your deficit

What does your week's deficit actually look like?

Seven weeks into Block 2 — Fat Loss + Hypertrophy, 4 May to 30 June — the question worth asking is not "am I in a deficit?" Most people running a structured training block are. The question is whether the deficit is sized correctly for the goal of holding muscle while losing fat. Those are different numbers, and the gap between them matters more than most training plans acknowledge.

The productive zone

A 2026 meta-analysis published in PMC examining resistance-trained adults in caloric deficit confirmed what a growing body of evidence has been pointing toward: there is a specific deficit window where body recomposition — simultaneous fat loss and lean mass preservation — is achievable. Outside that window, one or the other goal collapses.

The productive zone sits at 200–400 kcal below estimated daily maintenance. At this depth, resistance training preserves lean mass and fat oxidation increases measurably. Modest hypertrophy remains possible — not optimal, but not off the table — because protein synthesis can still outpace catabolism when training volume and protein intake are adequate.

The conservation-mode danger zone

Cross 500–600 kcal per day below maintenance and the picture changes. Muscle gain ceases. Retention becomes marginal because the body, under sustained energy shortage, shifts toward protein turnover that nets negative — the rate of muscle protein breakdown begins to exceed the rate of synthesis even with resistance training stimulus. Recovery time extends, training quality drops across the week, and the compounding effect shows up in progress stalls rather than dramatic single-session failures.

The practical requirements to stay in the productive zone are two: protein intake at or above 1.6 g per kg of bodyweight per day, and training volume maintained at a minimum of 10 working sets per muscle group per week. Below either threshold, the productive zone's protections erode — the same deficit becomes more costly to lean mass.

How to audit your deficit this week
StepMethodWhat it tells you
1 Weigh 7 mornings in a row (post-toilet, pre-food). Average the seven. A single bodyweight reading is noise. The 7-day average is signal.
2 Compare this week's 7-day average to last week's. Loss of 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week = productive zone for body recomposition. Inside the range: you're in the sweet spot. Proceed.
3 If trend shows >1% loss for two consecutive weeks, you're in conservation mode. Add carbohydrate around training sessions, or reduce cardio volume. Rate too fast = muscle is paying the cost, not fat.
4 If trend shows <0.2% loss after two weeks, the deficit isn't reaching the tissue. Recheck logged calories or session output. Rate too slow = the surplus is hiding somewhere in the logging.

Eight weeks to the holiday window

Block 2 closes on 30 June — eight weeks from today. The holiday window follows immediately: a week of different food, different routines, and less structured training. That window isn't a threat to the block's progress if the deficit has been sized correctly throughout. The aim of the remaining eight weeks isn't to push the rate of loss higher. It's to arrive at a body composition that holds steady through a week of pancakes and waffles and returns to training the week after without needing a reset.

1954

Brown v. Board of Education — 17 May 1954

  • 17 May 1954 — Chief Justice Earl Warren delivers the unanimous 9–0 ruling in Brown v. Board of Education.
  • Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) overturned — the "separate but equal" doctrine is ruled unconstitutional.
  • Equal Protection Clause — the Court holds that racially segregated public schools violate the 14th Amendment.

Seventy-two years ago this Sunday, Chief Justice Earl Warren read the Court's opinion aloud to a packed chamber. He had spent months before the ruling quietly lobbying each of the eight associate justices in private conference. A 5–4 decision, Warren believed, would not hold — the country needed the full weight of a unanimous Court. He got it. When the clerk posted the result, the nine votes were listed as one.

The lead plaintiff was Oliver Brown, who brought the case on behalf of his daughter Linda, denied entry to Sumner Elementary School in Topeka, Kansas — seven blocks from home — because she was Black. But behind Brown stood the NAACP Legal Defense Fund and its chief counsel, Thurgood Marshall. Marshall had spent years constructing a legal strategy aimed directly at the constitutional heart of segregation rather than at its individual expressions. He called expert witnesses — most notably the psychologist Kenneth Clark, whose doll tests demonstrated measurable psychological harm in Black children attending segregated schools. The Court cited that testimony.

Marshall went on to become the first African-American Justice of the Supreme Court, appointed by President Lyndon B. Johnson in 1967. He served until 1991.

The ruling did not end segregation on the day it was issued. The implementation order — Brown II, handed down in 1955 — instructed school districts to desegregate "with all deliberate speed." That phrase became a tool of delay. In September 1957, Governor Orval Faubus of Arkansas ordered the National Guard to block nine Black students from entering Little Rock Central High School. President Eisenhower sent the 101st Airborne to escort them inside. The busing battles of the 1970s — court-ordered transportation to integrate schools across district lines — generated their own years of legal and social conflict. The legal principle established in 1954 took decades to approach reality.

The ruling remains the foundation cited every time a US school district redraws boundary lines today.

Did You Know?

Brown v. Board of Education was not one case — it was five consolidated cases heard together. Brown came from Kansas. Briggs v. Elliott came from South Carolina. Davis v. County School Board came from Virginia. Gebhart v. Belton came from Delaware. Bolling v. Sharpe came from Washington DC. The Supreme Court grouped them under the Kansas case name because, of the five, it was the one that came from a Northern state — making the ruling harder to characterise as a regional Southern matter.

A Fact

The word honeymoon comes from a Babylonian custom 4,000 years old: a bride's father was obligated to supply his son-in-law with all the mead (honey wine) he could drink for one full month after the wedding — the "honey month." The quality of the mead was held partly responsible for whether the couple would have a son.
