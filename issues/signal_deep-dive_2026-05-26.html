The First World War — the order that was lost

An evergreen Deep Dive in three movements: the world in 1914, the conflict, the aftermath. Plain English; no battle-by-battle walkthrough; one frame committed to early and held throughout.

From August 1914 to November 1918 — and the half-century that followed.

This issue covers the First World War from its origins in the European state system of 1914, through four years of fighting, to the settlements and their consequences across the following two decades.

It is arranged in three movements. The first covers the world before the war: the alliance structure, the six great powers and how each was actually governed, the wider cast of states and empires drawn in, and the specific crisis in the summer of 1914 that converted a Balkan assassination into a continental war. The second covers the conflict itself: how the war aims of each power changed as the costs accumulated, how the home fronts were transformed by the demands of industrial warfare, and how four empires ended in the seven months of 1918. The third covers the aftermath: the five peace settlements, what happened to the original belligerents across the following decade, the reshaped international order, and one causal chain walked in plain prose from 1917 to the Cold War. Fourteen chapters, approximately 18,000 words.

The frame the issue commits to is set out in The Argument, the short chapter that follows this one. Briefly: the historical question worth asking about 1914 is not what interrupted the world but what was destroyed. The pre-war order — its levels of trade, capital mobility, labour migration, and financial integration — was not paused by the war. It ended. The 1920s tried to rebuild something resembling it; the 1930s tore that attempt up. This is sometimes called the destroyed-globalisation reading of the war's significance, and it shapes what this issue pays attention to throughout.

I

The Argument

The magazine's take

The First World War did not interrupt the world of 1914 — it destroyed it. That world was an order, not a date, and once it ended, nothing built in the following century managed to restore it on the same terms.

The frame this issue commits to is the destroyed-globalisation reading developed by Adam Tooze in The Deluge (2014): the pre-1914 world had reached levels of trade openness, capital mobility, labour migration, and sterling-anchored financial integration that would not be matched again until the 1990s. The war ended that order. The 1920s attempted to rebuild a version of it that no longer fitted the new politics of mass electorates, national debt, and erased empires; the 1930s tore that rebuilt version apart; 1945 produced a different order on Cold-War terms. The frame commits the issue to treating 1914 as a structural moment rather than a biographical one — which means it sidelines the "punitive peace" reading of Versailles as the hinge between the two world wars, and it sidelines the Fischer thesis that the war was primarily Germany's choice. Both of those readings get a hearing in the body where the evidence supports them. The two accounts the issue draws on most heavily alongside Tooze are Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers (2012) — the July Crisis as collective diplomatic failure, with distributed rather than concentrated responsibility — and Margaret MacMillan's Paris 1919 (2001), which argues that the peace negotiators did better than the circumstances allowed. Clark helps explain how the war started; MacMillan helps explain why the settlements were shaped as they were. The spine of the issue is the destroyed order. The rest arranges itself around that.

II

The European Order

Europe in 1914 was organised into two alliance systems, governed by conscript armies built on nearly identical principles, and threaded through a single financial network centred on the City of London. Those three facts — the alliances, the armies, and the money — are the structural picture. Each gets its own treatment below, but they need to be held together first, because the war became what it became only because all three were operating simultaneously and none had been designed with the others in mind.

The Alliance System

The Triple Alliance linked Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy. The original treaty was signed in 1882, renewed several times, and was still formally in force in 1914. The Triple Entente joined France and Russia by military convention in 1894, France and Britain by the Entente Cordiale in 1904, and Britain and Russia by a separate entente in 1907. Neither grouping was a unified military command. There was no joint general staff, no shared war plan, no common budget. The alliances were sets of treaty commitments — some public, some secret — specifying what each signatory was obligated to do if a partner went to war.

What made them consequential in 1914 was not their legal text but their interaction with mobilisation. Each continental power had built its war plans around the assumption that the enemy alliance would mobilise, and that the side that mobilised slowest would lose. Once one power ordered general mobilisation, the others faced a binary: wait and concede a strategic advantage, or mobilise immediately and treat the ally's commitment as activated. The Franco-Russian alliance worked precisely because France knew that a German attack on Russia meant German forces not on the French frontier — and vice versa. That two-front problem was the military logic the alliance embedded, and no German or Austrian planner could ignore it.

Italy's membership of the Triple Alliance was the most qualified. Rome had parallel secret understandings with both Britain and France, and the alliance text included a provision exempting Italy from obligations against Britain. When Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia in July 1914, Italy declared neutrality on the grounds that the alliance was defensive and Austria had been the aggressor. In May 1915, Italy entered the war on the Entente side, having secured territorial promises in the secret Treaty of London.

The broader structure was a relay, not a trigger. Each step in the July 1914 crisis required a decision by the government taking it. What the architecture removed was the ability to reverse any single decision without the whole relay running anyway. Russia's general mobilisation obligated Germany to respond; Germany's response activated the Franco-Russian alliance; Germany's attack on France through Belgium activated the 1839 Treaty of London, which guaranteed Belgian neutrality and which Britain had signed. None of this was automatic. But the sequence moved faster than the diplomacy could keep up with.

Just for a word — neutrality, a word which in wartime had so often been disregarded — just for a scrap of paper, Great Britain was going to make war on a kindred nation.

— Chancellor Theobald von Bethmann-Hollweg to the British ambassador Sir Edward Goschen, 4 August 1914

Bethmann-Hollweg made the remark in frustration and immediately regretted it. But it pointed, without meaning to, at the structural fact: the 1839 Belgian guarantee was not a sentimental document. It was exactly the kind of multilateral legal commitment on which the entire European order depended for coherence. An order whose treaties could be dismissed as scraps of paper when inconvenient had already described the terms of its own dissolution.

The Militarised Peace

All six European great powers maintained conscript armies except Britain. On the continent, male citizens of the appropriate age were legally required to serve: typically two or three years of active duty, then decades in the reserves, available to be recalled on mobilisation. France had roughly 776,000 men under arms in peacetime; Germany 808,000; Russia 1.4 million. Britain's regular army of 247,000 was a professional volunteer force, organised primarily for imperial policing and the defence of naval bases. Britain had no conscription law until 1916.

Conscription meant that the wartime armies were far larger than the peacetime ones. Germany could mobilise to roughly 3.8 million men within weeks; France to a comparable figure; Russia to several million. The reservists existed, their equipment was stored, and the railway timetables to move them had been refined over decades by each country's general staff. This created a structural asymmetry in any crisis: mobilisation was much harder to halt than to start. To begin calling up reservists, commandeering rolling stock, and moving supply depots forward was to commit. Stopping the process partway left troops without weapons at depots and plans without the forces to execute them — while a potential adversary's machine kept running.

The Schlieffen Plan

Germany's war plan was named for the former Chief of the General Staff who drafted its original version. It addressed Germany's two-front problem by sequencing: defeat France in roughly six weeks, then transfer forces east before Russia's slower mobilisation was complete. The six-week deadline was not a strategic preference but a mathematical constraint imposed by Russia's railway capacity. Any delay in the western timetable was assumed to leave Germany facing a fully mobilised Russian army before France had been eliminated. The plan committed Germany to attacking France immediately upon any general European war — and to attacking through Belgium, because the direct Franco-German border was heavily fortified.

The Schlieffen Plan was the most explicit expression of this timetable logic. Named for the former Chief of the German General Staff, it addressed Germany's two-front strategic problem by sequencing: knock out France in six weeks, then transfer forces east before Russia's slower mobilisation was complete. The six-week deadline was not a preference but a ceiling imposed by Russia's railway capacity. This is why, in the July 1914 crisis, the German military told the Kaiser that once Russia ordered general mobilisation, Germany had to act within hours. The plan had no pause button.

The assumption that war would be short was shared across every general staff in Europe, not only Germany's. Stocks of ammunition, industrial mobilisation schedules, and financial contingency plans all assumed a campaign of months, not years. The Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71 had lasted seven months and ended in a decisive German victory. The Russo-Japanese War of 1904–05 had ended in a negotiated peace. No staff in 1914 had a plan for four years of industrialised attrition, and no government had told them to write one.

The Globalised Economy

The pre-war economic order was unusually open by historical standards. International capital flows in 1913 reached approximately 5% of global GDP — a level not exceeded again until the 1990s. Roughly 36 million Europeans emigrated overseas between 1820 and 1914; approximately 1.2 million made the crossing in 1913 alone. The pound sterling settled roughly 60% of world trade. These figures describe a system, not a collection of statistics, and the system had a specific mechanism.

Sterling's dominance as the world's trading currency rested on London's institutional infrastructure. British merchant banks provided the trade finance that allowed exporters to ship goods before payment was received — a sterling acceptance, a short-term bill drawn on a City bank, was the most liquid financial instrument in the world. British marine insurers, principally Lloyd's of London, underwrote most of the world's ocean cargo. The Baltic Exchange matched ships to cargoes on routes where neither the buyer nor the seller had any British connection. A merchant in Buenos Aires could raise trade finance from a London bank, insure the cargo with a London underwriter, and ship it to Hamburg — all without either party holding any sterling assets. The mechanism worked because City institutions offered the deepest pool of short-term credit and the most reliable settlement infrastructure available.

Capital moved on a similar scale. Britain alone exported capital equivalent to roughly 8% of its national income annually in the decade before 1914, financing railways in Argentina, ports in Brazil, mining operations in South Africa, and government bonds across the Ottoman Empire and East Asia. France and Germany were large capital exporters too. The dependencies ran in all directions: an Argentine cattle rancher raising money in London, a Russian government borrowing in Paris, a Turkish railway built with German bank loans. Some economists at the time — Norman Angell prominently, in his 1910 book The Great Illusion — argued from this integration that a major European war had become economically irrational. The argument was correct and it made no difference.

Labour moved at comparable scale. Steamship technology had made the Atlantic crossing an affordable journey for a working-class emigrant rather than an expensive exile. Submarine telegraph cables — laid at enormous cost from the 1860s onward and mostly operated by British companies — had made real-time commercial communication between continents routine. A price change in Chicago reached London in minutes. The physical world was smaller than it had ever been, and the institutions connecting it were concentrated in a city that was, by August 1914, preparing for war.

The 1907 Hague Convention had provided a legal layer for all of this. The convention codified rules of war: protections for civilians, requirements for the humane treatment of prisoners, prohibitions on certain weapons. Every major power that would fight in 1914 had signed it. By 1916, every major power had breached it. The rules had been designed for an order that assumed its participants wanted to preserve the order. The order turned out to be less stable than it looked.

The three structures — alliances, armies, and economy — had coexisted since the 1890s without producing a general European war. The Moroccan crises of 1905 and 1911 had brought the great powers close to conflict and both times had been resolved by negotiation. The Balkan Wars of 1912–13 had reshaped south-eastern Europe without triggering the general mobilisation that the alliance relay would have required. Forty years of close calls produced a false confidence that the system would keep working. What it had actually produced was a set of mechanisms refined to the point of hair-trigger precision. The six powers that would take that mechanism to war are introduced below, and treated in full in the chapter that follows.

III

The Six Great Powers

Each of the six European great powers in 1914 had a distinct constitutional structure, a distinct relationship between civilian and military authority, and a distinct set of strategic interests. Understanding why each one went to war in August 1914 requires understanding what kind of state each actually was.

Britain

Britain governed its empire through three distinct mechanisms, and conflating them produces a misleading picture of how the whole thing actually worked. The first mechanism was direct Crown rule: the Colonial Office administered most colonies — in Africa, the Pacific, the Caribbean — through appointed governors who reported to London. The second was indirect rule through the princely states of India, where roughly 560 nominally sovereign rulers retained their thrones but accepted British Residents who held effective veto over external policy and, increasingly, internal affairs. The Viceroy in Calcutta sat above this system as the Crown's representative, with authority over both the directly governed provinces and the princely network. The third mechanism was self-governing dominion status: Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa each had their own parliaments and prime ministers, managed their own budgets, and largely ran their own domestic affairs, though the Crown's foreign policy bound them all and the Governor-General in each dominion retained formal reserve powers that were very rarely used.

The machinery that held the directly governed parts together was surprisingly thin. The Indian Civil Service comprised roughly 1,200 British officers at any one time — selected by competitive examination in London — who provided the senior administrative layer governing approximately 300 million people. Below them sat a much larger Indian civil service drawn from the subcontinent's educated elites, without whom the entire apparatus would have collapsed immediately. The ratio was about one British ICS officer per 250,000 Indians: a system that worked partly through genuine administrative competence, partly through the co-option of local intermediaries, and partly through the threat of the Indian Army.

Sterling's role as the world's reserve currency flowed from a specific structural fact rather than from simple British economic power. Most international trade in 1913 was invoiced in pounds and settled through London correspondent banks. When a Brazilian coffee exporter sold to a German importer, the transaction was typically denominated in sterling, insured by a Lloyd's syndicate, financed by a London acceptance house, and shipped on a vessel whose hull was also insured in the City. This meant that a very large share of world commerce moved through British financial intermediaries regardless of whether Britain was directly involved as buyer or seller. Around 60 per cent of world trade settled in sterling in 1913.

The British regular army in 1914 numbered about 247,000 men — the smallest professional army of any great power, by a large margin. This was a deliberate policy. The Royal Navy was Britain's primary strategic instrument: it kept the sea lanes open for trade, protected the empire's littoral territories, and gave Britain the ability to project power or withhold it from any coastline in the world. An army large enough to threaten a continental power would have required conscription, which successive governments resisted, and would have implied a continental commitment that British foreign policy had long avoided. The result was an army optimised for colonial policing and imperial garrisons, not for a sustained ground war in France — a mismatch that would become painfully obvious within weeks of the August 1914 mobilisation.

France

France had been a republic since 1871, when the Third Republic replaced the Second Empire after the defeat by Prussia. The defeat had cost France the provinces of Alsace and most of Lorraine, including the iron-ore fields around Metz — a loss that sat at the centre of French strategic thinking for the next four decades. French governments changed frequently (the Third Republic would have more than fifty different governments between 1871 and 1914), but on the question of the lost provinces and the threat from Germany, French policy was remarkably consistent.

Population gap France's population in 1914 was approximately 39.6 million. Germany's was 65 million and growing faster. France had one of the lowest birth rates in Europe; Germany had one of the highest. French military planners had been working around this demographic disadvantage for thirty years.

The demographic reality sharpened everything. France's population was roughly 39.6 million in 1914; Germany's was 65 million and growing. France could not match Germany man for man in a sustained war of attrition, which gave French military doctrine a particular urgency: the army had to attack fast, before German numbers became decisive. This reasoning produced Plan XVII, the operational framework the French army entered the war with — a doctrine of the offensive à outrance, the offensive carried to extremes, premised on the idea that moral force and relentless forward momentum could overcome material disadvantage. The theory cost France approximately a million casualties in the first four months of the war.

The two alliances France had built since 1871 were the structural answer to its demographic problem. The 1894 Franco-Russian alliance meant that any German war against France would require Germany to fight on two fronts simultaneously — a problem that German planning acknowledged openly. The 1904 Entente Cordiale with Britain was initially a settlement of colonial disputes in North Africa and South-East Asia, not a military alliance, but it had evolved by 1914 into a network of military staff conversations, joint naval dispositions, and political understandings that made British non-intervention in a Franco-German war increasingly difficult to sustain. France entered 1914 with a peacetime conscript army of approximately 776,000 and a large trained reserve, but its strategic position depended entirely on the two alliances holding.

French politics in 1914 were pulled between a strongly republican and anticlerical urban culture — concentrated in Paris, Lyon, and the northern industrial cities — and a Catholic, conservative, and often monarchist countryside. The Dreyfus Affair of 1894-1906 had polarised the country along these lines, with the republican left defending the wrongly convicted Jewish officer and the Catholic-military right defending the army's honour. By 1914 the republic had survived the crisis, but the underlying tension had not resolved. Raymond Poincaré, elected President in January 1913, represented the republican centre's determination to maintain French national unity and honour the alliances: his state visit to St Petersburg was in progress when the July 1914 crisis broke, and his absence from Paris in those critical days was itself a factor in France's delayed diplomatic response.

Germany

The German Empire was founded in 1871, created by Bismarck's engineering of three wars against Denmark, Austria, and France that left Prussia the dominant state in a new German federation. The federation's constitutional structure distributed substantial powers to its member kingdoms — Bavaria, Saxony, Württemberg, and the smaller states — while placing the Emperor, the Chancellor, and the army under Prussian control. The Reichstag, elected by universal male suffrage, held genuine power over the budget; it could block appropriations and had done so. What it could not do was appoint or remove the Chancellor, who was appointed by and responsible to the Kaiser alone. The army reported through the General Staff directly to the Kaiser, not through the civilian government. This produced a political system where democratic pressure could shape fiscal decisions but could not control the executive or the military — a combination that Bismarck had designed deliberately and that became genuinely dangerous when the men at the top were less prudent than he had been.

Germany's economic growth between 1871 and 1914 was the most rapid of any major power. By 1914 it had the largest economy in continental Europe, ahead of France, Austria-Hungary, and Russia. Its lead in specific industries was pronounced: the German chemical industry — BASF, Bayer, Hoechst — dominated world markets in synthetic dyes and, increasingly, pharmaceuticals. Electrical engineering firms like Siemens and AEG were building the electrical infrastructure of Europe and beyond. German steel output exceeded Britain's by 1900 and continued to grow. This industrial base made Germany both a formidable military power and a central node in the European trading system, exporting manufactured goods and importing raw materials through the same networks of trade finance centred in London that everyone else used.

The decision to build a battle fleet — the Tirpitz programme launched from 1898 under Kaiser Wilhelm II's Weltpolitik — changed Britain's strategic relationship with Germany in a way that the economic competition had not. Britain could accommodate a rival industrial economy; it could not accommodate a navy strong enough to challenge Royal Navy dominance in the North Sea, because the entire imperial system rested on sea control. The German High Seas Fleet never came close to matching the Royal Navy in numbers — by 1914 Britain had 29 dreadnoughts and battlecruisers to Germany's 17 — but its existence alone was enough to trigger a British naval arms race and push Britain into the arms of France. The fleet alarmed London without ever giving Germany a credible naval deterrent; it was an expensive political mistake that bought Germany an enemy it might otherwise have avoided.

Austria-Hungary

The Ausgleich (the German word for compromise) of 1867 had created the Austro-Hungarian dual monarchy by splitting the Habsburg Empire into two co-equal kingdoms sharing only three institutions: the monarch (Franz Joseph was Emperor of Austria and King of Hungary simultaneously, under different constitutional titles), the common army, and three joint ministries covering foreign affairs, war, and the finances required to run those two. Everything else — separate parliaments, separate governments, separate budget processes, separate languages of official business — was divided between Vienna and Budapest. The Hungarian half had its own Prime Minister and its own ministerial system; so did the Austrian half. The joint Foreign Minister and the joint War Minister reported to the Emperor-King, not to either parliament.

The practical consequence of this structure was that any decision requiring both halves to act together required either a direct imperial mandate or a diplomatic negotiation between Vienna and Budapest. The Hungarian government, led from 1903 by István Tisza, used its position to block constitutional reforms that might have given the empire's Slavic populations — Czechs, Poles, Ruthenians, Slovenes, Croats, Serbs — proportional representation or broader autonomy. Tisza's calculation was straightforward: Slavic political empowerment would dilute Magyar dominance of the Hungarian half of the monarchy. This made the empire progressively harder to govern as Slavic nationalism grew, and it meant that Austria-Hungary entered 1914 with an unresolved internal political crisis that the war would eventually tear open.

Franz Joseph had been on the throne since 1848 — longer than any other European monarch except the King of Sweden. The empire's population of approximately 51.4 million spoke at least eleven recognised languages: German and Hungarian were the languages of government in their respective halves; Czech, Polish, Ruthenian (Ukrainian), Slovak, Slovene, Croat, Serb, Romanian, and Italian each had official status in particular crownlands. The common army necessarily operated in multiple languages, with German as the language of command for officers. By 1914, 26 different languages were listed as spoken by Habsburg soldiers in their regimental records. The army was the one institution that genuinely tried to integrate the empire's peoples across linguistic lines; it was also, by 1914, stretched thin across multiple frontier commitments — facing Russia in Galicia to the north-east, Serbia on the southern border, and Italy along the Alpine frontier — with a peacetime strength of about 480,000 and an officer corps that knew it was not large enough for the wars it might face.

Russia

Russia in 1914 had a population of approximately 168.5 million — the largest of any European power by a wide margin, and the basis for the common assumption that Russia would eventually prevail in any sustained conflict. The assumption overlooked the structural constraints. Russia's per-capita GDP was the lowest of the six great powers: its economy was growing rapidly from a low base, but that growth was heavily concentrated in specific industrial sectors and specific regions, particularly around St Petersburg and in the Donbas coal and steel belt. The rail network was expanding but was still thin relative to Russia's geography, which was why the German Schlieffen Plan's working assumption — that it would take Russia six weeks to fully mobilise — had considerable force as late as 1912, though subsequent Russian investment was changing those timelines.

The political system was a constrained autocracy. The 1905 revolution — triggered by the catastrophic defeat in the Russo-Japanese War and by bread riots and general strikes — had forced Nicholas II to issue the October Manifesto, creating the State Duma as a legislative assembly with genuine budget powers. The autocrat retained final authority over foreign policy and the decision for war; he appointed ministers and could dissolve the Duma. The result was a hybrid that satisfied neither reformers nor conservatives: reformers found the Duma could be bypassed; conservatives found it kept alive an expectation of constitutional development that they wanted to suppress. Pyotr Stolypin's land reforms between 1906 and 1911 — which attempted to create a class of prosperous individual peasant farmers by dissolving the communal land-holding system — were genuinely significant economically, but Stolypin was assassinated in 1911 and the reform programme slowed thereafter.

Russia's strategic interest in the Balkans was a function of geography and of the Ottoman question. The Turkish Straits — the Bosphorus and the Dardanelles — were the only route from the Black Sea ports that exported Russian grain to world markets. Russian wheat exports moved through Odessa and Novorossiysk; any power that controlled Constantinople could throttle Russian trade. The protection of Slavic peoples in the Balkans from Austro-Hungarian or Ottoman pressure was a secondary interest — real, and politically important domestically — but the straits question was the primary strategic fact. Russia's peacetime army of approximately 1.4 million was the largest in the world, with a reserve mobilisation capacity that, when fully deployed, would produce forces on a scale no other power could match. The problem was the time and infrastructure required to deploy them.

Italy

Italian unification — the Risorgimento — had been completed between 1859 and 1870, when the capture of Rome from papal control gave the new Kingdom of Italy its capital. By 1914 the unified state was only forty-four years old, roughly the same age as the German Empire but considerably less stable. The constitutional monarchy under Vittorio Emanuele III functioned on a parliamentary system that relied heavily on trasformismo — the practice of building governing coalitions by absorbing opposition figures through patronage and political deals — rather than on coherent party programmes. Antonio Salandra had become Prime Minister in March 1914, following the election of a conservative government.

Italy was the poorest of the six powers and the least industrialised. Its economy was heavily agricultural, particularly in the south; the industrial north — Turin, Milan, Genoa — was growing, but the gap between the industrial north and the agrarian south (the questione meridionale) was a persistent internal fracture. In military terms, Italy maintained a peacetime army of about 290,000 but its army's record in the 1911-12 war against the Ottoman Empire in Libya — a conflict that had taken far longer and cost far more than anticipated — had exposed serious deficiencies in equipment, logistics, and command. Italy gained Libya and the Dodecanese islands from the war, but the operation had drained Italian finances and the military lessons were sobering.

Italy's formal alliance position in 1914 was membership of the Triple Alliance with Germany and Austria-Hungary, signed in 1882 and renewed periodically thereafter. The alliance was explicitly defensive: its terms required Italy to support its partners only if they were attacked, not if they were the aggressors. Italy had been simultaneously maintaining informal understandings with Britain and France for over a decade — the 1902 Prinetti-Barrère agreement had secretly assured France that Italy would stay neutral in a French defensive war, and similar quiet assurances had been extended to London. When Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia in July 1914, Italy invoked the defensive character of the Triple Alliance and declared neutrality. It would spend the following nine months negotiating with both sides over what price it could extract for intervention. The Treaty of London of April 1915, signed with the Entente, offered Italy the Trentino, Trieste, Dalmatia, and other territorial gains — enough to bring Italy into the war on the Allied side in May 1915. The Triple Alliance, by the time the war started, had meant almost nothing for a decade; Italy's choice in 1915 confirmed it.

IV

The Wider World

The European great powers in 1914 were not the only states with a stake in what happened next. The Ottoman Empire, the United States, and Japan each held positions that would shape how the war began, how it expanded, and what settled when it ended. Beyond those three, the European colonial system had drawn most of the rest of the world into the same financial and logistical web — which meant that when war came to Europe, it came, in different ways, everywhere else too.

The Ottoman Empire

The phrase "the sick man of Europe" was coined by Tsar Nicholas I in the 1850s to describe the Ottoman Empire's apparent fragility, and it became one of the most durable pieces of diplomatic shorthand in the nineteenth century. By 1914 it had outlasted its accuracy. The empire was under serious strain, but it was not passively declining. It was governed, since 1908, by a constitutional government that had come to power through a revolution.

The Young Turk Revolution of 1908 forced Sultan Abdülhamid II to restore the 1876 constitution and call a parliament for the first time in decades. The political movement behind that revolution — the Committee of Union and Progress, known by its French acronym CUP — was a coalition of reformist officers and officials who believed the empire could modernise its way out of the military and territorial losses that had been accumulating since the Russo-Turkish War of 1877-78. After a counter-coup attempt in 1909, Abdülhamid II was deposed and replaced by his more pliant brother Mehmed V. By 1913, following a coup that concentrated power in the hands of a triumvirate — Enver Pasha as war minister, Talaat Pasha as interior minister, and Cemal Pasha as navy minister — the CUP effectively ran Ottoman policy.

The Triumvirate

Enver, Talaat, and Cemal Pasha were the effective rulers of the Ottoman Empire from the 1913 coup onward. Enver was the military architect of the German alliance; Talaat the political organiser; Cemal the naval minister who briefly commanded forces in Syria. All three would die in exile after the war — Talaat assassinated in Berlin in 1921, Enver killed in Central Asia in 1922, Cemal assassinated in Tiflis in 1922. There was no surviving Ottoman portrait of the three together in the research bundle for this issue, which is its own kind of historical absence.

The territorial losses that framed the CUP's position were recent and severe. The Balkan Wars of 1912-13 had stripped the empire of almost everything it still held in Europe. Albania, Macedonia, most of Thrace — gone in two years. The empire that had controlled the Balkans in 1900 controlled almost none of it by the summer of 1914. What remained was Anatolia, the Levant including Palestine, Mesopotamia (today's Iraq), the Hejaz, and parts of the Arabian Peninsula. The population stood at around 21 million — about one-third of Austria-Hungary's, less than one-fifth of Russia's.

The CUP's response to this position was to look for a great-power patron, and Germany was the willing candidate. A secret alliance was signed on 2 August 1914 — the day after Germany declared war on Russia. The specifics of how the empire entered the war belong to the next chapter. The point here is structural: the Ottoman state in 1914 was not a passive residue of an earlier order but a government with clear political leadership and a programme, however improvised, for survival.

The United States

The United States in 1914 was, by GDP, the largest economy in the world — larger than Britain, larger than Germany, larger than France and Russia combined. It was not, by any conventional measure, a military power. The US Army in 1914 numbered around 130,000 men — the smallest of any major power and smaller than several minor ones. The Navy was second only to Britain's, but the army had been sized for frontier policing, not European-scale war. The institutional gap between American economic weight and American military capacity in 1914 was larger than that of any other state in the international system.

Woodrow Wilson had won the presidency in 1912 on a domestic platform he called the New Freedom — a programme of anti-trust reform, tariff reduction, and the creation of the Federal Reserve. Foreign policy was not a significant part of that campaign, and for the first two years of his presidency Wilson governed accordingly. He was suspicious of European entanglements, instinctively idealist in his view of international relations, and constitutionally inclined to see American involvement in other countries' conflicts as a corruption of the republic's principles rather than an extension of its interests.

What Wilson did possess, in incipient form, was a view of how international order should be organised — one built on open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, and the right of peoples to determine their own governments. Those ideas would crystallise into the Fourteen Points by January 1918. In 1914 they were a disposition more than a policy, and they pointed toward neutrality. The United States would stay out of the war for nearly three years, and the specific mechanisms of its entry — unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram — belong to that later chapter.

Japan

Japan's position in 1914 was both the clearest of any non-European power and the most easily overlooked by a European-centred account. In 1905 Japan had defeated Russia in a war fought over Manchuria and Korea — the first time a non-European state had defeated a European great power in a modern conflict. The defeat had shocked European capitals and forced a reconsideration of the racial and civilisational assumptions built into the idea of the "great powers." Japan, the reconsideration concluded, was one of them.

The Anglo-Japanese Alliance, first signed in 1902 and renewed in 1905 and again in 1911, was Britain's most important alliance outside Europe. Britain needed a naval partner in the Pacific; Japan needed diplomatic cover from a European great power while it consolidated its position in East Asia. The two states' interests aligned closely enough to sustain a formal alliance for twenty years. Under Emperor Yoshihito — the Taishō era had begun in 1912 following the death of Meiji — Japan was a constitutional monarchy with a parliament, though the oligarchic structures inherited from the Meiji modernisation meant that decision-making remained concentrated among a relatively small group of military and political leaders.

Japan's population stood at around 53 million. Its economy had industrialised fast from the 1870s onward, with steel, shipbuilding, and textiles as the leading sectors. There is no portrait of the Taishō-era leadership in the research bundle for this issue — the National Diet Library holds extensive archives of the period but none were surfaced in the verified image candidates — so the visual record here is a gap rather than a choice.

When war came to Europe in August 1914, Japan's calculation was straightforward. The Anglo-Japanese Alliance gave Britain the right to invoke Japanese assistance; Japan had its own reasons to act. Germany held colonial possessions in East Asia and the Pacific — the leased territory of Tsingtao on the Shandong Peninsula in China, and the island chains of the Marshall Islands, the Marianas, and the Carolines in the Pacific. Japan declared war on Germany on 23 August 1914, took Tsingtao after a brief siege in November, and occupied the German Pacific islands with minimal resistance. Japan would spend the rest of the war pressing China for territorial and economic concessions — the Twenty-One Demands of January 1915 being the sharpest instance — while remaining a largely distant partner from the European fighting.

The European empires were not walled off from their colonies when the war began. The colonial possessions were integrated into the European order through three overlapping systems: military recruitment, economic extraction, and financial architecture. When the war expanded, all three were pulled tighter.

Britain drew on India most heavily. Around 1.3 million Indian troops served the British war effort between 1914 and 1918, with approximately 74,000 dead. Indian soldiers fought on the Western Front in 1914 and 1915 before being redeployed to Mesopotamia and East Africa; they were present at Gallipoli, in Palestine, and at the siege of Kut. They were recruited through the Indian Army's existing regimental structure, which the Indian Civil Service — around 1,200 British officers administering roughly 300 million people — maintained as a fundamental instrument of imperial governance. France drew on West Africa in comparable ways. Around 200,000 soldiers from the Tirailleurs Sénégalais — the umbrella term for French African colonial troops — fought for France during the war, with approximately 30,000 dead. They served on the Western Front, in the Dardanelles, and in the Salonika campaign.

The economic mechanism that tied non-European territories to the European order was sterling. Most international trade in 1914 was invoiced in pounds and settled through the City of London, regardless of whether either trading party was British. British marine insurance and trade finance dominated the global system — Argentine beef exporters, Brazilian coffee growers, and Chilean nitrate producers all received payment through London-based banks and shipped their goods under policies written in Lloyd's. The 1914 trade data bear this out: international capital flows were running at around 5 per cent of global GDP, a level not exceeded again until the 1990s. Latin America was largely neutral in the war, but it was embedded in the financial system that the war would eventually disrupt. The neutrality was economic fiction underpinned by political form.

Around 1.3 million Indian troops served the British war effort. Around 200,000 African colonial troops fought for France. The war the European powers declared in August 1914 was already a world war before the first shot crossed a continental border.

The political implications of this mobilisation were not invisible in 1914. The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, had been pressing for Indian legislative representation and self-governance for three decades. In 1914 the dominant figure in Congress was Bal Gangadhar Tilak, whose wing of the organisation had grown steadily more assertive in its demands. In Egypt, where Britain had been the effective governing power since 1882, a proto-nationalist movement was already organising around the demand for greater Egyptian control of Egyptian affairs — what would become the Wafd Party after the war. Both movements would support the Allied war effort in 1914-15, calculating that wartime loyalty might purchase post-war political concessions. The calculation would be tested, and largely disappointed, by what happened after 1918.

The 1914 order, in short, was not a European order that happened to have colonies attached. It was a genuinely global system, integrated at the level of capital flows, shipping, and currency, in which the imperial periphery financed and supplied the European core. When that order was destroyed, the destruction reached everywhere the system had reached.

V

The Summer of 1914

The five weeks between the Sarajevo assassination and Britain's declaration of war on Germany are the most-studied weeks in modern history. They are also among the worst-understood. The crisis escalated not because the European powers lost control of a runaway machine, and not because any one capital planned a continental war from the outset. It escalated because each government, acting on what it thought were rational defensive grounds, made the decisions that made war unavoidable — and the alliance system built to prevent exactly that converted a Balkan quarrel into a European catastrophe in thirty-seven days.

Assassinations of European royals were a recurring nineteenth-century feature. What was new in July 1914 was the response. Austria-Hungary chose to read Sarajevo as state-sponsored aggression against the dual monarchy itself — an act by the Serbian government, or a faction within it, aimed at destabilising Habsburg rule over its South Slav populations. Vienna decided to use the assassination as the occasion to settle the Serbian question by force, and sought German backing before acting. Germany gave it: the "blank cheque" of 5–6 July offered Austria full support whatever it decided to do. The Austro-Hungarian leadership, reassured, drafted an ultimatum designed to be unacceptable.

Russia could not stand aside. A settlement that humiliated Serbia — the largest Slavic state outside Russian influence — would signal to every Balkan power that St. Petersburg could not protect its clients. Tsar Nicholas II's government was not willing to absorb that signal, particularly not after having been forced to do exactly that during the Bosnian crisis of 1908. France was bound to Russia by the 1894 military convention: a Russian mobilisation triggered French commitments. Germany, in turn, was bound by the logic of its own military planning. The Schlieffen Plan assumed a two-front war and required France to be defeated in roughly six weeks before Russia could fully mobilise. Once Russia began mobilising, German planners calculated they could not wait — every hour of Russian preparation closed the window. The system of conditional commitments designed to keep the peace converted a Balkan crisis into a continental war in five weeks.

Britain's decision

Britain's entry was not certain until 4 August. The Cabinet had been divided through the final days of July: a significant faction was prepared to stay out of a Franco-German war that Britain had no treaty obligation to join. Sir Edward Grey, the Foreign Secretary, had been manoeuvring for days to present any British intervention as a matter of obligation rather than choice. He found his argument when German troops crossed into Belgium.

The 1839 Treaty of London had guaranteed Belgian neutrality, with Britain among the signatories. When German Chancellor Bethmann-Hollweg asked the British ambassador Sir Edward Goschen whether Britain would really go to war over that guarantee, his phrasing revealed how completely Berlin had miscalculated. He called the treaty a "scrap of paper." The phrase was a gift to British war advocates. The Cabinet members who had been wavering now had a principle they could defend publicly. Belgium's invasion converted what had been a question of entanglement in a continental war into a question of whether Britain would honour a legal commitment it had made seventy-five years earlier.

Just for a word — neutrality, a word which in wartime had so often been disregarded — just for a scrap of paper, Great Britain was going to make war on a kindred nation.

— Theobald von Bethmann-Hollweg, German Chancellor, to British ambassador Sir Edward Goschen, 4 August 1914

Without Belgium, the British government would probably have stayed out and watched the continent fight. The majority of the Liberal Cabinet would not have sustained a decision to fight for France alone. Germany's decision to invoke the Schlieffen Plan — which required the march through Belgium — was the decision that brought Britain into the war.

Princip and the conspiracy

Gavrilo Princip was 19 years old, a Bosnian Serb nationalist, and one of seven conspirators deployed along the Archduke's route through Sarajevo on 28 June. The group had backing — weapons, training, and safe passage across the Serbian border — from the Black Hand, a secret paramilitary network with connections to Serbian military intelligence through Colonel Dragutin Dimitrijević ("Apis"), the head of Serbian Army intelligence. This was not a lone-gunman event. It was a planned operation with state-adjacent connections, even if the Serbian government itself did not authorise or control the attack.

The operation was poorly executed. Of the seven conspirators, most either lost their nerve or acted at the wrong moment. One threw a bomb at the Archduke's car; it bounced off and exploded under a following vehicle, injuring several people. Franz Ferdinand was unharmed and continued to the town hall. The assassination succeeded only because the Archduke's driver, taking a revised route to visit the bombing victims in hospital, took a wrong turn down Franz Joseph Street and stalled the car in front of Schiller's Delicatessen — where Princip happened to be standing. At a distance of about five feet, Princip fired twice.

A note on the folklore The famous story that Princip was eating a sandwich when the Archduke's car appeared in front of him is a post-war embellishment unsupported by contemporary sources. Historians have traced the detail to later accounts and found no contemporaneous record of it. The wrong-turn and the stalled car are well documented; the sandwich is not.

The Serbian government's actual role remains a genuinely contested question. Vienna concluded that the conspiracy had been state-sponsored; the evidence points to a more ambiguous relationship — army officers with connections to the Black Hand acting with some official tolerance but not the government's direction. That distinction mattered little to Austria-Hungary's leadership in July 1914, who had decided the assassination provided the necessary occasion for military action regardless of the precise chain of command behind it.

What came next

By 4 August 1914 five of the six great European powers were at war. Every general staff plan in Europe had been built on the assumption of a short war — six weeks to twelve weeks at the outside. The German Schlieffen Plan required France to be defeated before Russia could fully mobilise; French Plan XVII assumed a rapid offensive that would recover Alsace-Lorraine by autumn. Russia expected its vast reserves to overwhelm any opponent given time. None of the belligerents had made serious plans for a war longer than one fighting season. They were all wrong by years. The next chapter covers what the war they had started actually became.

VI

War Aims, As They Changed

Wars expand their own aims. The mechanism is simple: once you have paid a large price for something, you raise the minimum you will accept in return. What a government would not have asked for in August 1914 — because no one would have agreed to fight for it — becomes the floor of what it can accept in 1918, because anything less makes the dead seem to have died for nothing. This is not strategy. It is the sunk-cost logic of grief, applied to foreign policy. Every belligerent in the First World War went through the same process, at roughly the same pace, arriving at roughly the same place: by the final year of the war, each power's aims were maximalist in ways that would have been politically unthinkable when the guns first opened.

The pattern ran in one direction. Starting aims were — with one significant exception — genuinely limited. Defending a treaty obligation, recovering a province lost forty years earlier, preserving an empire from a rival's encroachment. By 1916 the aims had grown. By 1918 they were comprehensive: total military defeat of the enemy, territorial reorganisation, financial penalties, guarantees against any future threat. The dead had been too many to justify anything less.

Britain

Britain went to war on 4 August 1914 for a specific, limited reason: Germany had invaded Belgium, whose neutrality Britain had guaranteed under the 1839 Treaty of London. Alongside that was the longer-term concern about German naval power — the fleet that Tirpitz had been building since 1898 was not large enough to threaten the Royal Navy directly, but its existence required a British response that had drained money and attention for fifteen years. In August 1914 the British war aim was to restore Belgian integrity and contain Germany's naval ambitions. Neither of those required Germany's destruction.

By 1916 those aims had been practically achieved, or were being achieved. The German High Seas Fleet had been unable to break British command of the North Sea. German colonies in Africa and the Pacific were being seized. The naval threat, in practical terms, was being managed. What had changed was the cost: Loos in September 1915 had consumed six divisions for minimal territorial gain. The Somme in July 1916 cost 57,000 British casualties on its first day alone. Passchendaele in 1917 added another 260,000 casualties for a few miles of Belgian mud. After each of those battles, the minimum acceptable outcome ratcheted upward. By 1918, the British government's position was that Germany must be fully disarmed, must pay reparations, and that no negotiated peace — no settlement that left Germany's military capability intact — was acceptable. The original war aim had been limited. The cost had made it unlimited.

France

France's opening aim was the clearest of any belligerent and the one with the longest history behind it. Alsace and Lorraine had been taken by Germany after the Franco-Prussian War of 1870-71. Recovering them was France's irreducible minimum from the first day of the war. Beyond that, the aim was security: an end to the permanent German threat on France's eastern border.

Those aims sound finite, but the cost transformed them. France suffered roughly one million casualties — killed or missing — by the end of 1916, a per-capita rate no other major belligerent matched. The northern industrial region, including much of France's coal and steel capacity, was under German occupation for most of the war. By 1916 the French war aim had grown to include reparations large enough to cover the reconstruction of the devastated north-east, plus a security guarantee that went beyond mere treaty text. By 1918, the aim had expanded further still: permanent weakening of Germany's military capacity, and a Rhineland buffer zone that would put physical distance between Germany and France's borders.

At Versailles in 1919, Georges Clemenceau could not get the Rhineland buffer he wanted — Britain and the United States would not agree to a permanent occupation or detachment of the region. He traded it for a US-British security guarantee promising that both powers would come to France's defence if Germany attacked. The guarantee was worthless: the US Senate refused to ratify the Versailles treaty, which meant the guarantee was never operative. Clemenceau had gone to the peace conference with France's 1918 war aims — maximalist, comprehensive, expensive — and come away with less than he needed. France's aims had grown with its losses; the settlement did not deliver them.

Germany

Germany's case is the one significant exception to the pattern of aims expanding over time, because Germany's aims were not limited to begin with. The Schlieffen Plan, which required defeating France in roughly six weeks before turning to face Russia, was premised on continental dominance — not defence, but restructuring. On 9 September 1914, six weeks into the war and before the plan had been confirmed as a failure on the Marne, Chancellor Bethmann-Hollweg drafted what became known as the Septemberprogramm. It specified Belgium reduced to economic and military vassal status, Luxembourg annexed outright, a Polish buffer state under German control, and French iron and coal regions in Lorraine seized permanently. The aim was a German-dominated continental economic sphere — Mitteleuropa — that would give Germany the resource base to operate as a genuine world power.

Those aims did not change direction between September 1914 and November 1918. They only became larger as the cost grew. The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, signed on 3 March 1918 after Germany had effectively knocked Russia out of the war, showed what German war aims looked like when Germany was winning: Russia surrendered Ukraine, the Baltic states, Finland, and parts of the Caucasus. The territorial transfers were vast. Germany did not moderate its aims as the war continued; it saw each year of costly fighting as justification for more, not less. The Septemberprogramm had named the destination; the casualties named the price that destination now had to justify.

Russia

Russia went to war in August 1914 with two interlocking aims: securing the Slavic Balkans against Austrian and German domination, and gaining control of Constantinople and the Turkish Straits. The Straits were the chokepoint through which Russian Black Sea grain exports reached world markets — whoever controlled them controlled a substantial fraction of Russia's export revenue. In 1915 Britain and France formalised the promise in the Constantinople Agreement: if the Allies won, Russia would receive the city and the Straits. It was a significant concession, and it locked Russian commitment to the alliance for two more years.

By 1917 those aims had become academic. Two million Russian soldiers were dead. Bread was running short in Petrograd. The army's capacity to sustain offensive operations had been broken at the Brusilov Offensive's terrible cost in 1916. When Tsar Nicholas II abdicated in March 1917, the Provisional Government that replaced him faced a choice: honour the alliance commitment, or end the war. It chose to honour the alliance, partly under Allied pressure, and that choice was its death warrant. The slogan Lenin's Bolsheviks campaigned on in the months that followed — peace, land, bread — fitted the army's exhaustion exactly. In October 1917 the Bolsheviks took power and opened negotiations with Germany. Russia's war aims had moved from imperial expansion in 1914 to ending the war at any cost by late 1917. Brest-Litovsk in March 1918 formalised the collapse: Russia surrendered more territory in one treaty than any European state had ceded since Napoleon.

Austria-Hungary

Austria-Hungary started the war with the most specific aim of any belligerent: punish Serbia for the Sarajevo assassination, restore Habsburg prestige in the Balkans, and demonstrate that the dual monarchy was still a functioning great power. Behind that aim was a deeper structural problem. The empire's eleven major nationalities — German-Austrians, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, Slovenes, Croats, Serbs, Romanians, Italians, Ukrainians, Slovaks — were held together by the dynasty, the army, and three common ministries. Punishing Serbia was partly about suppressing the South Slav nationalism that threatened to pull those nationalities apart.

The war accelerated exactly the process it was meant to stop. Austria-Hungary's armies suffered catastrophic losses against Russia in 1914 and 1915 — around one million casualties in the first months alone — and became increasingly dependent on German military support to remain operational. By 1916, Habsburg war aims had contracted to a single question: could the empire survive? By 1917 the new emperor, Karl I, was attempting secret peace negotiations through his brother-in-law that would have traded Austrian territorial concessions for an exit from the war. The negotiations failed, and the attempt became public, damaging Austria's standing with its German ally. By October 1918 the dual monarchy was dissolving from within — Czech, Slovak, Polish, and South Slav representatives were declaring successor states before the armistice was signed. The Habsburg aim of preserving the empire had failed, and the empire's end came not from military defeat but from the centrifugal force of its own nationalities choosing to leave.

The United States

The United States was neutral until April 1917, when Germany's resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare — targeting any vessel, including American ships — and the revelation of the Zimmermann Telegram, in which Germany proposed a military alliance with Mexico against the United States, made neutrality politically untenable. Woodrow Wilson had spent three years articulating an alternative to the European war's logic. His January 1917 speech to the Senate had called for "peace without victory" — a settlement that no side would experience as humiliation, a basis for a stable postwar order rather than a renegotiated grievance.

When the United States entered the war, Wilson's Fourteen Points in January 1918 became its formal statement of aims. Open diplomacy (no secret treaties), freedom of the seas, national self-determination across Europe, and a League of Nations to adjudicate future disputes. These were aims of a different order from anything any European belligerent had articulated: no territorial claims, no reparations demanded, no rival to be permanently weakened. The United States arrived at the Paris Peace Conference in 1919 as the only major power without a land claim in Europe, which was as close as the conference would come to an honest broker — and even Wilson's position was constrained, ultimately fatally, by a Senate that would refuse to ratify the Versailles treaty or join the League of Nations his Fourteen Points had designed.

The Ottoman aside

The Ottoman Empire entered the war on the Central Powers' side in October 1914, drawn partly by a secret alliance with Germany signed in August and partly by the opportunity to recover territory and prestige lost in the Balkan Wars of 1912-13. Initial war aims included reclaiming ground in the Caucasus and re-establishing Ottoman influence in the Balkans. By 1916 those aims had been compressed into survival. The empire was fighting simultaneously on four fronts: against Russia in the Caucasus, against British forces in Mesopotamia, against British and imperial troops in the Sinai and Palestine, and — having repelled the Allied attack at Gallipoli by April 1916 — against the continuing Allied pressure in the Aegean region. Each front was consuming resources the empire did not have to spare. The question was no longer what the Ottomans might gain from the war but whether the empire could hold together long enough to negotiate a tolerable exit.

What the pattern means

By November 1918, every belligerent's war aims were larger than they had been in August 1914. Britain wanted a disarmed Germany. France wanted reparations and permanent security guarantees. Germany had attempted, and briefly achieved at Brest-Litovsk, a continental empire. Russia had exchanged expansionist aims for peace on any terms. Austria-Hungary had ceased to exist as a political entity. The United States wanted a new international legal order. The Ottoman Empire was trying to negotiate its own survival.

The aims had been chosen, in each case, by the dead. Every government that continued fighting past the point at which the original aim could have been satisfied was implicitly arguing that the soldiers already killed had died for something — and that something had to be worth what it had cost. The alternative was to admit that hundreds of thousands, then millions, had died for a Belgian treaty obligation, or a disputed province, or a dynastic quarrel. No government in the middle of that loss could make that admission. So the aims grew. The dead became, in each country, the argument for demanding more — not from cynicism, but from the political impossibility of claiming that their deaths had purchased less than everything. By 1918, everything was the minimum.

VII

The Home Fronts

The wars the European general staffs had planned for were expected to be short, paid for from pre-accumulated reserves, and fought by existing armies on existing stockpiles. The Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71 had lasted seven months; the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–05 had ended in roughly eighteen. No government in August 1914 had a budget line for four years of industrial attrition.

What the years 1914–18 actually required was something new: the total mobilisation of every industrial, agricultural, financial, and human resource each belligerent possessed. By mid-1916 that transformation was complete across the major powers. By 1918 it had remade not only their economies but their political systems, their labour forces, their food supplies, and the social contract between states and citizens. The mechanisms were shared across countries even as the national outcomes differed — which is why the home front is better understood through what the war demanded of every society than through what each government decided to do about it.

Production

The shell crisis of 1915 was the moment every belligerent government understood that the war it had planned for and the war it was fighting were not the same thing. By the spring of that year, British, French, and Russian forces were rationing artillery ammunition. Guns were firing two or three rounds per day when operational doctrine demanded dozens. The stockpiles had run out, and the peacetime armaments industries — sized for peacetime — could not replenish them fast enough.

The responses were structurally similar even if the institutional forms varied. In Britain, David Lloyd George took over a newly created Ministry of Munitions in May 1915. The Munitions of War Act that followed gave the ministry sweeping powers: it could take over factories, set prices and wages, control raw materials, and direct labour. Within eighteen months the ministry was supervising over two million workers. The output figures are striking — in 1914 British factories produced roughly 500,000 artillery shells per month; by 1917 the figure was close to 76 million. The difference was almost entirely a product of state organisation.

Germany went through a comparable transformation, but later and more disruptively. The 1916 Hindenburg Programme — named for the commander appointed Chief of the General Staff after the grinding losses at Verdun and the Somme — demanded a total restructuring of the German war economy for maximum output. Steel, chemicals, rubber, coal: all came under state allocation. Private firms continued to operate but now served as contractors to a state-directed production system. France, with its most industrialised northern departments under German occupation from the first weeks of the war, converted what survived of its industrial base southward and westward, and leaned heavily on British and American credit to import what it could not produce.

Labour

The production surge created an immediate problem: the men who had run the factories in peacetime were being conscripted into the armies that needed the factories' output. Britain introduced conscription in January 1916 — the first time in British history — and drew around three million men into the army over the following two years. Germany had been conscripting since the start of the war. The workforce replacement problem fell most visibly on women.

By 1918, roughly one million British women were employed in war industries, approximately 700,000 of them specifically in munitions. They filled roles that pre-war social convention had placed entirely off-limits: operating heavy machinery, handling explosives, driving vehicles, supervising assembly lines. The French war economy drew comparable numbers proportional to its smaller population. German factories employed women in similar roles, and from 1916 onward also used forced labour drawn from occupied Belgium, Russia, and Romania — a practice that generated the same output but on very different terms.

The political consequence of women's industrial labour was not immediately obvious, but it was structurally difficult for governments to ignore. The Representation of the People Act of 1918 gave the vote to British women over 30 who met certain property qualifications. Equal franchise — women voting on the same terms as men — arrived in 1928. Germany extended the vote to women in November 1918 as part of the republican revolution that ended the war. The connection between wartime labour and postwar suffrage was not a simple reward for service; governments had several motives and the timing was not uniform across countries. What was true was that women's labour had become economically necessary in ways that could not be quietly undone once the fighting stopped.

Food

The Royal Navy's blockade of Germany tightened steadily from 1914 onward. Britain controlled the sea lanes through which Germany imported food, feed for livestock, and the nitrates needed to produce both fertiliser and explosives. By 1916 those imports had largely stopped. The German government introduced rationing early in the war, but the food supply itself was shrinking faster than the ration cards could track. The harvest of 1916 was poor. The potato crop, which had become the primary caloric staple for the urban working class, failed badly. The winter of 1916–17 entered German historical memory as the Kohlrübenwinter — the turnip winter — because turnips were one of the few things available in sufficient quantity.

For working-class urban populations in Germany that winter, daily caloric intake fell to roughly 1,000 calories — well below the level required for sustained physical labour. Malnutrition became visible in urban areas: falling birth weights, rising mortality from diseases that adequate nutrition might have contained, the systematic deterioration of the elderly. Estimates of German civilian excess deaths attributable to the blockade over the course of the war run to approximately 700,000. The figure is debated — separating blockade effects from other wartime disruptions is difficult — but the scale of civilian suffering was real and documented.

Britain, as a large food importer, depended on Atlantic shipping lanes under submarine attack from 1917. The government resisted formal rationing until January 1918; when it came, it was reasonably well administered. France struggled with distribution throughout — its occupied eastern regions, its transport network commandeered for military supply, civilian logistics consistently subordinated to the front. Russia's food problem was the most politically consequential. By the winter of 1916–17 bread queues in Petrograd were the visible symptom of a supply system that had broken down. The shortages did not cause the February Revolution on their own, but they were the immediate context in which it happened.

Politics

The war hollowed out three regimes during its course and broke a fourth at the end. The mechanism in each case was some combination of battlefield losses, economic exhaustion, and a political system that could not absorb the strain.

Russia went first. By 1916, roughly two million Russian soldiers had died at the front. The Tsar had taken personal command of the army in August 1915, which meant that subsequent defeats attached directly to his person. In Petrograd, bread shortages produced street protests in late February 1917 that the garrison refused to suppress. Nicholas II abdicated on 2 March 1917 (15 March by the Western calendar). The Provisional Government that replaced him — drawn from the Duma and committed to liberal constitutional principles — made the decision to continue the war on the pressure of Allied governments who wanted the Eastern Front maintained. That decision was its death warrant. Lenin's slogan in the months that followed was "peace, land, bread," and the army that had been ordered to keep fighting voted with its feet. Desertion became mass desertion; whole units simply stopped obeying orders and went home. The Bolshevik seizure of power in October 1917 (7 November by the Western calendar) was made possible by an army and a state apparatus that had stopped functioning. The government was not overthrown so much as the authority it claimed had already dissolved.

Italy's political crisis came from the battlefield. The Caporetto disaster of October–November 1917 — a combined Austro-Hungarian and German offensive — produced approximately 300,000 Italian prisoners and 400,000 deserters. The front collapsed to the Piave river. The government nearly fell, and the political damage — a population suddenly confronted with catastrophe after being told the war was going well — deepened the post-war instability that produced Mussolini.

Austria-Hungary's dissolution was slower and structural. By 1917 Czech, Slovak, Polish, and South Slav representatives were planning successor states openly. By the summer of 1918 these were not aspirations but active political programmes. When the armistice came in November, the monarchy did not negotiate the terms of its surrender — it ceased to exist as the peoples inside it declared their own states.

Germany's home front held longer but broke harder. The Hindenburg Programme had kept production high at the cost of civilian living standards; the working-class population of industrial cities bore the most direct burden of the blockade and the most visible deterioration in material life. By autumn 1918, with the army retreating in France and the spring offensives' promise of victory gone, the internal pressure became acute. Workers' and soldiers' councils — structures borrowed from the Russian revolutionary model — formed in Munich, Berlin, and other cities. The High Seas Fleet mutinied at Kiel on 3 November 1918 rather than obey an order for a final suicidal sortie. Bread riots spread through the cities. Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated on 9 November and fled to the Netherlands. A republic was proclaimed in Berlin the same afternoon. The home front did not cause Germany's military defeat, but it removed the political structure that would have had to manage any alternative outcome.

Casualties as Multiplier

The casualty figures interacted with every other mechanism. France suffered approximately one million military deaths or missing presumed dead by 1916 — a higher per-capita rate than any other major belligerent for the full war. British losses were heaviest in 1916 at the Somme and 1917 at Passchendaele; the scale drove political pressure for any alternative that might stop the bleeding. Germany conscripted skilled industrial workers just as the Hindenburg Programme demanded maximum output — the same men, in the same months, needed simultaneously at the factory and the front. Every government faced some version of this arithmetic, and none had a good answer.

The structural lesson that all of this pointed toward was about the relationship between the state and society. The pre-war assumption — shared by governments, by military planners, by financial officials — was that war was a matter for soldiers, financed from pre-war reserves, conducted at a remove from civilian life. The events of 1914–18 had made that assumption impossible to recover. States had entered into the management of labour, food, finance, and industrial production in ways that could not simply be unwound in 1919. The women who had made the shells could not be unvoted. The farmers whose production had been directed and rationed could not be told the market had resumed as if nothing had happened. The citizens who had paid for the war through bond drives, rationing, and the deaths of their families had claims on the governments that had demanded those sacrifices. The 1920s would spend a great deal of energy discovering what those claims actually were, and what governments were now expected to do about them.

VIII

1918 — The Collapse

The conventional reading of 1918 is that the war ended with the Armistice on 11 November. The structural reading is that four empires — German, Russian, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman — collapsed between March and November 1918. The Armistice was the last of the collapses, not the first. Russia went in March. Austria-Hungary dissolved from within before it signed anything. The German Republic was proclaimed two days before the Armistice was signed. The armistice document itself only formalised what had already happened to the German state.

That sequence matters because the normal framing — a war ending on a fixed date — hides the mechanism. What happened in 1918 was not a military conclusion reached on a battlefield. It was a series of political and institutional failures that made continued fighting impossible. Understanding those failures is the point of this chapter.

Russia out

The first empire to exit the war had been hollowing out since 1916. By early 1918 the Bolshevik government, which had taken power in October 1917 on a platform that included immediate peace, had no army capable of continued fighting. The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk was signed on 3 March 1918. Under its terms, Russia surrendered Ukraine, the Baltic states, Finland, and substantial parts of the Caucasus to Germany and its allies. These were enormous territorial losses — roughly one million square kilometres and about a third of European Russia's population.

From Germany's perspective, Brest-Litovsk was the maximum strategic gain the war had produced. It freed approximately fifty divisions from the Eastern Front for transfer to the west. In the spring of 1918, for the first time since 1914, Germany had a window of numerical superiority on the Western Front. The window was narrow. American troops were arriving in France at roughly 250,000 a month. Germany had to move fast.

The Kaiserschlacht and the turn

Germany's spring offensives, known collectively as the Kaiserschlacht, began with Operation Michael on 21 March 1918 and ran through four subsequent operations into July. They were the largest offensive Germany had mounted in the war, and they initially succeeded in ways nothing had since the opening weeks of 1914. The line moved. British and French units were pushed back. By July German troops were within 60 kilometres of Paris and the Allied position looked genuinely dangerous.

The offensives failed for a specific operational reason: they outran their own supply. German storm-troop tactics — infiltration rather than frontal assault — were effective at breaking through, but the follow-on divisions could not be fed, resupplied, or reinforced fast enough to hold and expand the ground taken. Each successive operation gained less and cost more. When the offensives finally stalled in mid-July, Germany had spent its last mobile reserves and had nothing left for a strategic defence of the territory it held.

Erich Ludendorff, who as First Quartermaster-General effectively commanded the German war effort, called 8 August 1918 "the black day of the German army." That was the first day of the Allied Hundred Days Offensive. At Amiens, Australian and Canadian corps, supported by 450 tanks and close air support, broke through German lines and advanced eight miles in a single day — a distance that had taken months of fighting in 1916 and 1917. It was not the casualties that shook Ludendorff; it was the fact that German units surrendered in large numbers without fighting. The willingness to keep dying had stopped.

The Hundred Days Offensive ran from 8 August to 11 November 1918. By that point roughly 2 million American troops were in France, with another 2 million training stateside. The Americans had arrived at precisely the moment German strength was spent. Allied forces pushed the German army back steadily across northern France into Belgium and towards the Rhine. There was no single decisive battle; the German line simply retreated and thinned, and the logistics of feeding a modern army in continuous retreat proved impossible to sustain.

Empires falling one by one

Bulgaria was the first Central Power to sue for peace. The Salonika Front, where Allied forces had been pinned since 1915, broke open in September 1918. The Bulgarian army could not hold, and the government in Sofia concluded it had no remaining strategic options. Bulgaria signed an armistice on 29 September 1918. This opened a path through the Balkans toward Austria-Hungary's southern flank at a moment when the Austro-Hungarian state was already disintegrating from within.

The Ottoman Empire signed an armistice at Mudros on 30 October 1918. British forces had taken Damascus and Aleppo in October; the Mesopotamian front had collapsed; the imperial government in Constantinople had no territorial coherence left to defend. The Mudros agreement gave Britain and France effective control of the Straits and access to Ottoman territory — the beginning of the post-war partition process that would run for five more years.

Austria-Hungary signed at Villa Giusti on 3 November. The document formalised something that had already happened. Through October 1918, the dual monarchy had disaggregated. Czech and Slovak representatives declared a state from Prague on 28 October. Polish leaders in Krakow declared a Polish government on 28 October. The State of Slovenes, Croats and Serbs was proclaimed in Zagreb on 29 October, and in Ljubljana on the same day. The Habsburg emperor Karl I issued a proclamation on 16 October allowing Austria to reconstitute as a federal state, but the national councils ignored it. The army was no longer coherent; units were going home by nationality. Austria-Hungary did not collapse because it lost the war. It collapsed because the political bonds holding it together — the shared monarchy, the common army, the three shared ministries — stopped being respected simultaneously. The military armistice signed at Villa Giusti was the formal record of a dissolution that had already taken place.

The German collapse followed a similar pattern but at greater speed. The German High Seas Fleet had not fought a major engagement since Jutland in May 1916. On 24 October 1918, the naval high command ordered it to sea for a final sortie against the Royal Navy — an action the sailors understood as a death ride with no strategic purpose. Sailors at Kiel refused orders on 3 November. The mutiny spread within days to Wilhelmshaven, Bremen, Hamburg, and Lübeck. Workers' and soldiers' councils, modelled loosely on the Soviet pattern, formed in Munich on 7 November and in Berlin shortly after. The Bavarian king Ludwig III abdicated on 8 November. The structures of the German state were coming apart not under military pressure but under the weight of four years of war and the news that the war was lost.

Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated on 9 November and crossed into the Netherlands the same day. Philipp Scheidemann, the Social Democrat, proclaimed the German Republic from a window of the Reichstag that afternoon — reportedly unilaterally, to prevent a communist republic being declared first from the Berlin Palace. The armistice was signed in a railway carriage in the Compiègne forest at 05:12 on 11 November 1918. Fighting ceased at 11:00.

IX

The Settlements

When people say "Versailles," they usually mean the entire 1919–23 peace settlement. That shorthand is understandable and wrong. The Paris Peace Conference produced five separate treaties — one for each defeated power — and when the Ottoman settlement collapsed, a sixth followed three years later at Lausanne. These treaties were signed with Germany, Austria, Hungary, Bulgaria, and the Ottoman Empire in succession. They were not one event but a system, with a shared architecture and a shared set of contradictions running through all of them. This chapter treats them as that system.

The Conference

The Paris Peace Conference opened on 18 January 1919 — the anniversary, chosen deliberately, of the proclamation of the German Empire in the same palace at Versailles in 1871. Delegations from twenty-seven Allied nations arrived. Germany was not among them. The defeated powers would not be invited to negotiate; they would be presented with terms and required to sign.

Substantive decisions were made by the "Big Four": Woodrow Wilson for the United States, Georges Clemenceau for France, David Lloyd George for the United Kingdom, and Vittorio Orlando for Italy. Wilson brought the Fourteen Points — the programme of open diplomacy, national self-determination, and a League of Nations that had framed the American case for entering the war in 1917. Clemenceau brought something different: forty-eight years of memory since the German army had last occupied Paris, and a determination that France would not live through a third German invasion in his lifetime. Lloyd George sat between them, wanting a settlement firm enough to satisfy a British public that had lost close to a million men, but loose enough not to destabilise the continent he still needed as a trading partner.

The structural problem was that the conference had to do three contradictory things at the same time. It had to punish the defeated powers enough to satisfy the domestic politics of countries that had spent four years and millions of lives on the war. It had to contain the Bolshevik Revolution, which had established a hostile state in Russia and was actively promoting revolution elsewhere in Europe in early 1919. And it had to build something durable. The three aims pulled against each other throughout.

There were also the wartime treaties. The 1915 Constantinople Agreement had promised Russia the Turkish Straits; Russia had since left the war and the Bolshevik government repudiated all such arrangements. The 1915 Treaty of London had promised Italy a substantial slice of Adriatic and Adriatic-adjacent territory in exchange for joining the Entente. The 1916 Sykes-Picot Agreement had divided Ottoman Arab territory between British and French spheres. Each of these pre-existing commitments constrained what the conference could actually do, regardless of what Wilson's principles required.

Treaty of Versailles — Germany, 28 June 1919

Germany signed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles — the room where the German Empire had been proclaimed — on 28 June 1919, five years to the day after the assassination of Franz Ferdinand. The symbolism was deliberate on every side.

The territorial terms stripped Germany of about 13 percent of its pre-war territory and 10 percent of its population. Alsace-Lorraine, taken from France in 1871, was returned. The Saarland was placed under League of Nations administration for fifteen years, with its coal mines transferred to French ownership to compensate for the deliberate German destruction of French mines during the retreat. The Rhineland — German sovereign territory — was demilitarised, with Allied occupation of its western zones for up to fifteen years. In the east, a Polish Corridor was cut through German territory to give the newly reconstituted Poland access to the sea; Danzig, a German-speaking city at the Vistika's mouth, became a Free City under League supervision rather than part of either Germany or Poland. Germany lost all its overseas colonies, distributed among the Allied powers as League "mandates."

The military clauses were comprehensive. The German army was capped at 100,000 men with no general staff and no conscription. The navy was capped; Germany was to have no submarines and no military aircraft. The fleet was scuttled by its own sailors at Scapa Flow in June 1919 rather than surrendered — an act of defiance that was also the last operational decision of the Imperial German Navy.

Article 231 — the "war guilt" clause — assigned responsibility for the war to Germany and its allies, and made that responsibility the legal basis for reparations. The reparations figure was not set at Versailles; it was fixed in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks. That headline figure was politically significant but economically staged: of the 132 billion, roughly 50 billion represented the sum Allied economists actually expected Germany to pay in the near term. The remainder existed largely to satisfy public opinion while remaining technically outstanding. John Maynard Keynes, who had attended the conference as a British Treasury representative, resigned in protest, returned to Cambridge, and published The Economic Consequences of the Peace before the year was out. His argument — that the reparations were economically irrational and would destabilise the continent — set the terms of the debate for the next century.

The Other Four Treaties

Austria signed the Treaty of Saint-Germain-en-Laye on 10 September 1919. The Habsburg dual monarchy had already dissolved — it ceased to exist in October and November 1918 as its constituent peoples declared their own states. Saint-Germain ratified that dissolution and defined what remained. The new Austria was a German-speaking rump of roughly 6.5 million people, stripped of everything that had made the Habsburg state a going concern: the Bohemian industrial base now went to Czechoslovakia along with around 3 million German-speaking Sudeten inhabitants; the South Tyrol to Italy; Galicia to the reconstituted Poland; the Slovenian and Croatian lands to the new Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (Yugoslavia). The treaty also prohibited Anschluss — union between Austria and Germany — even though a majority in both countries favoured it. Self-determination, as a principle, stopped where it threatened the stability of the new order.

Hungary signed the Treaty of Trianon on 4 June 1920, the last of the main European settlements. The terms were severe even by the standards of the settlement system. Hungary lost approximately two-thirds of its pre-war territory and three-fifths of its pre-war population. Transylvania went to Romania. Slovakia and Carpathian Ruthenia went to Czechoslovakia. Croatia and Slavonia went to Yugoslavia. Roughly 3 million ethnic Hungarians ended up outside Hungary's new borders as a result. The Treaty of Trianon has had a long half-life in Hungarian politics; it remained a live grievance in Hungarian political rhetoric in the 2020s, a century after it was signed.

Bulgaria signed the Treaty of Neuilly on 27 November 1919, losing territory to Greece, Yugoslavia, and Romania, and taking on a reparations burden of 2.25 billion gold francs. It was the smallest and least-discussed of the settlements, but the territorial losses — including Western Thrace, which cut Bulgaria off from the Aegean — produced the same irredentist dynamic as Trianon produced in Hungary.

The Ottoman settlement was more complicated. The Treaty of Sèvres was signed on 10 August 1920 with the government of Sultan Mehmed VI in Constantinople, but it was never ratified. Sèvres would have partitioned Anatolia: Greek administration over Smyrna (Izmir) and its hinterland; French and Italian spheres in the south; an independent Armenia in the east; a Kurdish autonomous zone; British control over the Straits zone. The map it proposed bore almost no relation to the military realities on the ground, where Mustafa Kemal had already been organising resistance from Ankara since May 1919.

The Turkish War of Independence ran from 1919 to 1922. Kemal's forces defeated the Greek army, expelled the French and Italian contingents through negotiation, and presented the Allies with a military fait accompli. Sèvres was voided. The Treaty of Lausanne, signed on 24 July 1923, recognised the Republic of Turkey within borders that included all of Anatolia and eastern Thrace. Lausanne also ratified a compulsory population exchange between Greece and Turkey: roughly 1.5 million Greek Orthodox Christians left Anatolia for Greece, and around 500,000 Muslims left Greece for Turkey. Most of these people had no connection to the country they were sent to except religion. Lausanne was the last of the post-war settlements and the only one of the six that a defeated power renegotiated by force.

Brest-Litovsk The March 1918 Treaty of Brest-Litovsk had taken Russia out of the war on German terms, surrendering Ukraine, the Baltic states, Finland, and parts of the Caucasus. The Allied settlements voided Brest-Litovsk after Germany's defeat — but they did not restore Russian sovereignty over those territories. Poland, Finland, Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania became independent states. The Bolshevik government accepted this, for now, as the price of survival.

The League and the American Absence

Wilson had made the League of Nations the centrepiece of his peace programme. The Covenant of the League was incorporated into all five of the main treaties, which meant that ratifying any of them meant joining the League. The League opened for business in January 1920 with 42 founding members.

The United States was not among them. The Senate had refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles in November 1919; it refused again in March 1920. Wilson's refusal to accept the Republican amendments proposed by Senator Henry Cabot Lodge contributed to the defeat, but the deeper problem was that a significant portion of American political opinion was not willing to commit the United States to the collective security obligations the Covenant implied. The country that had designed the League never joined it. The United States signed separate peace treaties with Germany, Austria, and Hungary in 1921 — treaties that obtained the benefits of the settlement without the obligations of the Covenant.

The League's structural weakness was fixed at birth. It had no military force of its own. Its enforcement mechanism depended on the willingness of member states — specifically the great powers — to act collectively. With the United States absent and the Soviet Union excluded (it did not join until 1934), the two states whose cooperation would have been most consequential were outside the system from the start.

Two Readings

Two interpretations of the settlements have dominated the historical argument. The first is broadly associated with Keynes: the reparations were unpayable, Article 231 was a political humiliation that destroyed any chance of German buy-in to the new order, and the Carthaginian peace the conference imposed was the proximate cause of the instability that followed. This reading became dominant in English-language public discourse almost immediately — Keynes's book was a bestseller — and it shaped the policy choices of the 1920s and 1930s, when British and American statesmen repeatedly chose appeasement partly on the grounds that the original settlement had been unjust.

The second reading is the comprehensive revisionist counter, most clearly stated by Margaret MacMillan in Paris 1919 (2001). MacMillan's argument is that the negotiators did the best that was possible in genuinely impossible circumstances. The reparations were politically necessary given what France and Belgium had suffered and what their electorates demanded; the sum actually expected to be paid in the short term was not economically irrational; and the failures of the 1920s and 1930s came not from the treaty itself but from the deliberate choice of the Allied governments not to enforce it. Germany rearmed openly after 1933; the Rhineland was reoccupied in 1936; neither Britain nor France moved. On this reading, Versailles was neither uniquely punitive nor uniquely destabilising — it was a settlement that its own guarantors abandoned.

Neither reading is wrong on its own terms. The issue's frame suggests both miss something larger. The negotiators in Paris were attempting to settle a 1914 world that no longer existed. The Habsburg state was gone; it could not be restored. The Russian Empire was gone and had been replaced by something explicitly committed to destroying the order the conference was trying to reconstruct. The Ottoman Empire was dissolving in real time during the negotiations. The system of 1914 — the multilateral financial order, the free movement of capital and labour, the sterling-anchored trade network, the imperial governance structures — had been destroyed by the war, not suspended by it. Paris 1919 was attempting to build a new order on the ruins of an old one, with a set of tools designed for the old one, while the most powerful state in the world declined to participate. The question of whether the settlements were too punitive or about right misses the prior question: whether a durable settlement was achievable at all, given what the war had actually destroyed.

X

Six Countries, After

Chapter V of this issue treated the six European great powers as they stood in 1914 — their political systems, their economies, their structural strengths and weaknesses on the eve of war. This chapter follows each of them through to the early 1930s. The purpose is not to catalogue what happened to each country between 1918 and 1933, but to explain why each arrived where it did. The war had destroyed an international order; these are the stories of how that destruction worked itself out inside six different political systems, at six different speeds, producing six different kinds of wreckage — and, in Britain's case, something that looked from the outside like stability but was in fact a managed retreat.

Britain

Britain won the war and lost the economic order that had made it the world's leading power. Before 1914, Britain had been a net creditor nation — it exported capital to the rest of the world, collected interest, and recycled those returns through the City of London into further investment. The war reversed that position. Britain sold its overseas assets and borrowed from the United States to finance the fighting, converting itself from the world's largest creditor into a net debtor for the first time in two centuries. Sterling had been the anchor of the pre-war global financial system; after 1918, the question was whether it could be again.

The answer that the British government gave in 1925 was yes. Winston Churchill, then Chancellor of the Exchequer, returned the pound to the gold standard at its pre-war parity of $4.86 to the pound. The mechanism of how gold convertibility worked meant that maintaining that exchange rate required British prices and wages to stay competitive at that level. The problem was that British industry had lost markets during the war, American productivity had grown faster than British productivity, and wages had risen. At $4.86, British exports were priced out of world markets. Coal was the sharpest case: British coal was more expensive than German or American coal at that exchange rate, and coal mines were the dominant employer in the north of England, Wales, and Scotland. When mine owners cut wages to restore competitiveness in 1926, the Trades Union Congress called the General Strike — nine days in May 1926 that tested whether the government or the union movement would break first. The government held. But the underlying problem — an over-valued currency making British industry uncompetitive — was not solved by breaking the strike. It persisted until 1931, when a banking crisis forced Britain off gold again. The 1925–31 attempt to restore the pre-war financial order at pre-war terms is the clearest worked example of what it meant that the war had ended that order: Britain tried to rebuild it and found that the world had moved.

Elsewhere, the empire expanded in the Middle East. The collapse of the Ottoman Empire left Iraq, Palestine, and Transjordan as League of Nations mandates under British administration. Ireland was partitioned in 1921: the Irish Free State left the United Kingdom after a war of independence; Northern Ireland remained. The Statute of Westminster in 1931 formally recognised that the dominions — Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa — had legislative independence from Westminster. Britain was still the centre of the largest empire in history. But it was no longer the centre of the global financial system.

Sterling parity The pre-war rate of $4.86 to the pound was not chosen arbitrarily in 1925 — it was the pre-war rate, restored as a signal that Britain was serious about stability. The signal was credible enough to attract capital. The problem was that the signal required the entire structure of British wages and prices to stay in alignment with that rate, which they could not do without deflation and unemployment.

France

France was the military victor of the war and the country most structurally damaged by it. About 1.4 million French soldiers died — roughly 4 percent of the entire population, and a far higher share of the military-age male cohort. Demographers called the cohort born around 1893–1900 la classe creuse, the hollow generation: fewer men of that age survived than had been born, and those who had survived were disproportionately the less fit, those who had been kept back from the front, those who had been lucky. The demographic distortion ran through French society for a generation.

The reparations settlement was supposed to compensate France for the damage done to its northern industrial region, which had been under German occupation for most of the war and had been systematically stripped of machinery and flooded in some cases before German withdrawal. The 1921 final reparations figure of 132 billion gold marks sounded enormous. The problem was collection. Germany paid in cash through 1922, then defaulted. In January 1923, France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr — Germany's industrial heartland — to extract reparations in coal and industrial goods directly. Germany responded with a campaign of passive resistance, paying its workers to strike rather than work for French extraction teams. The crisis ended in the German hyperinflation of 1923. The Dawes Plan of 1924 restructured the reparations timetable and brought in American loans to stabilise Germany. France had spent two years in an expensive confrontation and got less out of it than the original settlement had promised.

The Locarno Pact of 1925 — in which Germany accepted its western borders as permanent and agreed to arbitration over its eastern borders — and the 1928 Kellogg-Briand Pact, which formally renounced war as an instrument of national policy, were French attempts to lock the post-war settlement in place through multilateral agreements. They reflected a structural anxiety: France had won the war but could not afford to fight another one. The Maginot Line, the fortification system France began building along its eastern border in 1929, was the architectural statement of that anxiety. Its existence was an admission that France intended to fight the next war defensively — absorbing rather than projecting force — because the demographic and economic cost of 1914–18 had made any other posture politically impossible.

Germany

Germany emerged from the war as a republic — the Weimar Republic, proclaimed on 9 November 1918, two days before the armistice. The republic inherited not just defeat but the terms of defeat: the war guilt clause of the Versailles treaty (Article 231), reparations, the loss of Alsace-Lorraine and the Polish Corridor, a cap on the army at 100,000 men. German conservatives refused to accept that the army had been defeated in the field, preferring instead the Dolchstoßlegende — the stab-in-the-back myth — that revolution at home had robbed an undefeated army of victory. The republic's legitimacy was contested from its first day.

The mechanism of the 1923 hyperinflation is worth understanding specifically. The German state had financed the war by borrowing rather than taxing — the assumption had been that the indemnities extracted from France and Russia after a victorious peace would cover the debt. When Germany lost, that calculation inverted: the debts remained but there were no indemnities. Reparations then had to be paid in foreign exchange — specifically gold marks, not paper marks — because the Allies would not accept inflated German currency. Germany could only earn foreign exchange through exports. Its export capacity had been damaged by the war and by the loss of territory. When Germany defaulted on reparations in 1922, it was not simply refusing to pay; it genuinely lacked the foreign exchange to pay at the required rate. The government's response was to print money to cover domestic expenditure while the foreign exchange problem remained unresolved. By November 1923, the exchange rate had reached 4.2 trillion marks to the dollar. Middle-class savings denominated in paper marks were wiped out.

Stabilisation came in 1924 under the Dawes Plan, which restructured the reparations timetable and unlocked a stream of American private loans to Germany. The years 1924–29 were relatively stable: the German economy grew, unemployment fell, the republic functioned. The stability was built on American capital, which meant it had a structural dependency. When Wall Street crashed in October 1929, American banks began calling in their German loans. German unemployment rose from roughly 1.3 million in 1929 to 6 million by 1932 — about a third of the industrial workforce. The Weimar parliamentary system required coalition governments to command majorities, but no stable majority coalition could be assembled from parties willing to implement the deflation the creditors required and the welfare cuts the employers demanded. The parliamentary system had not collapsed — it had been put in a position where it could not build a governing majority against the structural shocks it faced.

The National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP) had won 2.6 percent of the vote in 1928. By July 1932 it was at 37.4 percent, making it the largest single party in the Reichstag. When President Hindenburg appointed Hitler as Chancellor in January 1933, he did so on the advice of Franz von Papen, who calculated that the Nazis could be managed in a coalition in which conservatives held most of the senior posts. That calculation was wrong within weeks.

Austria-Hungary

The dual monarchy did not survive the war. In late October and early November 1918, as the fighting ended, the successor nationalities declared their independence faster than the central government could respond. Czechoslovakia was declared on 28 October. The State of Slovenes, Croats and Serbs was proclaimed the same day. Poland re-emerged on 7 November. The Austrian rump parliament voted on 12 November to constitute German-Austria as a republic and to seek union with Germany. Hungary declared itself a separate republic on 16 November. The dual monarchy that had governed central Europe since 1867 ceased to exist in a fortnight.

The peace treaties then shaped what the successor states became. The Treaty of Saint-Germain-en-Laye reduced Austria to a German-speaking rump of about 6.5 million people — roughly the size of Switzerland, without Switzerland's economic self-sufficiency. The vote for union with Germany was explicitly prohibited. Vienna, which had been designed as the administrative capital of an empire of 52 million, was now the oversized capital of a small republic it had no obvious way to economically sustain. Austria spent the 1920s in a condition of perpetual fiscal crisis, dependent on League of Nations loans from 1923 onward.

Hungary's trajectory under the Treaty of Trianon was different in kind. Hungary lost about two-thirds of its pre-war territory and three-fifths of its pre-war population. Roughly three million ethnic Hungarians ended up outside Hungary's new borders — in Romania, Czechoslovakia, and Yugoslavia. The territorial losses were not a background fact in Hungarian politics; they were the central organising grievance. The brief Bolshevik regime under Béla Kun (March to August 1919) was suppressed by Romanian forces and replaced by a conservative regency under Admiral Miklós Horthy, who governed as regent for the absent king of a kingdom that technically still existed but had no king. Horthy's Hungary became authoritarian in structure, irredentist in foreign policy, and obsessive about recovering the lost territories — a posture that eventually brought it into alignment with Nazi Germany in the late 1930s.

The most successful successor state was Czechoslovakia, a parliamentary democracy that combined the Czech industrial west with the predominantly Slovak and Ruthenian east. It functioned as a constitutional republic from 1918 until the Munich Agreement of 1938 stripped it of the Sudetenland under German pressure. Yugoslavia — the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes until 1929 — struggled throughout the 1920s with Croat-Serb tension that made stable parliamentary government nearly impossible; King Alexander's royal dictatorship from January 1929 onward was the outcome. Poland was reconstituted after 123 years of partition between Russia, Prussia, and Austria, and fought wars against Soviet Russia (1919–21) and several smaller neighbours to establish its borders. The structural lesson of the Habsburg dissolution was straightforward: the dual monarchy had been the institutional container for national identity claims it was unable to resolve. Once the container was removed, each successor state had to build its legitimacy from scratch on the borders it had been given — and not all of them could.

Russia

The Bolshevik seizure of power in October 1917 did not end the violence in Russia. It began three more years of it. The civil war between the Bolshevik Red Army and the disparate White forces — supported at various points by British, French, American, and Japanese intervention — lasted until 1922, killing several million people through combat, famine, and epidemic. The Bolsheviks won because they controlled the major cities and the railway network, and because the Whites were politically divided and geographically dispersed. The Union of Soviet Socialist Republics was formally constituted in December 1922.

Lenin died in January 1924. The succession struggle that followed was resolved by Stalin's elimination of every serious rival between 1925 and 1928. Leon Trotsky was expelled from the party in 1927 and exiled in 1929. Grigory Zinoviev and Lev Kamenev were defeated politically by 1926. Nikolai Bukharin, the last serious alternative, was removed from the Politburo in 1929. Stalin's consolidation was not merely personal ambition operating in a power vacuum; it was driven by a specific political-economic problem that needed an answer.

The problem was this: the Soviet Union needed to industrialise rapidly — its leadership believed that without industrial capacity equivalent to the western powers, the USSR would be defeated in any future war, as Tsarist Russia had been defeated in this one. Industrialisation required imported machinery and technical expertise. Machinery had to be paid for in foreign exchange. Foreign exchange came from exports. The only exportable surplus large enough to generate the required foreign exchange was grain. But grain production under the New Economic Policy was controlled by the peasantry — specifically by the relatively prosperous farmers the Bolsheviks called kulaks — who had no incentive to sell grain to the state at artificially low prices. Collectivisation was the answer to that political-economic problem: compulsory consolidation of peasant land into state-controlled collective farms, which would allow the state to extract the grain surplus regardless of the peasants' preferences. The famine that followed in Ukraine in 1932–33 — the Holodomor — was the cost of that extraction, concentrated in the most agriculturally productive region of the Soviet Union. Estimates of the death toll range from 3 to 7 million. The grain was exported anyway.

We are fifty or a hundred years behind the advanced countries. We must make good this distance in ten years. Either we do it, or they crush us.

Josef Stalin, address to industrial managers, February 1931 — the explicit statement of why the pace of industrialisation was non-negotiable, and why its human costs were treated as subordinate to the strategic requirement.

The First Five-Year Plan (1928–32) achieved its industrial targets by the measures it used to enforce them. The Great Terror of 1937–38 then killed or imprisoned most of the senior military and political figures who had survived the previous decade. By 1939 the USSR was an industrial power, capable of producing tanks and aircraft at scale. It was also a state that had consumed a significant fraction of its own population in the process.

Collectivisation logic Stalin's grain problem was not invented — it was real. The NEP's market prices were too low to induce the surplus sales the state required. The choice was between raising prices (which the state could not afford), allowing shortfalls in industrialisation (which the leadership believed meant military vulnerability), or compulsion. Collectivisation was the third option, chosen because the other two were seen as existentially unacceptable.

Italy

Italy fought on the winning side and came away feeling cheated. The 1915 Treaty of London, which had brought Italy into the war on the Entente side, had promised substantial territorial gains: Trentino, Trieste, Istria, northern Dalmatia, a share of the Ottoman Empire's collapse. At the Paris Peace Conference, Italy received Trentino and Trieste but not most of Dalmatia — Wilson's principle of national self-determination cut against Italian claims to Slavic-majority territories. The Italian nationalist Gabriele D'Annunzio coined the phrase vittoria mutilata — mutilated victory — to describe the gap between what had been promised and what had been delivered. The phrase became the political organising principle of Italian nationalism for the next decade.

Post-war Italy was socially destabilised. Veterans returned to an economy that could not absorb them. Socialist parties made large electoral gains in 1919 and 1920. Factory occupations in northern industrial cities in the autumn of 1920 — the so-called biennio rosso, or two red years — alarmed industrialists and landowners. Benito Mussolini's movement, which had started as a disorganised group of nationalist ex-soldiers and syndicalists, positioned itself as the force that would crush the socialist threat. Fascist squadrons carried out organised violence against socialist offices, unions, and cooperatives throughout 1921 and 1922, with the tacit support of local authorities and landowners.

The March on Rome in October 1922 was less a revolution than a piece of political theatre. Mussolini mobilised his supporters to march on the capital; King Victor Emmanuel III, who had the constitutional authority to order the army to disperse them, declined to do so. He invited Mussolini to form a government instead. The Fascists were a minority in the coalition cabinet. But Mussolini consolidated power steadily: the Acerbo Law of 1923 changed the electoral system to give the largest party two-thirds of the seats; by 1925–27, the leggi fascistissime — the most fascist laws — had abolished opposition parties, independent trade unions, and a free press. Italy had the first fascist regime in Europe by 1922; its methods and its ideological vocabulary were studied carefully by nationalist movements across the continent.

The Ottoman Empire — a short account

The Ottoman Empire is not one of the six original great powers this chapter is tracking, but its dissolution between 1918 and 1923 reshaped the map of the Middle East and the eastern Mediterranean so completely that it requires a brief account.

Allied forces occupied Constantinople from November 1918. The Treaty of Sèvres, signed in August 1920, would have partitioned Anatolia among Greek, Italian, French, British, Armenian, and Kurdish zones, reducing the Ottoman state to a small enclave around the capital. The treaty was never implemented. Mustafa Kemal, an Ottoman general who had distinguished himself defending Gallipoli in 1915, organised a nationalist resistance movement in central Anatolia. The Turkish War of Independence (1919–22) expelled the Greek army — which had landed at Smyrna in 1919 under Allied sponsorship — and forced a renegotiation of the peace terms. The Treaty of Lausanne in July 1923 recognised the Republic of Turkey, with Anatolia and eastern Thrace as its territory. The Republic was declared on 29 October 1923. The Caliphate — the Ottoman Sultan's claim to leadership of Sunni Islam — was abolished in March 1924.

Lausanne also formalised a population exchange between Greece and Turkey that moved about 1.5 million Orthodox Christians from Anatolia to Greece and about 500,000 Muslims from Greece to Turkey. The exchange was compulsory; it produced enormous human suffering and permanently altered the demographic character of both countries. The Armenian Genocide of 1915–16 — which had killed an estimated 600,000 to 1.5 million Armenians — had already removed the largest Christian minority from Anatolia before the exchange. The post-war settlement had recognised an independent Armenia in the Treaty of Sèvres; Lausanne voided that recognition. The modern Republic of Turkey was thus built on the demographic foundations of two of the most violent population displacements in twentieth-century European history.

The pattern across the six powers is the pattern of the war's argument. In 1914, all six had political systems that had existed, in recognisable form, for at least a generation. By 1933, five of them did not. Germany was a one-party state under National Socialist rule. Russia had become the Soviet Union under Stalin's personal dictatorship. Italy was a fascist regime. Austria was governed by Engelbert Dollfuss's authoritarian Christian Social government, which suspended parliament in March 1933. Hungary had been an authoritarian regency since 1920. Only Britain remained a parliamentary democracy operating on roughly the same constitutional terms as it had in 1914 — though with a drastically reduced international position, a failed attempt to restore its pre-war financial role, and an empire in the early stages of the retreat that would take another four decades to complete. The five powers that had failed the political test had each been broken by a different mechanism: Germany by the combination of defeat, reparations, and the structural fragility of a coalition parliament under economic shock; Russia by the collapse of the Tsarist state under the weight of the war and the Bolshevik response to it; Italy by the gap between the war's promises and its peace; Austria-Hungary by its own internal national contradictions once the fighting stopped. Each failure had its own logic. All of them traced back to a war that began in July 1914.

XI

The Reshaped World

The previous chapter tracked six powers through the 1920s. This one steps back from the national stories and asks what kind of world replaced the one that existed in 1914. Five structural changes stand out: a new political map drawn on the ruins of four empires; the withdrawal of the one economy large enough to underwrite a stable international order; the collapse of the openness that had characterised pre-war trade and capital flows; the rise of communism and fascism as governing ideologies rather than dissident movements; and the continuation of European empire under a different legal label, in a political environment that had begun to erode its legitimacy. None of these five were accidents of personality or policy. Each was a direct consequence of how the war had been fought and how it ended.

The New Map

The armistices and peace treaties between 1918 and 1923 produced ten new states in central and eastern Europe and the Near East where four empires had stood. Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Czechoslovakia, a rump Austrian republic, a rump Hungarian state, and Yugoslavia all emerged from the Habsburg and Russian wrecks. The Republic of Turkey replaced the Ottoman Empire in 1923 after the Turkish War of Independence voided the Treaty of Sèvres. Ireland was partitioned in 1921 — the Irish Free State left the United Kingdom; Northern Ireland remained — making a tenth new political unit from the British isles.

The designers of this map worked, at least nominally, from Woodrow Wilson's principle of national self-determination: each people should live under its own government. The principle was sound in theory and unworkable in practice across a region where centuries of migration and empire had scattered populations so thoroughly that almost no border could separate national groups cleanly. The Treaty of Trianon cut Hungary's borders back so sharply that roughly three million ethnic Hungarians ended up living in Romania, Czechoslovakia, and Yugoslavia. Substantial German-speaking populations — the Sudeten Germans in Czechoslovakia, Germans in the Polish Corridor, the German community in Danzig — sat outside Germany's new borders. The Baltic states contained Russian and German minorities who had no desire to be minorities. The new map did not resolve the national grievances of central Europe; it redistributed them.

Czechoslovakia was the settlement's most successful product: a functioning parliamentary democracy through the 1920s and into the 1930s, with a diversified industrial economy and a stable civil service. Yugoslavia was the most precarious: three religions, two alphabets, and a political contest between Serbs who wanted a Serbian-dominated unitary state and Croats and Slovenes who wanted a federal one — a contest that Croatian and Slovene politicians would be losing by the late 1920s when King Alexander imposed a royal dictatorship in 1929. Poland was reconstituted after 123 years of partition, governing a patchwork of Poles, Ukrainians, Belarusians, and Jews, and adopting authoritarian rule under Józef Piłsudski after the coup of 1926. Every one of the new states carried the seeds of the next set of crises forward.

The American Century, Withdrawn

The United States emerged from the war as the dominant economy in the world by a wide margin. By 1929 US GDP was approximately 42 per cent of global output. American banks held most of the war debt that Britain and France had run up. The dollar and American capital had kept the Allies solvent from 1915 onward, and formal US entry in April 1917 had supplied the men whose arrival broke the military stalemate in France. Wilson had designed the peace settlement — the fourteen points, the League of Nations, the mandate system — and the US delegation was the largest and best-resourced at Paris.

Then the United States withdrew. The Senate refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles in November 1919 and again in March 1920, which meant the US never joined the League of Nations its President had created. The guarantees to France that had persuaded Clemenceau to accept a less punitive peace — a US-British security guarantee to defend France against future German aggression — lapsed when the Senate rejected the treaty. Congress raised tariff barriers with the Fordney-McCumber Tariff of 1922, then raised them higher with the Smoot-Hawley Tariff of 1930. The 1921 and 1924 immigration quota acts cut transatlantic migration sharply and introduced for the first time a national-origins system that privileged northern Europeans and excluded most Asians.

The structural problem this created was plain. Sterling could no longer anchor the global trading and financial system: Britain was a net debtor for the first time in two centuries, had returned sterling to gold at pre-war parity in 1925 at a rate that made British exports uncompetitive, and came off gold again under pressure in 1931. The dollar could have replaced sterling as the system's anchor, but the US political system had no appetite for the role. The world's largest economy wanted the benefits of international trade and none of the responsibilities of maintaining the order that made it possible. The Dawes Plan of 1924 and Young Plan of 1929 — American-brokered schemes to restructure German reparations by routing US bank loans through Germany and out to Britain and France as reparations receipts — were workable only so long as the loan flow continued. When Wall Street crashed in October 1929 and American banks called their loans, the entire mechanism collapsed. German unemployment hit six million by 1932.

Globalisation Undone

The world of 1914 had been more economically integrated than anything that followed it for decades. International trade ran at roughly fourteen per cent of world GDP. Capital moved freely across borders — international capital flows were about five per cent of world GDP in 1913, a level not exceeded again until the 1990s. About 1.2 million Europeans emigrated overseas in 1913 alone. The pound sterling, City of London finance, British shipping and insurance, and the gold standard together formed a system that settled most of the world's trade in one currency through one market.

The war dismantled this system at every level. Britain's overseas investments were largely liquidated to pay for the war. The gold standard was suspended in 1914 and only partially rebuilt between 1925 and 1928. International capital flows collapsed and did not return to pre-war levels for three-quarters of a century. Trade as a share of world GDP fell from fourteen per cent in 1913 to thirteen per cent at the post-war peak in 1928 — a recovery that took fifteen years and never quite closed the gap — then crashed to six per cent in 1932 as tariff walls rose and the Depression choked demand. By the mid-1930s the world economy was less integrated than it had been before anyone had fired a shot. The 1914 order had been dismantled, attempted in a weaker form in the late 1920s, and then destroyed again by the Depression and the trade wars that followed it.

New Ideologies as State Ideologies

The Russian Revolution did not just replace one Russian government with another. It placed Marxist-Leninist doctrine at the head of a large state with a foreign policy committed to spreading the revolution. The Communist International — the Comintern — was founded in March 1919 and immediately began funding and directing communist parties in France, Germany, Britain, and beyond. For the first time an ideology of radical economic transformation had an army and a treasury behind it. Every liberal government in Europe now faced a domestic left whose ultimate loyalty ran to Moscow rather than to the national parliament.

Fascism arrived in the same decade as a direct response to this. Mussolini took power in Italy in October 1922 — the first fascist head of government — after his movement had positioned itself as the forceful answer to labour unrest and the parliamentary gridlock that liberal parties could not resolve. The mechanisms differed by country: Hungary under Horthy (regent from 1920) maintained a conservative-nationalist authoritarian system rather than a fascist mass movement; Portugal under Salazar from 1933 built a Catholic corporatist state; Germany under Hitler from January 1933 combined racial nationalism, anti-Bolshevism, and a direct repudiation of the Versailles settlement; Spain under Franco after 1939 was a military-clerical dictatorship drawing on all three preceding models. These were not the same thing. They had local roots, different social bases, and different relationships to violence. But each defined itself partly in opposition to the 1918 settlement — specifically to the Wilsonian notion that liberal democracy and collective security were the natural post-war order — and partly in opposition to the Bolshevik model that the Comintern was promoting across the continent.

The League's structural problem The League's Covenant required unanimous Council votes to authorise action against an aggressor. Any great power could veto. It had no standing army, no automatic enforcement mechanism, and no American membership — the economy large enough to make sanctions effective had refused to join. When Japan invaded Manchuria in 1931, the League investigated, published a report, and Japan left the organisation. When Italy invaded Abyssinia in 1935, oil sanctions were proposed and then not applied. The League did not fail through lack of will alone; it was built without the constitutional capacity to compel the powers that mattered.

Liberal democracy was in retreat across Europe before the Depression made the process acute. Poland moved to authoritarian rule in 1926. Lithuania in 1926. Yugoslavia in 1929. The Depression accelerated the process: German unemployment at six million by early 1932 produced an electoral collapse of the centre parties and a combined Nazi-Communist vote of over fifty per cent in the July 1932 Reichstag election. The Weimar coalition system was not simply unlucky; it had been built on an economy that could not absorb the 1929 shocks and a political culture in which the parties of the democratic centre lacked both the parliamentary arithmetic and the social base to govern when conditions were severe. The structural inheritance of the war — the reparations burden, the exposure to American capital flows, the social dislocation of four years of total mobilisation — created the environment in which extreme ideologies were the inheritors of the failed centre.

Empire Continued, Weakening

The British, French, and to a lesser extent Belgian empires emerged from the war territorially larger than they had entered it. The League of Nations mandate system distributed the former German and Ottoman colonial territories among the victors under a legal framework that nominally distinguished mandates from colonies: Class A mandates (Iraq, Palestine, Transjordan under Britain; Syria and Lebanon under France) were territories considered close to self-governance that the mandatory power would prepare for independence; Class B and C mandates were more direct administration. In practice, Iraq, Palestine, Syria, and Lebanon were governed as British and French colonies with extra paperwork. South West Africa went to South Africa; German Pacific islands and Tsingtao went to Japan; Cameroon and Togo were divided between Britain and France.

The political cost of this expansion was already visible in the 1920s. Indian nationalism had been building throughout the war — 1.3 million Indians had served in British forces; the reward was the Rowlatt Act of 1919, which extended wartime emergency detention powers into peacetime, and the Amritsar Massacre of April 1919, when British-commanded troops fired on an unarmed crowd in a walled garden and killed several hundred people. Gandhi's first nationwide satyagraha (civil disobedience) campaign ran from 1919 to 1922. Egyptian nationalism produced a revolution in 1919 and a nominal independence in 1922 — nominal because British forces remained and Britain retained control of the Suez Canal and Sudan. The empires had won the war but were now governing populations whose political expectations the war itself had raised. Voting rights expanded sharply across Europe between 1918 and 1928: women gained the vote in Germany and the USSR in 1918, in Austria in 1918, in Sweden in 1921, in Britain for all women over twenty-one in 1928. France, which had sent two hundred thousand African colonial troops to fight for la patrie, did not extend the vote to women until 1944.

The Failed Reconstruction

The 1920s made a serious attempt to rebuild something like the 1914 order on the foundations that the war had left. The Dawes Plan (1924) and the Locarno Pact (1925) represented genuine stabilisation: American loans underwrote German reparations, the Rhineland evacuation was agreed, and the mid-decade period from 1924 to 1929 was one of relative growth and political calm across western Europe. This was not illusion. It was the window in which a different outcome was theoretically possible.

The window closed in October 1929. The American lending that had recycled reparations through Germany and back to Britain and France stopped. German unemployment destroyed the political coalition that had made Weimar work. The trade and tariff wars that followed the Depression turned the world economy inward. The gold standard, rebuilt imperfectly in the mid-1920s on a sterling-and-dollar base, could not hold: Britain left gold in September 1931, the United States in 1933, France and the gold bloc in 1936. Almost every major economy had left by the end of the decade.

What emerged from all of this was not the 1914 order rebuilt, and not the Wilsonian-League order that the peace settlements had attempted. It was something materially poorer, politically more fragile, and ideologically more dangerous than either. The destroyed-order frame names this outcome not as a failure of nerve by particular statesmen — though there were failures of nerve — but as the structural consequence of how the war had been fought and paid for. A war that mobilised entire economies, killed nine million soldiers, and ended four empires could not be followed by a settlement that simply restored what had been. The 1920s tried; the 1930s demonstrated that the attempt had failed.

XII

The Long Shadow

The war produced a great many long-term consequences, and it is tempting to list them. The temptation is worth resisting. A list of consequences — even a long, careful one — teaches nothing about how those consequences came to pass, and it is the mechanism that matters. What follows walks one chain in full: from the Bolshevik seizure of power in October 1917 through Stalin's industrialisation of the 1930s, through the German-Soviet war of 1941 to 1945, to the partition of Europe that followed. The other long threads are gestured at briefly at the close. One chain, walked carefully, teaches the pattern. The others can then be read the same way.

Step one: why the Bolsheviks could take power

The Tsarist state entered the war with structural weaknesses it could not repair under wartime pressure. By 1916 the army had suffered approximately two million dead, supply chains were breaking down, and food was not reaching the cities in sufficient quantities. Nicholas II had refused to share real political authority with the Duma since 1905, which meant there was no legitimate political institution capable of managing the discontent. When bread queues in Petrograd turned into street demonstrations in February 1917 and soldiers refused to fire on the crowds, Nicholas abdicated within days. The institution had no reserves of legitimacy to draw on.

The Provisional Government that replaced him made a decision that determined its fate: it chose to continue the war. The Allied powers pressed for this; the government's liberal leadership also believed that seeing the war through was the responsible course. The decision was politically fatal. Lenin's slogan in the months that followed — "peace, land, bread" — named exactly what the army and the hungry cities wanted and the Provisional Government was refusing to provide. The army voted with its feet. Desertion reached a scale no government could hold against. When the Bolsheviks seized the Winter Palace in late October 1917, they were not overthrowing a functioning state. They were occupying a vacuum that the war had created.

Step two: how the Bolsheviks survived

Seizing power and holding it were different problems. From 1918 to 1922, the Bolsheviks fought a civil war against the White armies — a loose coalition of monarchists, liberals, Socialist Revolutionaries, and foreign interventionists. The Whites lost because they were geographically fragmented, operating on the periphery while the Bolsheviks controlled the railways, the industrial heartland, and the food-requisitioning machinery. By December 1922 the USSR was formally constituted across the former Russian Empire's core.

Lenin died in January 1924. The succession was resolved not by formal process but by factional manoeuvre. Stalin had spent the civil war years building control of the party's organisational machinery — the Secretariat, the appointment of provincial officials, the patronage networks — and outmanoeuvred Trotsky, Zinoviev, Kamenev, and Bukharin in succession, not by winning policy arguments but by controlling who held which offices. By 1928 he had enough of the apparatus behind him to move.

Step three: industrialisation and its cost

Stalin's First Five-Year Plan, launched in 1928, aimed to transform a peasant economy into a heavy-industrial one in less than a decade. The method was forced collectivisation of agriculture: the state requisitioned grain from the peasantry at fixed prices, eliminating the independent farming class, and used the foreign-currency earnings from grain exports to purchase the capital equipment — machine tools, turbines, steel plant — the industrialisation drive required. The cost was enormous. In Ukraine in 1932 and 1933, state grain-requisition targets were met while the population was starving; the Holodomor killed an estimated three to seven million people. Millions more passed through the Gulag system in these years. The scale of coercion was the mechanism, not a side-effect.

The strategic result, however, was this: by 1939 the Soviet Union had the industrial base to fight a continental war. It had moved much of its new heavy industry east of the Urals specifically to put it beyond the reach of any invader from the west. It was producing steel, tanks, artillery, and aircraft at scale. That capacity had not existed in 1914. It was built, deliberately and at horrifying cost, in the twelve years between 1928 and 1940. Without the Bolshevik victory in the civil war there was no Stalin. Without Stalin's industrialisation there was no Soviet capacity to absorb the invasion of June 1941 and eventually reverse it.

Step four: the German-Soviet war

When Germany invaded the Soviet Union on 22 June 1941, the initial advance was the largest military operation in history: three million German and Axis troops across a front of nearly 3,000 kilometres. By December 1941 German forces had reached the outskirts of Moscow and encircled Leningrad. The Wehrmacht had destroyed enormous quantities of Soviet equipment and taken around three million Soviet prisoners in the first six months. On the face of it, the eastern front in late 1941 looked like a decisive German victory in progress.

It was not. The Soviet Union could replace what it was losing. Tank production east of the Urals — beyond the range of German bombers — continued through the winter; the T-34 was being produced in thousands. The Red Army of 1942 was learning rapidly from the catastrophic losses of 1941 and receiving the equipment to apply those lessons. The German army, by contrast, had stretched its supply lines to their limit and was fighting a war of attrition it had not planned for. Stalingrad (November 1942 to February 1943) and Kursk (July 1943) confirmed the reversal. Soviet forces entered Berlin in April 1945; Germany surrendered unconditionally in May.

Step five: the partition of Europe

At Yalta in February 1945, Churchill, Roosevelt, and Stalin met to negotiate the post-war order. The real negotiation was not about principles — it was about geography. The lines drawn at Yalta largely confirmed where the armies were. The Red Army held Poland, most of eastern Germany, Hungary, Romania, and Bulgaria. American and British forces held western Germany, France, Italy, and eventually Austria as a divided occupation zone. The Iron Curtain that descended across Europe from 1945 to 1989 followed those lines. The Cold War's geographical shape was determined by where the armies stopped.

Walk the chain back: the partition of Europe in 1945 followed from where the Red Army was standing at Germany's surrender. The Red Army was in Berlin because it had defeated the Wehrmacht in four years of industrial-scale warfare. It could sustain that industrial-scale warfare because Stalin's collectivisation and Five-Year Plans had built the capacity in the 1930s. Stalin was in a position to drive that industrialisation because he had consolidated control of the Soviet state through the party machinery after Lenin's death. Lenin's Bolsheviks had been in a position to seize and hold power because the First World War had destroyed the Tsarist state and left the Provisional Government unable to govern. The chain from the summer of 1914 to the Iron Curtain is causal, each step producing the conditions for the next.

The Cold War's geographical shape was determined by where the armies stopped in 1945. Where the armies stopped was determined by who had built the industrial capacity to push them there.

The other threads

The Bolshevik-to-Cold-War chain is one path. There are others, each traceable the same way if you choose to walk them.

The European empires that entered 1914 as the dominant organising structures of the globe came out of the two wars that followed structurally spent — in debt, with nationalist movements in India, Egypt, Algeria, and Indochina accelerated by the war's disruptions; the decolonisation of 1947 to 1975 ran along those fault lines. The United States, which entered the war in 1917 as a creditor lending to the Allies, emerged from 1945 as the organiser of the non-Soviet world, with Bretton Woods in 1944 installing the dollar as the global reserve currency in place of sterling. France and Germany, having initiated two continental wars in thirty years, built the European Coal and Steel Community in 1951 and the common market in 1957 as an explicit structural attempt to make a third war impossible — each step of integration traceable back to the same calculation. The modern borders of Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, and Israel-Palestine derive from the post-Ottoman mandate settlement of 1919 to 1923, drawn by Britain and France across a region that had been administered as Ottoman provinces. The 1918 influenza pandemic, which killed approximately fifty million people worldwide by spreading along the routes of troop movement, shaped the public-health institutions modern states built in its wake.

The chain matters more than the list

Each of those threads can be walked the same way: not as a named outcome, but as a connected sequence in which each step produces the conditions for the next. What the war removed, in the end, was the assumption that the prior order could simply be picked up again — that the trade networks, the financial architecture, the imperial governance structures, and the political institutions of 1914 could be restored after a period of disruption. They could not. The order that existed before August 1914 ended with it.

Deep Dive·26 May 2026

This issue was written in May 2026 using the destroyed-globalisation frame — the reading, associated with Adam Tooze's The Deluge, that treats 1914 as the moment a genuinely integrated world economy was ended, not paused. It is for anyone who wanted to understand not just what happened but what was lost, and why the order that followed was so much harder to hold together.

Images in this issue are drawn from Wikimedia Commons — public-domain WWI material digitised from the Library of Congress, the Imperial War Museum, and the Bundesarchiv — together with selections from the Wellcome Collection and the Commonwealth War Graves Commission.
