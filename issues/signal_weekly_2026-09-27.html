BAND 02 — THE LETTER FROM THE EDITOR

A week between the sacred and the switched-on.

A pope tells the machine-makers to slow down; a title race becomes a family feud; and something small lands on the electricity bill.

Twice this week a contest turned on a fraction. In Paris, Pope Leo XIV — the first papal visitor to France in nearly twenty years — stood in the Élysée and asked the people building artificial intelligence to slow down, to keep "the question of the human in play" before we drift into a paradise of machines. The loudest ethical brake on this year's AI boom came from Rome, landed in the most secular capital in Europe, and drew near-silence from Silicon Valley.

The other contest was literal: Formula 1 ran its first-ever Saturday grand prix in Baku, and George Russell held it by 0.196 seconds — a blink across fifty-one laps — while the championship quietly became a Mercedes family argument, Kimi Antonelli now sixty-six points clear of his own team-mate. Then, on Thursday, something smaller lands: VAT comes off the domestic electricity bill, about £45 a year back in the meter. A pope, a photo finish and a tax cut don't obviously belong on the same page — except that each, in its way, is an argument about who is still holding the wheel.

— The Editor

A POPE, A PRESIDENT AND THE MACHINES

Rome brings the AI question to Paris — and the machines' makers say nothing

Pope Leo XIV opened a four-day state visit to France on Friday, the first by a pope in nearly twenty years, and put artificial intelligence at the centre of it: a warning at the Élysée against losing our humanity to a "paradise of machines." The loudest ethical challenge to this year's AI build-out came from Rome, landed in secular Paris — and was met with near-silence from the people building the machines.

BY THE EDITOR · PARIS · 27 SEP 2026

Pope Leo XIV arrived in Paris on Friday for a four-day state visit — the first by a pope to France in nearly twenty years — and he did not spend it on the things a papal visit usually turns on. He spent it on artificial intelligence. Standing at the Élysée Palace beside President Emmanuel Macron, the first American pope warned that a world built too quickly around thinking machines risks becoming a "paradise of machines," a place where human judgement is slowly conditioned out of daily life. It was the sharpest ethical challenge anyone has put to this year's AI build-out, and it was delivered not from a select committee or a research lab but from the papacy, in the most self-consciously secular republic in Europe.

The words were carefully chosen, and worth hearing in full. This was not an aside dropped into a longer speech about faith; it was the speech.

Leo has made AI the signature theme of a papacy only sixteen months old, and the Élysée remarks gave the theme its bluntest phrasing yet. Newsweek framed them as a warning that the technology could become "an instrument of domination" — a tool that concentrates power over people rather than serving them. The precautionary case he was making has a definite shape: that the speed of deployment has outrun the work of deciding what these systems should be allowed to do, and that the gap between the two is being filled by default rather than by choice. On this reading the intervention is welcome precisely because it comes from outside the industry — a moral brake applied by someone with no product to ship and no funding round to close, speaking for the people on the receiving end of the machines rather than the engineers at the controls.

FIG. 02 Pope Leo XIV, the first American pope, in a 2025 portrait. Artificial intelligence has been the recurring theme of his sixteen-month papacy — and, he insists, not because he wants the technology stopped.Vatican Media · CC BY-SA 4.0 · Wikimedia Commons

What Leo was not doing is calling for the machines to be switched off, and the distinction matters because it is the one most easily lost in the reporting. He said so plainly.

That is not the sermon of a man who wants the technology banned. It is an argument about pace and about agency: not whether AI should exist, but how fast it should be woven into schools, courts, hospitals and homes, and whether the people living with it still get to decide. The "Luddite pope" caricature — the church squaring off against the future — does not survive contact with what he actually said. Leo's quarrel is not with the tool. It is with the assumption that because a thing can be automated it must be, and quickly, and without anyone first being taught to ask what is lost. He wants the question of the human kept open at the point where it is most tempting to close it.

Macron, receiving him, did not treat the warning as an awkward lecture from a guest. He answered it in his own secular register and, strikingly, in agreement: in the face of digital power, he said, "human beings must retain their rightful place," and "artificial intelligence must be a servant, not a master" — "the digital revolution must liberate, not enslave." Coming from the president of a republic whose founding principle is laïcité, the strict separation of church and state, it was an unusual harmony. The head of secular France and the head of the Catholic Church, standing in the same courtyard, reached for almost the same warning about the same machines — and neither of them, notably, was one of the people actually building them.

And then there was the silence. The natural people to answer a charge like this are the ones building the systems — the executives whose products the pope was, in effect, describing. NBC News, canvassing the industry, found the AI boom's most prominent figures mostly silent: the highest-profile executives declined to weigh in, even as some lower-profile industry voices signalled quiet support for his overarching message. No rebuttal, no engagement, no defence of the pace. For a sector that markets itself as the most consequential force of the age and rarely misses a chance to say so, the decision to say nothing to the most widely covered ethical critique of the year is itself a kind of statement.

There are two honest ways to read that silence, and they do not sit together comfortably. The first is the one Leo's own words invite: that the industry has no good answer, that the pace is indefensible on its merits, and that the safest response to a moral critique you cannot rebut is to let it pass without a reply and trust the news cycle to move on. The second reading takes the same silence and finds it entirely rational — and it is the stronger objection to everything the pope said.

THE CASE AGAINST · THE ACCELERATION READING

Perhaps nothing was said because a warning with no lever behind it changes nothing.

The acceleration case answers the silence by pointing at what the silence is made of. A papal address, however widely reported, binds no company and no government; the real contest over AI is being fought between firms racing one another and states racing rivals, and a moral appeal with no enforcement mechanism does not enter that fight. On this view, slowing down is not caution but forfeit — a lab that eases off cedes the ground to one that will not, and a country that regulates hard simply watches the work move offshore. The pace the pope wants to check is, to the people setting it, the whole point: the benefits in medicine, discovery and productivity arrive faster too, and every month of delay carries a cost measured in things not cured and not found. Read this way, the quiet was not evasion but a calculation that the argument was not one worth having in public — and that engaging would only lend a sermon the weight of a negotiation.

Both readings describe the same four days. What they disagree about is whether Leo's intervention was a serious brake or a well-covered gesture, and the events themselves will not settle it — a warning can be both morally clarifying and practically inert, and this one may turn out to be exactly that. But the geometry of the week is harder to argue with than either verdict. The most forceful ethical challenge to the AI build-out this year did not come from a regulator, a rival company, or a court. It came from Rome, was delivered in the most secular capital in Europe, drew an unusually warm echo from a president sworn to keep religion out of the business of the state — and was met, from the people with the most direct power to act on it, with near-silence.

That is a strange alignment, and it tells you something about where the argument now sits. A moral case about machines is being carried by the one institution most of Silicon Valley long assumed had nothing useful to say about the future, to an audience that mostly declined to reply. Leo has framed the question he wants kept in play — not whether to build, but how fast, and who stays in charge of the answer. Whether anyone building the machines chooses to answer it back is, for now, the open part of the story.

BAND 06 — THE TOUCHLINE SPORT
FIG. Kimi Antonelli's Mercedes — the man who leads the drivers' championship, 66 points clear of the team-mate who out-raced him in Baku.FIA F1 Austria 2026 · CC BY-SA 4.0 · via Wikimedia Commons

The title race has become a family argument — and it played out, for the first time in the sport's history, on a Saturday. Formula 1 pulled the Azerbaijan Grand Prix forward a day to clear a national day of mourning, and George Russell won it around the walls of Baku by 0.196 seconds, the narrowest winning margin in Formula 1 for more than twenty years. But the man on the top step is not the story of the weekend. The story is the two Mercedes sitting either side of the championship: Kimi Antonelli, who clawed back to fifth from sixteenth on the grid, now leads the drivers' standings on 302 points; Russell, the actual race winner, sits second on 236. Sixty-six points, one garage, two team-mates.

Tuned · 98.4 · Baku & Turin
98.4 · AZERBAIJAN GP · BAKU

Russell holds on by 0.196s

George Russell led from pole to flag around the Caspian waterfront and beat Max Verstappen to the line by 0.196 seconds — the closest F1 finish in over twenty years — with Isack Hadjar third for a Red Bull double podium. Charles Leclerc took fourth and Lewis Hamilton sixth on a quiet afternoon for Ferrari. 51 laps, decided by a blink.

What makes that a problem for Mercedes rather than a happy one is the arithmetic. Antonelli crashed in qualifying, started sixteenth, and still finished the day ahead of Russell in the championship; Russell out-qualified him, out-raced him, won the grand prix, and remains 66 points adrift. With the season into its closing rounds, a team that expected to be managing a single number-one driver instead has two drivers clear of the field and one title to divide between them. The fight for this championship is now being had inside a single team — and it is the driver who did not win in Baku who is winning the war.

BAND 07 — PIXEL & BYTE GAMING

Everyone gets out of the way — the autumn games calendar has one immovable date on it, and the rest of the schedule is quietly rearranging itself around it. Grand Theft Auto VI locks in for 19 November on PS5 and Xbox Series X|S, and Rockstar's launch is the one nobody wants to open the shutters against. So September and October have become a scramble to get out ahead of it: a definitive edition here, a long-awaited boxed release there, everyone clearing the runway before the biggest game of the year lands and the oxygen leaves the room. This is less a week of new releases than a week of studios reading the calendar and blinking first.

  • 104.7

    Silksong gets a box, and a date

    The long wait was for the game; the newer wait was for something to put on the shelf. Team Cherry has now confirmed and dated physical editions of Hollow Knight: Silksong, already out digitally, with the Nintendo Switch 2 edition ($45 — reversible cover art, a 32-page manual and a folded poster) shipping 16 October. The timing is not an accident: a boxed release that slips much later risks landing straight into GTA VI's shadow, so mid-October is the sensible window to grab. 16 October, Switch 2.

    Ghost of Yotei, completed

Inside: a new story expansion, "Echoes of Sekigahara", which brings Jin Sakai back into the frame, and a roguelike survival mode called "Most Wanted". For anyone who held off at launch, the complete package a fortnight before the crunch really bites is the version to wait for. 1 October, PS5.

  • 106.1

    The gravity well

    What ties the month together is the thing not out yet. GTA VI's 19 November date is functioning as a deadline for the entire industry — a wall everyone would rather land in front of than behind. That makes the run-up to it, counter-intuitively, a good few weeks to be a player with a backlog: a genuine spread of releases, all trying to be seen before the calendar goes quiet. Clear the runway now; November belongs to one game. Backlog before the storm.

  • Hollywood, Ending

    John Green · literary fiction · his first adult novel

    Green built a readership on young-adult novels that took teenagers seriously; "Hollywood, Ending" is the first book he has written explicitly for grown-ups. It is a behind-the-scenes love story set in an industry that runs on performance, and its real subject is trust — how you decide to believe another person in a world engineered to be untrustworthy. The familiar Green machinery is here (smart people talking themselves into and out of feeling), pointed now at adult stakes rather than the last summer of childhood. The reviews frame it as a genuine step across, not a young-adult novel with the ages revised upward. A known name in an unfamiliar register, and the safest place on this list to start.

  • Incarnate

    Alma Katsu · literary horror · the discovery pick

    Katsu has spent a decade folding real history into horror, and "Incarnate" turns that instinct on the present: a reimagining of "The Picture of Dorian Gray" for the internet age, where the portrait ageing in the attic becomes something closer to a carefully curated feed. It is the book on this list furthest from the usual shelf — no dragons, no epic map — and the better for it. If the appeal of Wilde was always the bargain and the rot underneath the beautiful surface, Katsu has found the contemporary machine that makes exactly that bargain at scale.

  • Crone

    Keith Rosson · folk-horror · a craft pick

    Rosson is a writer other writers keep pointing at, and "Crone" is the kind of book that explains why. A father goes looking for his missing daughter and finds something darker waiting at every turn — a premise that could be a straight thriller but bends steadily toward folk-horror, where the landscape itself seems to be keeping secrets. It is the pick here for the sentences as much as the story: propulsive dread built line by line rather than jump by jump, the sort of book that trusts atmosphere to do the frightening. Three very different registers, then — reinforcement, discovery and craft — for one nightstand.

BAND 10 — THIS WEEK IN HISTORY HISTORY · DATED

On the night of 27 September 1066 — 960 years ago this week — a fleet that had waited most of a month for the wind to turn finally slipped out of the Norman harbours and pointed north across the Channel, carrying with it an invasion that would remake England from the throne down.

The Conquest · Autumn 1066, In Order

The man behind it was William, Duke of Normandy, who held that the childless Edward the Confessor had promised him the English crown and that Harold Godwinson had sworn on holy relics to support the claim. When Harold took the throne himself in January 1066, William spent the summer building the case in the eyes of the Church and building ships on the Norman coast — a purpose-built invasion fleet, raised alongside an army of Normans, Bretons and Flemish mercenaries drawn by the promise of English land. Then he sat waiting, first at the mouth of the Dives and later at Saint-Valery-sur-Somme, for a southerly wind that stubbornly would not come. Weeks passed on the shore; men and horses had to be fed and held together while the weather refused to cooperate. The wind came round at last on the evening of 27 September. The ships put to sea overnight and made landfall at Pevensey, on the Sussex coast, on the morning of the 28th, where the army dug in and threw up a fortification almost before it had unloaded.

The timing was cruel for Harold. Days earlier he had beaten off a second invasion entirely — Harald Hardrada of Norway, killed at Stamford Bridge in Yorkshire on 25 September — and now had to turn an exhausted army round and force-march it the length of the country. The two sides met near Hastings on 14 October 1066. It was a long day's fighting, and it ended with Harold dead on the field — by tradition, though not certainly, from an arrow to the eye — his shield-wall broken and the last Anglo-Saxon king of England gone. William was crowned at Westminster Abbey on Christmas Day.

What followed was less a change of monarch than a change of country. A French-speaking aristocracy replaced the old English one almost wholesale; within twenty years barely a handful of the great landholders recorded in England were English. Castles went up across the land as instruments of control, and when the north rose against the new order William answered with the Harrying of the North, a deliberate devastation of Yorkshire and its neighbours whose scars the tax records still showed a generation later. The tenurial system, the church hierarchy and eventually the language itself were reshaped by the newcomers — the surveyors of the Domesday Book, compiled in 1086, were in effect counting a country that had changed hands.

The crossing that began it all survives most vividly in the Bayeux Tapestry, the near-contemporary embroidery — some seventy metres of wool worked on linen — that narrates the whole campaign in pictures. It shows the ships being built, provisioned and loaded with horses, then driven out under a following wind with their sails full: the closest thing the eleventh century left us to a photograph of the day the wind finally turned. Nine hundred and sixty years on, it is still the image the story is told through.

NASA has switched on the biggest digital eye it has ever built for the sky — and the first thing it sent back looked like a field of out-of-focus green doughnuts. That is exactly what the engineers wanted to see.

FIG.First light, September 2026: the Wide Field Instrument's debut frame, deliberately defocused so engineers can align the optics — the "green donuts" that mean the 300-megapixel detector is alive.NASA / Roman Space Telescope team · public domain

This week the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope powered on its Wide Field Instrument for the first time, and the camera behind it is enormous: 300 megapixels, feeding a field of view roughly 100 times larger than Hubble's. That last number is the whole point of the mission. Roman sees the sky at the same fine resolution Hubble does — it just sees vastly more of it in a single shot. Where Hubble peers through a keyhole, Roman looks through a picture window.

The debut images are not meant to be pretty. Each star appears as a fuzzy green ring because the instrument was pointed slightly out of focus on purpose, a standard first step that lets the team check the detector, the electronics and the optics before anything is sharpened up. A clean power-on with the whole 300-megapixel array reading out is the milestone here, not the picture itself — proof that the primary instrument survived launch and the journey, and that its electronics respond as designed. The green tint is just the false colour the team assigned to the raw readout; the doughnut shape is the signature of a mirror doing its job before final focus.

FIG.The observatory itself, in a 2025 NASA render — Hubble-class optics wrapped around a far wider survey camera, now bound for the L2 point.NASA / GSFC · public domain · via Wikimedia Commons

Roman is now heading for the L2 point, the gravitationally stable spot about 1.5 million kilometres beyond Earth where the James Webb telescope also sits, with science operations planned to begin in 2027. Once it starts working in earnest, that wide field is what turns it into a survey machine: instead of studying one object at a time it can sweep huge patches of sky, mapping billions of galaxies to trace how dark energy is stretching the universe and catching the brief brightenings that reveal planets around other stars. For now, the green doughnuts will do. The camera works, and the successor to Hubble's wide gaze is coming online.

BAND 12 — (ROTATING)

The old gym anxiety — that every mile run quietly undoes a set of squats — turns out to be mostly wrong. A new umbrella review pooling the concurrent-training meta-analyses lands the answer plainly: training strength and endurance together does not blunt whole-muscle growth compared with lifting alone, in most cases. The muscle still gets bigger.

What the evidence does flag is narrower and more useful than the blanket "interference effect" the worry is built on. Two findings carry the practical weight. First, session order matters for the nervous system: putting the strength work first optimises neuromuscular adaptation — the strength and power gains that depend on the muscle firing hard and coordinated, rather than on raw size. Do the hard lifting while fresh, and it is the endurance work that pays the fatigue tax, not the other way round.

Second, the interference that does show up is dose- and mode-dependent. It appears mainly at the demanding end — a high volume of endurance work — and, tellingly, it is worse when that endurance work is running rather than cycling. The pounding, eccentric load of running competes with recovery and with strength adaptation in a way that the smoother, low-impact pedal stroke of a bike largely does not. A couple of easy jogs a week is not the problem the review is pointing at; a heavy running block stacked on top of a heavy lifting block is.

Read as general guidance for anyone chasing a bigger lift and a decent 10k at the same time, the two reviews point the same way: keep the hardest runs and the hardest lifts on separate days where the schedule allows, and when they have to share a session, lift first. Where the goal is to protect strength and size, cycling is the gentler cardio partner to pair with the barbell. The takeaway is not "pick one" — it is "sequence and dose them, and the two goals mostly coexist." The sources are the umbrella review and the sequencing work behind it.

MONEY · THE ELECTRICITY BILL

A tax change lands on the household electricity bill this Thursday. From 1 October, VAT on domestic electricity drops from 5% to 0% — worth around £45 a year to a typical home, and the first cost-of-living measure from the Burnham government to arrive as a number on a real bill rather than a line in a speech. It is temporary, running to 31 March 2027, and it is funded by scrapping the planned digital ID programme. Suppliers apply it automatically: there is nothing to claim, no form, no switch to flip, and it lands whether the tariff is fixed, variable or prepayment.

The distinction worth holding onto is that the cut is on electricity only — gas VAT stays at 5%. On a dual-fuel bill the two are metered and taxed separately, so the 0% should show against the electricity charge alone, and a bill that quietly applied it to the whole thing (or to neither) would be wrong in the household's favour or against it. It is a small saving — on a typical bill, closer to a few pounds a month than a windfall — but it is one of the few this year that arrives without anyone having to chase it, which also makes it one of the easiest to lose track of. And because it is temporary, it is worth treating as a rebate with an expiry date rather than a permanent shift in the running cost of a home. The MoneySavingExpert breakdown has the mechanics.

DO THIS WEEK · THE LEDGER

Check the first dual-fuel bill after 1 October splits the cut correctly — the 0% should show on the electricity portion only, with gas still at 5%.

The saving is automatic, so nobody is prompted to verify it; a mis-applied rate on a combined bill is exactly the kind of quiet error that goes unnoticed precisely because the money involved is modest.

This week a pope stood in Paris and sent a message to the future about the machines we are building. Almost exactly eighty-eight years earlier, an American electrical company sent a message to the future too — literally, into the ground. In the autumn of 1938, on the site being cleared for the New York World's Fair at Flushing Meadows, Westinghouse lowered a seven-and-a-half-foot torpedo of a container fifty feet into the earth and sealed it not for a decade or a century but for five thousand years. It is not to be opened until the year 6939.

Inside went a portrait of 1938 assembled for strangers unimaginably far away. There was a copy of Life magazine and a Sears, Roebuck catalogue — the era's twin windows onto how Americans lived and what they bought. There was a Kewpie doll, a slide rule, a pack of Camel cigarettes, a fountain pen, and seeds of the common food crops. Most valuable of all was more than ten million words on microfilm: newsreels, technical manuals, a survey of art and science, and letters from Albert Einstein and Thomas Mann written to whoever, or whatever, might one day read them. The vessel itself was cast from "Cupaloy," a copper alloy engineered to outlast fifty centuries of burial.

The hard part was never the burying; it was making sure anyone would ever find the thing, or understand it once they had. Five thousand years is long enough for English to go as dead and unreadable as Sumerian. So Westinghouse printed a slim volume, the Book of Record of the Time Capsule, giving the exact location, instructions for reconstructing the English language from its sounds, and an appeal to future generations to keep re-copying the book so the knowledge would never lapse. Copies were posted to libraries, museums and monasteries around the world — entrusted to the one kind of institution with a proven record of carrying words across the millennia.

And that is the quiet gamble at the bottom of the shaft. The capsule assumes there will be a 6939, that someone will still be curious, that some archive's distant successor will still hold a legible copy of the map. It is a message with no guaranteed reader — a bottle thrown not into the sea but into deep time, on the faith that the future will want to know us. A second capsule joined it in 1965; both now wait together beneath a granite marker in what is today a public park, counting down the some 4,900 years still to run. The Pope worried this week about handing the future over to the machines. Westinghouse, in 1938, simply handed the future a box, and trusted it to care.
