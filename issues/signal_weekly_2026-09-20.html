The long way round

507 years ago this week, five ships slipped out of a Spanish river mouth to prove you could reach the East by sailing west. Three years later one of them came home — battered, half-crewed, and a full day out of step with the calendar on shore. That missing day was the strangest souvenir of the first lap of the world.

BY THE EDITOR · SANLÚCAR DE BARRAMEDA · 1519–1522 · A STANDING STORY

On 20 September 1519, five ships worked out of Sanlúcar de Barrameda, where the Guadalquivir meets the Atlantic, and turned southwest. About 270 men were aboard the Trinidad, the San Antonio, the Concepción, the Victoria and the little Santiago, under Ferdinand Magellan, a Portuguese captain sailing for the Spanish crown. Their orders were simple to state, impossible to carry out: reach the Spice Islands by sailing west, through whatever passage ran around the New World. Nobody had done it; nobody knew it could be done. The point of the voyage was to find out.

FIG. 01 The whole circle on one sheet: west across the Atlantic and Pacific to the Spice Islands, then the Victoria's lone track home around Africa. A 2011 rendering of a route first sailed 1519–1522.Map: Semhur · CC BY-SA 3.0 · via Wikimedia Commons

The reason for going the wrong way was money and treaty law. The 1494 Treaty of Tordesillas had split the unexplored world down an Atlantic line, giving Portugal the eastward road around Africa and Spain the westward half. The cloves, nutmeg and mace of the Moluccas outsold their weight in silver, but Portugal already held the long route to them round the Cape of Good Hope. To win a share, Spain had to sail west and hope the Spice Islands fell on its side — the gamble Magellan, passed over in Lisbon, offered the Spanish king.

The trouble began early. The fleet crossed the Atlantic, coasted down South America, and found only unbroken shore. Wintering at Port San Julián on the bleak Patagonian coast, the strain broke into mutiny: several captains tried to seize the fleet. Magellan crushed the rising — one ringleader killed, another marooned — and among the men he pardoned was a Basque master, Juan Sebastián Elcano; hold onto that name. The winter cost a ship too: the Santiago, scouting ahead, was wrecked in a storm, her crew saved. Four ships were left, the door still unfound.

They found it in October 1520, near the continent's southern tip — a cold, twisting maze of channels and dead ends that would bear Magellan's name. It took 38 days to feel through its roughly 350 miles, never sure whether the next reach opened onto ocean or rock. Somewhere inside, the San Antonio, carrying much of the fleet's provisions, quietly came about and deserted for Spain, to report Magellan a madman. Three ships came out at the end of November into water so calm after the strait's gales that Magellan named it the Mar Pacífico — a cruel joke waiting to be told.

The Pacific was the real discovery, and it very nearly killed them all. Magellan had entered the strait believing the Spice Islands lay a short hop beyond; instead the ocean unrolled month after month with no land, on a scale no one aboard had foreseen. For roughly three and a half months the food ran out, the biscuit rotted to weevilled powder, then to nothing; men gnawed the leather off the yards, hunted rats, and died of scurvy one after another. When they raised Guam in March 1521 they had proved what no map had shown: the world was not merely round, which the learned had never doubted, but immense — an expanse so vast that crossing it was closer to a death sentence than a passage.

The ocean was the discovery. Not that the world was round — the learned had known that for two thousand years — but that it was this staggeringly, murderously large. TRANSMISSION №026 · THE LONG READ

From Guam the survivors reached the islands later called the Philippines, and here Magellan's luck ran out. He had allied with a ruler on Cebu and agreed to help bring a rival chief on neighbouring Mactan to heel. On 27 April 1521, leading a small party ashore against a far larger force under a chief remembered as Lapulapu, Magellan was cut down in the shallows and killed. He never saw the Spice Islands he had sailed halfway round the world to reach, and never closed the circle — dead a third of the way home, in a fight that was not his to pick.

After Mactan the expedition was a wreck. With too few men to work three ships, they burned the Concepción and pressed on with two. In November 1521, more than two years out, the Trinidad and the Victoria reached the Moluccas and loaded the cargo the whole venture had been mounted to fetch — tons of cloves, worth a fortune in Seville. Then the two gambled differently. The Trinidad, leaking, tried to beat back east across the Pacific and failed, seized by the Portuguese. The Victoria, under the pardoned mutineer Elcano, ran on westward alone, across the Indian Ocean and round the Cape of Good Hope, through waters Portugal patrolled, to close the loop the long way.

It should not have worked. Elcano drove the Victoria across the Indian Ocean without stopping, to avoid Portuguese ports, at the cost of another slow harvest of dead. He rounded Africa, crept up the Atlantic, and on 6 September 1522 the Victoria came back into Sanlúcar de Barrameda — the river mouth she had left almost three years before. Of the roughly 270 who had sailed, 18 gaunt survivors stepped ashore, having kept going west until west became east and it brought them home. The cloves in the hold covered the entire expedition — by the brutal accounting of the age, the voyage had even turned a profit.

FIG. 02 Ferdinand Magellan, in an anonymous portrait of about 1600 — the captain who planned the circle and named the Pacific, but died at Mactan before it closed.Anonymous portrait, c. 1600 · public domain · via Wikimedia Commons

Then came the detail that has fascinated people ever since. The Victoria's survivors had kept a careful log the whole way round, and Antonio Pigafetta — the Venetian who had chronicled the voyage and was one of the 18 to come home — checked their tally against the calendar ashore in Spain. It was wrong: the ship's reckoning was a full day behind, the crew certain of one date, the townspeople of the next. Nobody had blundered. Sailing steadily westward around the planet, the ship had met each sunrise slightly later and, over one complete lap, banked one fewer than those who stayed put. A westward circuit loses a day; an eastward one gains it — the first hard demonstration of a fact nobody had been placed to notice, because none had ever gone all the way round.

That single missing day is the through-line of the whole story. The voyage is usually remembered for proving the Earth a sphere — old news, measured by Greek astronomers seventeen centuries earlier. What Magellan's fleet actually settled was harder: that you could sail on in one direction and come home from the other side, that the oceans were vaster and deadlier than anyone had imagined, and that the round world carried a hidden bookkeeping problem for anyone who circled it. That lost day was a puzzle without a home in 1522, and it took until an international conference in 1884 to answer it — an imaginary seam drawn down the Pacific, the International Date Line, where the calendar may jump. The Victoria's confused logbook was the first faint sighting of that line, three and a half centuries early, written by exhausted men who knew only that somewhere out on the water they had misplaced a Tuesday.

THE CASE AGAINST · THE OTHER READING

Whose voyage was it, really?

We call it Magellan's circumnavigation, but Magellan never circumnavigated anything — he was dead on a Philippine beach with the hardest two-thirds of the loop still to sail. The man who actually closed the circle was Juan Sebastián Elcano, and it is worth remembering he first appears in the story as one of the mutineers Magellan crushed at Port San Julián. Spain and Portugal have squabbled over the credit for five hundred years, and the honest answer is that neither captain did it alone: the achievement belongs as much to the 250-odd men who died on the way as to the two names on the maps. There is a deflating reading, too, of the "discovery" itself. The fleet proved the oceans were survivable to cross only by very nearly not surviving them; the route they pioneered was so lethal and so long that almost nobody sailed it again for its own sake. The first lap of the world was less a highway opened than a warning posted — magnificent, unrepeatable, and paid for at a price no sane venture would meet twice.

BAND 09 — BOOKMARK BOOKS · ON THE NIGHTSTAND

  • The Wager: A Tale of Shipwreck, Mutiny and Murder

    Narrative non-fiction · 2023 · shipwreck & mutiny

    In 1741 a Royal Navy vessel, the Wager, broke apart on a desolate island off the coast of Patagonia while chasing a Spanish treasure galleon. What David Grann reconstructs is not one survival story but several, mutually incriminating: the castaways who limped home years apart told wildly different versions of what happened on that shore, and the Admiralty had to decide whose account would send men to the gallows. It reads with the propulsion of a thriller — Grann's specialty — while staying rigorous about the archive it is built from: logbooks, court-martial testimony, the self-justifying memoirs each faction rushed into print to control the story first. A natural companion to a week spent thinking about the long sea route and the price it exacted, and a bracing step off the fantasy shelf into a true story stranger than most invented ones.

  • Sea of Tranquility

    Science fiction · time-slip · standalone

    Emily St. John Mandel's slender, time-hopping novel threads a single strange moment — a glitch in a forest, a note of violin music where there should be the roar of an airship terminal — across four centuries, from a 1912 exile in the woods of Vancouver Island to a moon colony centuries on. It is SF that wears its ideas lightly, more interested in how ordinary people carry loss than in the mechanics of its central conceit, and it closes the loop with the same quiet precision that made Station Eleven a modern favourite. Self-contained and readable in a single weekend. One for the diary while the shelf is turned to the genre: Becky Chambers returns on 13 October with the standalone As You Wake, Break the Shell, new from the writer who turned gentle, low-stakes SF into an art.

BAND 10 — THE DESK SERVICE

The Session

FITNESS · BLOCK 3, CONCURRENT LOAD

Block 3 is eleven weeks of hypertrophy work, run at maintenance-to-surplus, and now the 10k is back in the same week — so the old worry surfaces with it: will the running eat the muscle you're building? Land the answer plainly before the caveats: the interference effect is real, but it is manageable. The clearest reference point, a 2012 meta-analysis of concurrent training (Wilson et al., in the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research), found that adding endurance work does blunt strength and hypertrophy gains — but that the size of the hit scales with how much endurance you pile on, and that running interferes more than cycling because of its eccentric, muscle-damaging load. A couple of moderate runs a week is not marathon mileage; it is not going to undo a hypertrophy block.

The free wins are in how you arrange the two. Keep concurrent endurance to roughly two or three sessions a week while the block is on, so the volume stays in the range the evidence calls noise rather than drag. Separate the hard run and the heavy lift by at least about six hours where the day allows it — better still, put them on different days — so fatigue from one doesn't tax the quality of the other. And when they have to share a session, do the priority quality first: barbell before the road on a lifting-led week, so the loaded work that drives the adaptation gets your freshest legs. Where lower-body hypertrophy is the week's job, some of those runs can become bike work — similar aerobic stimulus, far less pounding on the muscle you're trying to grow.

The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% on 17 September — a fourth successive hold, this time on a 6–3 vote, with Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and Huw Pill pushing for a rise to 4%. Quantitative tightening rolls on underneath it: the committee voted unanimously to keep running down its gilt holdings at roughly £20bn of active sales a year on top of maturities. There is no fresh forecast until the 5 November round, so the Bank's own read of things holds until then. For anyone waiting on a rate cut to ease the household squeeze, the honest signal is that the relief this autumn is coming from somewhere else entirely.

That somewhere is the bills side. VAT on domestic electricity drops from 5% to 0% on 1 October, and the £2 single-bus-fare cap returns — a disinflationary tax cut landing just as the Bank sits still, so the pressure eases from what you pay rather than what you borrow. The catch is who gets nothing: a hold is no help to fixed-rate mortgage holders, whose payments don't move with it, and it quietly tests savers, because an easy-access account should still be tracking close to Bank Rate — and plenty have stopped. All of it sits in the shadow of the first Burnham Autumn Budget and the 'Rewiring the State' white paper on 28 October, which may reset the arithmetic again.

TUMBLE INTO The wave of molasses that outran Boston

There is the proverb — slow as molasses in January — and then there is what molasses actually did in January. On 15 January 1919, in the crowded North End of Boston, a steel storage tank holding 2.3 million gallons of the stuff tore itself apart. The syrup did not ooze. It came out as a wave up to twenty-five feet high, moving through the streets at around thirty-five miles an hour — faster than anyone caught in front of it could run. When it finally stopped, twenty-one people were dead and about a hundred and fifty were injured.

The molasses belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, which fermented it into ethanol — for rum in the years before Prohibition and, in the war that had just ended, for the industrial alcohol that went into munitions. The tank stood some fifty feet tall beside the waterfront, thrown up in a hurry in 1915. It had groaned and leaked almost from the day it was filled; rather than fix it, the company had it painted brown so the seepage would not show. On an unseasonably mild morning, with warm syrup expanding under its own weight, the weak seams gave way all at once.

FIG. 01 The wrecked collection tank and the tangled North End waterfront the morning after the flood, January 1919.Boston Public Library · public domain · Wikimedia Commons

The wave did what a wall of two-and-a-half thousand tonnes of liquid does. It lifted buildings off their foundations, swept a firehouse aside, and buckled the steel supports of the Boston Elevated Railway, the overhead line that carried trains through the district — a train missed the collapse by moments. Horses, carts and people were carried along in a rising, sticky tide that then cooled and thickened where it settled, so that rescuers had to dig the living out of the same setting mass that held the dead. Crews worked for days in molasses that reached the waist.

The clean-up ran for weeks. Salt water and sand were hauled through the streets to break the syrup down, and the inner harbour reportedly ran brown until the summer. The stranger legacy was olfactory: for decades afterwards, people who lived in the North End insisted the neighbourhood still smelled faintly of molasses when the weather turned hot.

FIG. 02 A 2015 locator map of the North End flood zone, between Boston's waterfront and the line of the elevated railway.Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

What outlasted even the smell was the lawsuit. United States Industrial Alcohol blamed anarchist saboteurs; the flood of residents and victims' families who sued did not believe a word of it. Their case — one of the first great class-style actions Massachusetts had seen, folding scores of claims into a single hearing — dragged on for years, and it turned entirely on engineering. The tank had been filled to the brim without the structural calculations to justify the load, its plans waved through by a company treasurer with no technical training who could not read a stress drawing. The auditor found for the plaintiffs, and the company paid out.

The wider consequence reached far beyond one Boston block. The disaster is widely credited with hardening American engineering-accountability standards — pushing states toward rules that structural plans be prepared, stamped and certified by a licensed professional engineer, so that no treasurer could ever again sign for a tank he was incapable of calculating. It is a grim thing to owe a rule to, but much of the quiet machinery that now makes someone qualified answer for what they build was set in motion by a wave of syrup that, for one January morning, was anything but slow.
