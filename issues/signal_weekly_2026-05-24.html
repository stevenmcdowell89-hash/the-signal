Two endings arrived on the same weekend, and it felt like the country held two different kinds of breath. On the political side: a Prime Minister besieged by his own MPs, a cabinet secretary gone, councils up and down the country having turned against the government in ways that don't easily reverse. On the pitch: 22 years of waiting — and then, finally, Arsenal's. One story is about something falling apart; the other is about something arriving so late that the relief is almost indistinguishable from disbelief.

This issue sits with both. The World This Week goes deep on the Starmer endgame — the arithmetic of a leadership challenge, the candidates beginning to circle, and what a Labour implosion at this stage of a parliament actually means. The Touchline takes the longer view on Arsenal's title and turns toward Budapest: six days and a Champions League Final against PSG.

Elsewhere, the Mandalorian and Grogu verdict is in (60% on Rotten Tomatoes, which is its own kind of story), and the Listen section is entirely occupied by Boards of Canada breaking thirteen years of silence. Stray lands on Switch 2 in four days. The Champions League trophy decides itself in six.

It's a full one. Settle in.

The Starmer Endgame — What happens when ninety-five MPs say go

Photo: Lauren Hurley / No. 10 Downing Street, Crown Copyright. Licensed under the Open Government Licence

There is a number at which a leadership crisis stops being a crisis and becomes a mathematical certainty. For Keir Starmer, that number appears to be 95 — the count of Labour MPs who had, as of Sunday 24 May, publicly called for his resignation or demanded a timeline for his departure. Whether that count is enough, and whether the 147 MPs who have yet to take a public position are leaning toward him or away, is the only political question in British public life right now.

The proximate cause was a set of local election results so bad they defied calibration. Labour lost control of more than thirty councils. Roughly 1,500 councillors were defeated — chased out of seats not by one insurgency but two, with Reform UK swallowing the right flank and the Greens absorbing the left. These were not protest results at the margins. They were a structural verdict on a government that had, in eighteen months, managed to alienate two different electorates simultaneously. The party that won a landslide in 2024 is now losing its own backyard.

The resignation of Wes Streeting on 14 May crystallised what the numbers had been suggesting for weeks. The Health Secretary — one of the government's more prominent communicators, and among those most publicly identified with the 2024 majority — announced he had "lost confidence" in the Prime Minister's leadership. Four junior ministers and a number of ministerial aides followed in the days afterward. A cabinet departure of this kind is qualitatively different from a backbench rebellion; it signals that the damage has climbed above the parliamentary waterline.

Eurasia Group, which tracks political risk across markets, raised its probability estimate of Starmer being ousted in 2026 to 80%, identifying an MP-forced leadership election by September as the most credible scenario. That timeline is not speculation pulled from thin air: it maps to the parliamentary recess window and the timetable constraints of a Labour leadership contest under the party's current rules. The mechanism is visible even if the trigger hasn't fired yet.

"The party that won a landslide in 2024 is now losing its own backyard — chased out by Reform on the right and the Greens on the left simultaneously."

— The Signal's reading of the May 2026 council results

Names are already circulating. Andy Burnham, still regarded in many quarters as the party's most naturally political operator. David Lammy, the Foreign Secretary, whose international profile has grown during the first eighteen months. Shabana Mahmood, Justice Secretary, seen by some as a credible reformist. Ed Miliband, whose climate legacy is more defensible than his previous leadership record suggested. Al Carns, less well-known but flagged by several Eurasian and political analysts as a candidate with moderniser appeal. None of these names has yet declared. None needs to — the first move is Starmer's, not theirs.

As of this Sunday, Starmer has not resigned. He has stated publicly that he would stand in any leadership contest, framing his position as a commitment to the project rather than a concession to the pressure. That is, on the available evidence, a difficult position to sustain as the count rises. The 147 MPs who have not yet taken a public stance are watching each other, and that is what makes the next fortnight so fragile: a stampede is self-reinforcing once it begins, and the 95 figure is already a majority of a majority in the wrong direction.

The structural reading of what this means is harder than the procedural one. A Starmer departure — whether voluntary or forced — would be the second Labour leadership change in five years, after Jeremy Corbyn. It would hand the Conservatives and Reform a propaganda gift at the worst possible moment, confirming a narrative about Labour's ungovernability. Against that, a leader who has lost the cabinet, the councils, and the confidence of a third of his parliamentary party is not, in any practical sense, governing. The question of whether it is better to lance the crisis now or to manage it into the ground is the one being answered behind closed doors across Westminster this weekend.

The Angle

The Eurasia Group number — 80% — is the signal. Political risk analysts are not in the business of dramatic flourish; that figure reflects a structural assessment, not a temperature reading. The scenario they judge most likely (MP-forced leadership contest by September) is credible because it requires nothing unexpected to happen: just the arithmetic continuing to move in the direction it is already moving. Starmer can survive if the 147 uncommitted MPs decide the risks of removal outweigh the risks of continuation. That is possible. It is not, on current evidence, probable.

The Hormuz Bargain — Trump says "largely negotiated"; Tehran isn't sure that's true

On 23 May, President Trump told reporters that a peace agreement with Iran reopening the Strait of Hormuz was "largely negotiated" and would be announced shortly, describing a "very good call" involving leaders from Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, Pakistan, Jordan, Egypt and the UAE. Within hours, Iran's Fars news agency issued a response: the deal was "incomplete and inconsistent with reality". Both statements can be true simultaneously, which is perhaps the most useful way to understand where this stands.

The ceasefire that came into force on 8 April has been fragile since the beginning. Skirmishes have continued through April and into May. Almost no commercial shipping has used the Strait since the conflict began — UNCTAD is projecting global growth at 2.6% for 2026, with the energy disruption a significant contributing factor. The economic logic of a deal is overwhelming; the political logic, from Tehran's perspective, is considerably more complicated.

Sticking Points
  • Enriched uranium — US insists Iran relinquish stockpiles
  • Natanz — demands for dismantlement of the enrichment facility
  • Fordow — buried facility; Iran has refused to consider closure
  • Isfahan — conversion plant; disputed status in talks
  • Israel — continues to push for a harder military posture, complicating US leverage

Trump's demand package remains what it has been throughout: Iran surrenders its enriched uranium and agrees to dismantle the Natanz, Fordow and Isfahan nuclear facilities. For Iran, these are not bargaining chips — they represent years of investment and the core of what its negotiating position has always been protecting. The gap between "largely negotiated" and "done" may be measured in weeks or it may be measured in the distance between two parties who define the deal differently.

The complicating variable is Israel, which has continued to push for a harder military posture throughout the ceasefire period and has limited interest in a diplomatic resolution that leaves Iran's nuclear infrastructure partially intact. Trump needs a deal — the economic cost of a closed Strait is borne disproportionately by US trading partners in the Gulf, who are also the counterparties to his other diplomatic priorities. Whether "largely negotiated" is an accurate description of the state of play, or a negotiating posture delivered via press conference, is a question the next week or two will likely begin to answer.

The Cat Game Comes to Switch 2 — and Brings Mouse Controls With It

Four years after its PC and PlayStation debut, Annapurna Interactive's Stray arrives on Nintendo Switch 2 this Thursday, 28 May, priced at $29.99. The package includes 4K resolution output, an improved frame rate, and — the most interesting addition — dedicated mouse controls. If the mouse integration lands cleanly, Stray on Switch 2 becomes something its original console incarnations weren't: a game where pointing at things with your cursor is part of the vocabulary.

The original Stray was released in July 2022 and won awards for a quality that's genuinely rare in games: tone. You play as an orange tabby navigating a walled cyberpunk city populated entirely by robots, solving environmental puzzles, knocking things off shelves, and eventually piecing together the city's history. Developer BlueTwelve Studio built something that didn't need to be longer than it was — roughly five to six hours, depending on how much you explored — and that restraint was the point. The game understood that a cat's attention span is not a bug, it's the lens.

The Switch 2 port adds to an increasingly compelling secondary pipeline for Nintendo's new hardware. This month alone has seen Indiana Jones and the Great Circle (14 May), Yoshi and the Mysterious Book (21 May), and now Stray — all multi-platform titles arriving weeks or months after their lead-platform debuts, each with something new to justify the double-dip. Mouse controls on a puzzle-exploration game is a more credible addition than simply bumping resolution, because it changes the interaction: pointing and clicking has a different rhythm from analogue thumbstick navigation, and in a game built around curiosity and environmental reading, that shift in input could meaningfully change pacing.

Whether mouse controls on a Switch 2 actually feel good will depend heavily on how BlueTwelve Studio has mapped the input — the Joy-Con mouse mode is a genuinely novel control scheme, and Stray is the first major third-party port to commit to it rather than offering it as a cosmetic option. That makes 28 May a minor test case for the platform: if the controls work well, it sets a precedent for how puzzle and strategy developers approach Switch 2 ports going forward.

The broader question is whether Stray's particular spell still holds. The game's emotional weight came from mystery and miniature scale — the city was large, the cat was small, and the gap between those two things was where the atmosphere lived. That quality is not format-dependent. It should survive 4K and a docked screen just fine. At $29.99 for a first-time Switch 2 owner, it remains one of the stronger entry-point arguments for a platform that's still building its software identity.

Did You Know?

Stray was first announced at a PlayStation showcase in June 2020 under its working codename HK_Project — a reference to Hong Kong, one of the inspirations for the city's layered, neon-lit aesthetic. The final game released two years later, in July 2022, to a score of 82 on Metacritic and a Steam rating of "Overwhelmingly Positive". Annapurna Interactive, its publisher, also released Outer Wilds and What Remains of Edith Finch — a catalogue that values environmental storytelling over combat loops.

Gemini Goes Everywhere — Including Someone Else's Phone

The week after Google I/O is traditionally when the announcements start arriving in actual products, and this week that process is underway. Gemini 4 integration is rolling into Google Search, expanding AI Overviews to more query types. A drafting assistant powered by Gemini is appearing in Google Docs. YouTube is gaining a Gemini-powered summarisation feature for longer videos — ask what a video covers and get a structured reply rather than a timestamp hunt.

The detail that stuck from the I/O keynote itself, though, was the device Google chose for a live demo: an iPhone. Google demonstrated Gemini Spark on Apple hardware, centre stage, without apology. The strategic logic is hard to miss — Google is positioning Gemini as platform-agnostic AI in a way that Apple Intelligence, which runs locally and is tied to Apple silicon, structurally cannot be. If the message to consumers is "the best AI assistant doesn't care what phone you use", then demoing on an iPhone is not a quirk, it's the argument.

Apple WWDC 2026 is scheduled for the coming weeks, and the expectation is that Apple will respond with the next generation of Apple Intelligence features. The gap between Google's approach (cloud-first, cross-platform, heavy on integrations) and Apple's (on-device, privacy-forward, hardware-locked) is sharpening into something the average consumer will increasingly have to choose between. That's a more interesting question than benchmark comparisons.

Every AI item here passes the consumer test: these are features arriving in apps and services most people already use, not developer tooling. Gemini in Search is something anyone with a Google account will encounter this week, whether or not they went looking for it.

The 22-year wait — and the seven-day wait that follows it

It was confirmed on a Tuesday evening in Bournemouth, of all places. Manchester City drew 1-1 with the south-coast side on 19 May, and in that moment Arsenal became Premier League champions for the first time since Arsène Wenger's Invincibles walked away with an unbeaten title in 2003-04. Twenty-two years. The longest gap a club has ever endured between consecutive English league titles. Mikel Arteta, who played in that 2003-04 side, had spent half a decade building toward this. The moment duly arrived — and it arrived in a season that makes a compelling argument for being the most defensively dominant Premier League campaign since the league's modern era.

The numbers are worth pausing on. Arsenal conceded just 26 goals across 37 games — a league-low, representing fewer than one goal per match — with 19 clean sheets. In the Champions League, they added 9 further clean sheets, also the best in the competition this season. These are not the numbers of a team that merely accumulated points; they are the numbers of a side that suffocated opponents systematically and then found the clinical edge to punish the gaps. The Invincibles comparison is inevitable and probably premature, but the structural resemblance — defence as the architecture of everything — is real.

On final day at Crystal Palace, the title was already secure and the afternoon became a celebration in plain clothes. Elsewhere, the lower-table drama that everyone had expected delivered: Tottenham beat Wolves 1-0 to survive on goal difference, sending West Ham down alongside Burnley and Wolves — all three confirmed relegated, with West Ham beating Everton 2-1 but finding that insufficient as Spurs held on. The season's storyline was not at either end; it was settled a week earlier in Bournemouth, and what awaits in Budapest is the second act.

Budapest, Saturday 30 May — 18:00 CET

The 2026 Champions League Final is the first held in Hungary, at the Puskás Aréna in Budapest — a 67,000-capacity stadium named for Hungary's greatest footballer. Arsenal reached the final by defeating Atlético Madrid 2-1 on aggregate in the semi-finals. Their opponents, Paris Saint-Germain, beat Bayern Munich in the other semi. PSG are aiming for back-to-back UCL titles under Luis Enrique — a feat only achieved by Real Madrid in the modern era with any regularity. Arsenal have never won the Champions League; they were runners-up to Barcelona in 2006 on a night defined by Jens Lehmann's early red card and a rearguard that came agonisingly close to holding the line.

The betting markets have PSG as favourites. Bet365's lines place PSG at -152 (60.3% implied probability) and Arsenal at +120. The logic is not hard to follow: PSG's knockout pedigree, their Champions League pedigree specifically, and the weight of what Arsenal are attempting — a domestic and European double in the same week, with a title parade in Islington already scheduled for 31 May. But the counter-argument is the one Arsenal's season makes by itself: the stingiest defence in the Premier League, the most clean sheets in Europe's premier cup competition, and a manager who was moulded in the Wenger tradition and has spent seven years refining his own version of it.

A fortnight after Inter sealed Serie A under San Siro fireworks — both finalists overcame Italian opposition in the knockout rounds, with the side PSG knocked out in the semis aside — the final has the shape of two philosophies colliding: PSG's individual brilliance and attacking dynamism against Arsenal's system-first, defensive-excellence model. On paper, PSG should win. On recent evidence, it is harder to be certain than the odds suggest.

The Take

The 26 goals conceded is the number that keeps surfacing. Not the wins, not the title — the goals against. It says something specific about what Arteta has built: not a team that wins by scoring more than everyone else (though they score enough), but a team that wins by making losing unacceptably difficult. That is a harder system to maintain, and a more coherent one. PSG's route to Budapest was built on attacking brilliance; Arsenal's was built on not giving opponents angles. On a neutral ground, in a final, the system that doesn't leak is usually the one to back. The markets disagree. That is the argument in Budapest.

Aaron Rai, two gloves, and the first English PGA Champion since 1919

Aaron Rai — Aronimink 2026

Photo: Jamie Squire/Getty Images via PGA Championship press

The thing about Aaron Rai is that he was easy to discount. He arrived at Aronimink Golf Club with a reputation as a methodical grinder — not the kind of player whose name appears at the top of a leaderboard and stays there with the authority of a Scheffler or a McIlroy. He wears two gloves. He keeps his iron covers on between shots at a tempo that other players find baffling. He has the quiet competence of someone who has thought carefully about every variable and dealt with it in advance. These are not the qualities that generate pre-tournament profiles. They are precisely the qualities that win major championships.

On Sunday at Aronimink, Rai shot a final-round 65, finishing at 9-under (271 total) and three shots clear of Jon Rahm and Alex Smalley. He became the first English-born player to win the PGA Championship since Jim Barnes in 1919 — 107 years — and the first player of Indian descent to win a men's major in golf. The Wanamaker Trophy, which goes to the winner of the PGA Championship, now belongs to a player who made the field treat him as an afterthought and then found a way to make it count.

The margin tells part of the story. Three shots, against a field that included Rory McIlroy and Scottie Scheffler, is not a scramble to the line — it is a controlled Sunday that accelerated in the right direction at the right time. Rai's prize was $3.69 million and 750 FedExCup points; the automatic US Open entry that comes with a major win added immediate weight to the victory beyond the trophy. Golf Monthly's round-by-round coverage had tracked his ascent through the week — he was never hidden, exactly, but he was not the story the broadcasters had planned for. He rarely is. He tends to be the story that arrives anyway.

The Boroughs — when the Duffer Brothers stop chasing nostalgia

There's a telling distinction in the credits of The Boroughs. Matt and Ross Duffer are listed as executive producers. Not creators. Not directors. The show belongs to Jeffrey Addiss and Will Matthews — and that gap between producing and making is, it turns out, where the most interesting television is currently being made.

The premise is surface-level familiar: a retirement community in a seemingly picturesque neighbourhood discovers that something otherworldly is closing in. That sentence could easily describe the Duffer Brothers' own flagship. But The Boroughs, which dropped all eight episodes on Netflix on 21 May, earns its 95% on Rotten Tomatoes by doing something Stranger Things has occasionally forgotten to do — treating its setup as a story rather than a delivery mechanism for references and callbacks.

Alfred Molina carries the series as Sam Cooper, a recently widowed aeronautical engineer who finds himself, improbably, at the centre of events he is completely unequipped to understand. Molina is seventy-one, plays the character's grief with a bone-dry composure, and is precisely the kind of lead that prestige television claims to want and rarely actually casts. Around him, Addiss and Matthews have assembled one of the more remarkable television ensembles in recent memory: Alfre Woodard, Denis O'Hare, Clarke Peters, Geena Davis, Carlos Miranda. These are not cameo slots. Each performer has a character with a legible interior life, stakes of their own, and something to do when the crisis arrives.

What Addiss and Matthews understand — and what the Variety review picks up on — is tonal control. The comedy is dry rather than camp. The threat is treated with genuine dread rather than genre-signalling. The show never winks at the audience to confirm it knows what kind of show it is. That confidence allows the cast to inhabit the material without protective irony, and the result is that the oddness of the premise lands with actual weight rather than nostalgic comfort.

The Duffer pedigree is present mostly as infrastructure — the production values are high, the pacing is tight, the eight-episode run is clean enough that nothing outstays its welcome. But this is not Hawkins with a different postcode. The Boroughs has its own register, its own pace, and its own sense of what an older ensemble of characters brings to a genre usually dominated by teenagers. Strongly recommended.

The Mando movie at 60% — a verdict the franchise has to live with

Last week this section flagged the arrival of The Mandalorian and Grogu as the first Star Wars theatrical film in seven years, directed by Jon Favreau and starring Pedro Pascal alongside Sigourney Weaver, Jeremy Allen White, and Martin Scorsese. The opening-weekend tracking aimed at $160M+ globally. The critics have now spoken.

The Rotten Tomatoes score sits at 60%. That is not merely mixed — it is one of the lowest theatrical scores in franchise history, sitting below Attack of the Clones at 62%. For a film returning Star Wars to cinema after seven years, with the goodwill generated by the show's best seasons behind it, 60% is a result that requires honest examination rather than fanbase rationalisation.

The critical consensus is internally consistent. Reviewers praised Jon Favreau's handling of the action sequences, the visual scale, and — almost uniformly — the Din Djarin-Grogu dynamic, which remains the franchise's most emotionally legible relationship. Pedro Pascal is cited as doing everything asked of him. Where the film falls down, according to critics, is in narrative ambition. Deadline's box office preview notes reviewer frustration with thin plotting and a reliance on fan-service callbacks that read as structural rather than earned — the movie knows what the audience will respond to and delivers those beats without building the dramatic architecture that would make them land properly.

This is a recognisable failure mode in franchise cinema. The mechanics of emotional payoff exist independently of the story that would justify them. A 60% score suggests the film does enough with its central relationship and spectacle to satisfy many viewers while failing to function as a complete film for those measuring it against the medium's broader possibilities.

What the score actually signals about the franchise's theatrical instincts is the more interesting question. The Mandalorian worked in part because the television format gave the Din-Grogu relationship space to breathe across seasons. Compressing that into a single theatrical film — while simultaneously trying to justify cinema scale with action-sequence ambition — may be the structural problem no amount of goodwill can fully solve. A 60% opening is not the end of Star Wars in cinemas. But it is a data point the next film will need to actually answer.

A Second Front for Leucine

The standard leucine story is mTOR and muscle. Cologne just published a different one.

For decades, the amino acid leucine has had one dominant story in cell biology: it activates mTOR signalling, which drives muscle protein synthesis. That story is well-established and largely correct. But a paper published in Nature Cell Biology in May 2026 by researchers at the University of Cologne's CECAD Cluster of Excellence on Ageing Research describes something else entirely — a second mechanism, operating in a completely different part of the cell, that connects leucine to the very machinery that keeps cells energised.

The finding comes from a team led by Professor Thorsten Hoppe. Their work centres on a regulatory protein called SEL1L. Under normal conditions, SEL1L helps direct a cellular quality-control process: it marks certain proteins for degradation, clearing them from the outer mitochondrial membrane. When leucine is present at meaningful concentrations, the researchers found, it suppresses SEL1L activity. The consequence of that suppression is specific and significant: a class of transport proteins on the outer mitochondrial membrane — proteins responsible for shuttling metabolic molecules into the mitochondria — is protected from degradation. With those transport proteins intact, the supply of metabolic substrate flowing into the mitochondria is maintained, and ATP synthesis runs more efficiently.

Mitochondria are routinely described as the cell's power plants, which is accurate but flattening. They are also sites of calcium signalling, apoptosis regulation, and reactive oxygen species management. Almost every tissue in the body depends on them. The Cologne finding is notable precisely because it draws a dietary line — a line from the leucine in a piece of chicken or a bowl of lentils — directly to the outer membrane of that machinery, via a protein most people have never heard of.

"From food to fuel: this study helps explain how a nutrient from everyday foods like meat, dairy, eggs and legumes can directly influence our cells' energy factories."
— CECAD Cluster of Excellence Outreach, University of Cologne

The research team's framing is careful and precise. They are not claiming this mechanism explains the bulk of leucine's effects on human physiology. They are not recommending any particular intake. What they have documented is a molecular pathway — SEL1L suppression leading to preserved outer mitochondrial membrane proteins leading to enhanced ATP synthesis — that was not previously known to exist. The Nature Cell Biology paper itself, along with the CECAD outreach coverage and a summary published by ScienceDaily on 20 May, consistently positions this as a mechanistic finding with downstream implications for cancer, metabolic disease, and ageing research. Those implications are not speculative decoration. Mitochondrial dysfunction is a consistent feature across all three disease contexts; a newly identified route by which a common dietary amino acid modulates that function is, for researchers in those fields, genuinely significant.

It is worth being clear about what this pathway is not. It operates independently of the mTOR pathway. It is not a refinement of muscle protein synthesis science. The two mechanisms almost certainly run in parallel — leucine acting on mTOR in the cytoplasm and on SEL1L at the outer mitochondrial membrane simultaneously — but they are not two versions of the same process. The Cologne team's contribution is the identification of a distinct molecular route, not a revision of an existing one.

Leucine is found at useful concentrations in meat, dairy, eggs, and legumes. It is not a rare or exotic amino acid, and the finding does not suggest any shortage in ordinary protein-sufficient diets. The interest here is scientific rather than prescriptive: a new mechanism has been identified, and its implications for ageing biology, metabolic research, and cancer cell energetics will now be tested by other groups. The canonical sources — the Nature Cell Biology paper, the ScienceDaily summary, and the CECAD outreach piece — are all linked for anyone who wants the primary source.

What this means in practice

Leucine is already present in adequate concentrations in any protein-sufficient diet — meat, dairy, eggs, and legumes are all reliable sources. This finding does not change supplementation thresholds or establish a new dietary recommendation. The mechanism — SEL1L suppression preserving outer mitochondrial membrane transport proteins — is a basic science finding, not a clinical intervention. Its significance is for researchers working in cancer biology, metabolic disease, and ageing: a new pathway has been mapped. The practical read is simply that the leucine in a normal varied diet is doing more at the cellular level than the mTOR story alone suggested.

A Fact

The phrase "give someone the cold shoulder" did not originate with medieval hosts serving cold mutton to unwanted guests — that story is a folk etymology with no evidence. The actual coinage is Sir Walter Scott in his 1816 novel The Antiquary: "showing o' the cauld shouther." It was likely a mistranslation of dederunt umerum recedentem — Nehemiah 9:29 in the Vulgate — meaning "stubbornly they turned their backs on you." The cold shoulder is a turned back, not a dinner course. Wikipedia · phrases.org.uk
