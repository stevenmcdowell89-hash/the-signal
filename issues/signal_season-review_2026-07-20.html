I
FILE 01

The Tournament at a Glance

Thirty-nine days, 104 matches, and one team that never lost

It ended the way the whole month had quietly promised it would: Spain, unbeaten, passing a tiring opponent into submission, and a substitute arriving to finish what the starters had spent a hundred minutes building. Ferran Torres's left-footed goal in the 106th minute of the final — 39 seconds into the second period of extra time, from Nico Williams's headed pass — beat Argentina 1–0 at MetLife Stadium on Saturday evening and closed the 104th and last match of the biggest World Cup ever staged. Spain are champions of the world for the second time, sixteen years after the first, and the first nation ever to hold the men's and women's titles at once.

Hold the final in your head as a miniature of the tournament. It had a record — Emiliano Martínez's ten saves, the most ever recorded in a World Cup final, in front of a defence that attempted no shots at all in regulation. It had an ageing legend refusing to leave the stage quietly: Lionel Messi, 39, dragged a limited Argentina to within one save of penalties, then sat on the grass while the confetti fell for someone else. It had a teenager at the centre of everything, Lamine Yamal's free kick drawing the best of Martínez's ten. It had a red card, Enzo Fernández's second yellow in second-half stoppage time. And it had America wrapped loudly around all of it — a 27-minute half-time show, Post Malone before kick-off, and the US president handing the trophy to Rodri.

He was wrong, narrowly, and the way he was wrong is the story this file spends the next nine chapters unpacking. Betting against Messi turned out to be exactly what this Spain were built for: suffocate the space, survive the moment, and trust that eight games of control eventually produce one unguarded second. It did — once — and once was enough.

The shape of the thing, before the detail. Forty-eight teams arrived, arranged into twelve groups across three countries; a new round of 32 meant the winners played eight matches instead of seven. The football answered most of the format's critics and vindicated a few of them: 308 goals is a record with an asterisk the size of forty extra fixtures, and the group stage produced both the tournament's best story — Cape Verde, population half a million, unbeaten through it — and its emptiest afternoons. The knockouts, once they arrived, were the strongest in living memory: Brazil went out in the last 16 to a Haaland-possessed Norway, Argentina twice escaped from the dead, and the bronze final finished 6–4.

Everything in this review is filed under a simple rule: results first, verdicts second, and every number carries its source. The tournament at a glance is below; how Spain actually did it is next.

II
FILE 02

How Spain Won It

Eight games, six clean sheets, one late red card, and a substitute's left foot

Every champion has an official story and a true one. Spain's official story is serene: eight games, eight results, no defeats, a record six clean sheets, and a final won by a substitute's first goal of the tournament. The true story is stranger, because it begins with the closest this team came to embarrassment — a goalless draw, in their opening game, against the smallest nation ever to qualify.

That 0–0 with Cape Verde on the first weekend was read at the time as a warning. In hindsight it was a syllabus. Spain's response was not to panic but to narrow: four unanswered goals past Saudi Arabia — Lamine Yamal's first World Cup goal among them, and a Mikel Oyarzabal brace — then a professional 1–0 over a Uruguay side that would leave the tournament winless and take Marcelo Bielsa's reputation with them. Group H was won without conceding, and without ever quite entertaining. The pattern held through the whole knockout run: Austria dismissed 3–0 in the round of 32, Portugal beaten 1–0 in the last 16, Belgium 2–1 in the quarter-final — the only goal Spain conceded all month, Charles De Ketelaere's, answered by Fabián Ruiz and the substitute Mikel Merino — and then France, the most feared attack in the field, held to nothing in Dallas.

The semi-final was the masterpiece, and it was a masterpiece of suppression rather than expression. Oyarzabal's 22nd-minute penalty, won when Lucas Digne kicked Yamal, and Pedro Porro's strike just before the hour gave the scoreline its shape; the substance was underneath. ESPN's stats desk framed the game as "Yamal 6 – 0 Mbappé" on chances created, and measured France's attacking output as their worst in a knockout tie in sixty years of data. Kylian Mbappé arrived as the tournament's top scorer and left without a shot worth the name. No team in this World Cup did that to anyone else.

Then the final, which tested the one thing the previous seven games had not: patience against a team with no intention of playing. Argentina's plan was explicit and, for 105 minutes, magnificent. They attempted zero shots in regulation. Not few — zero, against fifteen for Spain, with the corner count nine to one. Emiliano Martínez turned the game into a personal exhibition, ten saves in all, the most ever recorded in a World Cup final, the best of them a full-stretch dive to a Yamal free kick from 22 yards. When Enzo Fernández collected a second yellow card in second-half stoppage time — a reckless challenge that sent Pau Cubarsí somersaulting — the question stopped being whether Spain would create enough chances and became whether ten Argentinians and one inspired goalkeeper could reach the lottery of penalties.

They fell one minute short of it, and the manner of the goal was the tournament's thesis in miniature. Luis de la Fuente had removed Oyarzabal — his semi-final scorer — for Torres just after the hour, a change that looked like rotation and functioned as a plan. Fresh legs against a spent ten; a header flicked on by Nico Williams; and Torres, arriving on the left of the six-yard area, finishing first time past a goalkeeper who had stopped everything hittable for two hours. Thirty-nine seconds into the second period of extra time. One goal, in the one hundred and sixth minute, from a player who had not scored all month. That is not luck. That is what a fifteen-man squad is for.

It is worth being precise about what kind of great team this is, because it is not the 2010 side reborn, whatever the anniversary romance says. That Spain sanctified possession; this one weaponises denial. Rodri's Golden Ball was won facing his own goal as often as the opponent's, closing the counter-attack before it existed. Unai Simón collected the Golden Glove on the strength of six shut-outs while frequently touching the ball fewer than thirty times a game. Cubarsí, nineteen, was named the tournament's best young player for a month of defending that made experienced forwards look pre-read. The attack, meanwhile, was a rota: Yamal created, but the goals came from everywhere — Oyarzabal, Ruiz, Merino, Porro, finally Torres. No Spaniard finished among the Boot contenders, and no Spaniard needed to.

The Champion's Shape

Not the beautiful Spain of 2010 and not trying to be — a team that treated every match as a door to be closed, and closed eight of them.

The last word on how it was won belongs to the bench. De la Fuente's substitutions scored or created the decisive goal in the quarter-final, the semi-final and the final. In a 48-team, eight-game tournament played through an American July, the deepest squad was always likely to outlast the best eleven. Spain had both, used the second only when the first had done its work, and became the oldest manager's youngest-feeling champion — a side whose two defining players are nineteen and a goalkeeper's nightmare. The 2030 favourites, by the way, are not in doubt. They will be defending the thing at home.

Three more days belong on the list, even without their own plates. The twentieth of June, when Türkiye — many people's dark horse, with Kenan Yildiz and Arda Güler in full flow between the boxes and nobody to finish for them — lost 1–0 to ten-man Paraguay and were out after two games. Their 62 shots without a goal across those two matches is the most in any two-game World Cup span in data reaching back to 1966; futility of that precision is almost an achievement. Then the twenty-ninth and thirtieth, the great penalty cull of the round of 32: Germany beaten from the spot by Paraguay, and the Netherlands eliminated when Bart Verbruggen caught Soufiane Rahimi's penalty with both hands and carried it over his own line — the save that scored. Morocco walked through the gap and did not stop until the quarter-finals.

And the fifth of July twice over: while Haaland was dismantling Brazil, England were surviving the game of the tournament — 3–2 against Mexico, in the Azteca, against a co-host, a sent-off player and 80-odd thousand certainties. CNN called it the best match of the World Cup within an hour of the whistle. It was also the day Thomas Tuchel's England stopped being a question and became a bracket problem for everyone else.

The last hinge came in Dallas on the fourteenth of July, and it is the one this review keeps returning to because it decided the character of the champion. Spain 2–0 France was not a contest; it was a demonstration that the tournament's best attacking player could be subtracted from a match by structure alone. When the semi-final's story is a penalty won by a seventeen-year-old and a statistical line — France's worst knockout attacking return in sixty years — the final's story is already half-written. Argentina, a night later in Atlanta, beat England 2–1 by the older method: fall behind, wait for the game to fray, and let Enzo Fernández and Lautaro Martínez settle it late. Two schools of tournament football booked MetLife, and the colder one won.

FILE 04

The Nearly Men

Argentina's silver, England's bronze, France's strange fortnight

Silver is the strangest medal in sport: it is awarded for losing. Argentina will wear this one differently, because everything about their month argued against them being at MetLife at all. The champions of 2022 arrived older everywhere it matters, survived Cape Verde by an own goal in the 111th minute, needed three goals in eleven minutes against Egypt, and beat England with their only two shots of consequence. What carried them was not the old brilliance — it was a squad-wide refusal to be eliminated that borders on the theological, plus one 39-year-old who kept producing the exact moment each round required. Eight goals for Messi, second in the Boot race; the Golden Ball's silver; a final in which his team declined to attempt a single shot in regulation and still nearly reached penalties. It was a heist plan, executed to within one minute of the vault.

England's tournament ended twice in seventy-two hours, and the second ending will outlive the first. The semi-final defeat in Atlanta was the modern England loss in its classical form: ahead through Anthony Gordon early in the second half, structurally in control, then undone by one swing of Enzo Fernández's right boot from distance in the 85th and a substitute's winner — Lautaro Martínez, on for exactly this. Thomas Tuchel's selection gamble, leaving Bukayo Saka out for Morgan Rogers's physicality, will be argued about for years; what is not arguable is what Saka did with the grievance.

The bronze final in Miami Gardens was the maddest match this competition has staged in decades — ten goals, the most in any World Cup game since 1982 and the most ever in a third-place fixture. Declan Rice's third-minute screamer started it; Saka's hat-trick built a 4–0 half-time lead; France's four-man half-time substitution, Ousmane Dembélé among them, nearly erased it; England won 6–4 anyway. A match invented for people who claim third-place games mean nothing, won by a team that plainly disagreed.

Which leaves France, the tournament's great riddle. They scored freely through five rounds behind Mbappé's ten-goal Boot campaign, beat Morocco 2–0 in a controlled quarter-final even after Yassine Bounou saved a Mbappé penalty — and then produced, by ESPN's sixty-year dataset, the worst attacking knockout performance in their modern history when it mattered most. Four days later their reserves conceded six to England before nearly rescuing a 4–0 deficit. A squad that contains both of those fortnights ends the summer with the Golden Boot, a fourth-place certificate, and a genuine question about what it is for. Didier Deschamps's era of results-first pragmatism has now returned one trophy from its last three tournaments; the pragmatism remains, the results have stopped.

The half-step below the podium deserves its roll call, because this was the strongest last eight in memory. Norway took England to 2–1 in the quarter-final through Andreas Schjelderup's reply to Jude Bellingham's brace — a first quarter-final in their history, built entirely on the Brazil night. Morocco confirmed 2022 was a structure, not a miracle: through the Netherlands on penalties, 3–0 over Canada in the last 16, and out only to France, again, with Bounou saving a penalty on the way. Belgium put four past the host nation and gave Spain their only genuinely nervous knockout evening. And Switzerland quietly reached the last eight before Argentina beat them 3–1 — the kind of run nobody remembers and every federation would take.

The Awards, Tied to Moments

Six prizes, each pinned to a dated moment from the file

An award without a moment attached is just a list. The rule here, as in every review this magazine files: each prize is pinned to a date and a thing that happened on it, so that in five years the name still summons the scene. Four of these are FIFA's official honours; the last two are this file's own, chosen from the moments chapter and defended like any other verdict.

The Golden Boot — Kylian Mbappé, and the moment is the 60th minute of the quarter-final on 9 July. He had just missed a penalty, saved by Yassine Bounou; a lesser tournament dies right there. Instead he curled in his eighth goal, set up Dembélé's second six minutes later, and finished the month on ten from eight games — the first player, in the men's or women's game, to win back-to-back World Cup Golden Boots. That the Boot belongs to a fourth-place player is France's problem, catalogued two chapters ago; the award itself is unarguable.

The Golden Ball — Rodri, and the moment is the whole of 14 July. The Dallas semi-final was the closest a midfielder comes to a perfect game: France's record scorer erased, the tournament's most feared counter-attack never once launched, and ESPN's sixty-year dataset needed to describe what had just not happened. The Ball usually goes to the man who makes things occur. This one went, correctly, to the man who made them impossible — with Messi taking the silver at 39, eight goals into his ninth decade of relevance.

The Golden Glove — Unai Simón, and the moment is a number: six. Six clean sheets in one World Cup had never been done. The honest footnote, printed here because this file prints its workings: Simón was the least-worked elite goalkeeper in the field, protected by the best defensive structure of the modern era. The Glove measures the fortress, not just the man. It still had to be kept, eight times, and was.

Best Young Player — Pau Cubarsí, and the moment is second-half stoppage time of the final. The 19-year-old spent the month making experienced forwards look pre-read, and the final's decisive incident happened to him: the reckless challenge that sent him airborne and reduced Argentina to ten. A defender's award, won the way defenders win things — by being where the tournament's violence eventually arrived.

Performance in Defeat10Vozinha · 3 · VII
Match of the Tournament3–2Azteca · 5 · VII

The Signal's Performance in Defeat — Vozinha, 3 July, Miami. Ten saves against the world champions, five of them from Messi, in a game his country led twice and lost to an own goal in the 111th minute. Emiliano Martínez would later make ten saves in the final and the world called it the greatest goalkeeping display a final has seen; Cape Verde's 40-year-old had already produced its equal in the round of 32, for a nation of half a million, and went home for it. This award exists for exactly that injustice.

The Signal's Match of the Tournament — Mexico 2–3 England, 5 July, Estadio Azteca. Chosen over the ten-goal bronze final on a strict rule: stakes count. The bronze was carnival; the Azteca was a knockout tie in which a host nation, its stadium and a refereeing storm all bent towards one result and England refused it anyway. CNN filed it as possibly the best game of the World Cup the same night, and nothing in the fortnight after threatened the claim while the stakes stayed real. Five goals, a red card, 80-odd thousand voices, and the tournament's two best communal hours.

One prize deliberately not given: goal of the tournament. Torres's winner was the most important strike of the summer and nowhere near its best; the candidates below it were not verifiable to this file's standard from the record available. A review that invents a winner to fill a line has stopped being a review.

FILE 07

The Tournament in Numbers

What 104 matches actually measured

The headline number — 308 goals, more than any World Cup before it — is true and mostly meaningless, because this tournament played forty more matches than the ones it beat. A numbers chapter worth the name divides before it celebrates. So: divide. The workings are printed under each exhibit, and every figure names its source.

Divided, the record survives — barely, and interestingly. At 104 matches, 308 goals is 2.96 a game, against 2.69 for Qatar's 64 matches and a shade under 2.7 for the two editions before that. The expansion's critics predicted forty games of parked buses and dead rubbers dragging the average down; the opposite happened, and the scorecards chapter already told you why. The sides ranked thirtieth to sixtieth in the world have closed the gap faster than the top ten have pulled away, and a format that admits them produces more football, not less. That is the honest pro-expansion argument, and this was the first tournament to make it with data.

The stat band's third cell is the one the record book will not print, so this file will. Scientists for Global Responsibility calculated the tournament's greenhouse-gas footprint at roughly double the average of the four World Cups before it — a consequence of three enormous host countries and a travel map drawn for television. Alongside it sit the summer's other off-field ledger lines: afternoon kick-offs hot enough that dozens of players signed a letter, coordinated by the New Weather Institute, demanding stronger heat protocols; cooling breaks that broadcasters promptly filled with advertising; and FIFA's demand-based ticket pricing, which supporters' groups said priced ordinary fans out of the marquee dates. None of this cost the football its quality. All of it belongs in the account of what the quality cost.

Thirty-nine days as they crossed your own Sunday mornings

Yesterday morning your weekly went to press with the final unplayed. This chapter settles the account: the tournament as it actually crossed your five Sunday mornings, and the one that ended a few hours after the last of them.

What Changes Next

2030 is already argued over — here is what is actually settled

A World Cup's last act is always a land grab for the next one, and this one's ended with the argument already running. What follows is the file's forward commitment: what is settled about 2030, what is merely proposed, and the calls this magazine is prepared to make now, in ink, to be graded when the centenary tournament arrives.

The Cheat Sheet · 2030, As It Stands
Settled
Three hosts, one centenary

Spain, Portugal and Morocco stage the 2030 World Cup from 8 June. The reigning champions co-host; Morocco's back-to-back quarter-finals just made that feel less like geography and more like seeding (Al Jazeera, 19 Jul).

Settled, oddly
South America gets three games

Centenary fixtures at the Estadio Centenario in Montevideo — where the 1930 final was played — plus one each in Buenos Aires and Asunción. Three intercontinental away days bolted onto a European-African tournament, in honour of the first one.

Holding
48 teams, for now

The 2026 format carries forward as things stand: 48 teams, 104 matches, a round of 32. Every structural lesson of this summer — travel, heat, the eight-game grind — transfers directly to the next edition's planning.

The live fight is the number. CONMEBOL, with Uruguay's federation president as its loudest voice, has formally proposed expanding the centenary edition to 64 teams — half the member nations of world football at one tournament. Spain, Portugal and Morocco oppose it. So do UEFA and CONCACAF, and so, on this summer's evidence, should anyone who watched the thing being celebrated. The strongest case for 48 was made on the pitch in June: Cape Verde held the champions, DR Congo returned after five decades and advanced, and the goals-per-match rate went up, not down. That case has a load-bearing wall — the middle class of world football earned its places through qualifying, and a 64-team edition would hand out what 2026's newcomers earned. This file's committed position: 48 vindicated itself; 64 would spend the credibility it just banked. If FIFA expands anyway, re-read this paragraph in 2030 and grade it.

Full Time

Put the file away; the club season is five Saturdays off

Every World Cup leaves one image behind that outlasts its own highlights reel. From 39 days and 104 matches, this file's nomination is not the trophy lift, and it is not even Messi on the grass at MetLife, though that one will hang in more places. It is from the first Friday of July, in Miami: a 40-year-old goalkeeper from a country of half a million people making his tenth save of the night against the champions of the world, two hours from the biggest upset the tournament has ever nearly staged. Cape Verde went home that evening. The idea of them did not, and the manager's version of it deserves to close the file the way it opened the summer.

The verdict on the whole enterprise, committed in ink like everything else here: this was a very good World Cup wearing the costume of an excessive one. The 48-team experiment produced more football and, against most predictions including this magazine's instincts, better football — its group stage carried by nations the old format never admitted, its knockouts the strongest in memory, its final a genuine clash of methods settled by a substitute's only goal of the month. The costs were real and are itemised three chapters back: the heat, the pricing, the emissions ledger, the sheer commercial volume of the thing. The record will keep the football. The account books should keep the rest.

And now it stops. The bunting comes down in three countries, Spain fly home with a second star, and club football clears its throat five Saturdays away — with a title defence to organise in Turin's case, a striker to replace, and seven internationals coming home with stories. The next World Cup is four years and one long argument off. Put the file away.
