THE WALL STREET CRASH · 97 YEARS ON
NOT THIS WEEK · A STANDING STORY · 1929–1954 · LATEST DEVELOPMENT NOV 1954

The permanently high plateau

Days before the market fell further and faster than it ever had, the most famous economist in America told the country the good times were a new and settled altitude. He was wrong by a quarter of a century. The week this is published sits inside the anniversary of the days he was wrong.

BY THE EDITOR · THE LONG READ · NEW YORK · A STANDING STORY

On the morning of Thursday 24 October 1929, the crowd that gathered on Wall Street did not yet have a name for what it was watching. By midday the pavement outside the New York Stock Exchange was dense with men in hats and overcoats, standing and waiting, because the one thing they could not do was see inside. On the floor, prices were falling faster than the ticker could print them; the tape ran so far behind the trades that a share sold at ten o'clock might not be reported until noon, by which time it was worth less again. Nobody in the street knew the real number. They knew only that the number was moving the wrong way, and that it had been doing so for long enough to bring them down to the Exchange to stand under its columns and wait. The day would be remembered as Black Thursday, the first of the four days that ended the long boom of the 1920s.

FIG. 01 The crowd on Wall Street outside the New York Stock Exchange during the panic of October 1929 — shut out of the floor, and reading the fall from one another's faces.US-gov · public domain · via Wikimedia Commons

The fall was so startling because the climb had felt so secure. Through the second half of the decade the American stock market had risen almost without interruption, and the rise had drawn in people who had never owned a share before. Much of the money they bought with was not theirs. Investors bought on margin — putting down a fraction of a stock's price in cash and borrowing the rest from their broker, who borrowed in turn from the banks — so that a small sum could command a large holding, and a rising market handed back profits far larger than the stake. By August 1929 brokers had lent out more than $8.5 billion to customers buying on margin, a sum larger than the entire amount of currency then circulating in the United States. Prices had climbed so far ahead of the earnings beneath them that the average share changed hands at 32.6 times its company's annual profit — a level that assumed the profits would go on growing more or less forever.

Into that assumption, in the first week of October, stepped Irving Fisher. Fisher was not a crank; he was the most eminent economist in the country, a Yale professor whose work on interest and prices still shapes the discipline. He had money in the market himself. And he told anyone who would listen that the high prices were not a bubble about to burst but a new and permanent elevation — that stocks had reached, in the phrase that outlived everything else he wrote, a permanently high plateau.

He was not alone, and that is the point of quoting him. Fisher said out loud what the market had priced in: that the ordinary rules had been suspended, that the climb was the new ground floor rather than a ledge. The plateau was not a forecast so much as a description of what everyone already believed. It is the belief, not the man, that the phrase has come to stand for — the moment at which a market stops thinking of its gains as gains and starts thinking of them as the natural order of things.

The natural order lasted a little under seven weeks. When Black Thursday's selling began, it was met by one of the stranger episodes of the whole crisis. A group of the most powerful bankers in New York, led by Richard Whitney, acting vice-president of the Exchange, pooled their resources and sent Whitney onto the floor to buy. He walked to the post where U.S. Steel traded and bid well above the going price for a large block of it, then moved from post to post placing similar orders in the other blue-chip names. It was theatre as much as finance — a public show of confidence by men who could afford to make one — and for a day it worked. The market steadied into the close and rallied on Friday. Over the weekend the newspapers reported that the worst was over. The bankers had held the line.

THE DETAIL · THE CROWD THAT COULDN'T SEE IN

On Black Thursday the ticker ran so far behind that nobody in the street knew the real number.

The one thing the men on the pavement could not do was see inside the Exchange. The tape ran so far behind the trades that a share sold at ten o'clock might not be reported until noon, by which time it was worth less again. They knew only that the number was moving the wrong way — and that it had been doing so long enough to bring them down to stand under the columns and wait.

They had not. When the Exchange reopened on Monday 28 October the selling resumed, and this time there was no floor beneath it and no syndicate willing to catch it. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 12.82 per cent in a single session, to 260.64 — a larger one-day drop than anything the index had recorded. Tuesday 29 October was worse still. Around 16.4 million shares changed hands, a volume that would stand as a record for decades, as holders sold at any price they could get and often at no price at all, because there were no buyers. The Dow lost another 11.73 per cent, closing at 230.07. Taken together, Black Monday and Black Tuesday erased 23.05 per cent of the market's value in two days — the worst two-day fall in the index's history, a record that still stood the better part of a century later.

What the two-day figure does not convey is how long the drop kept going after the headlines stopped. The crash of late October was not the bottom; it was the beginning of a slide that ran for nearly three more years. The record of the moment makes this clearer than any summary. The New York Times front page of 30 October 1929 — the morning after Black Tuesday — set the collapse in the same severe column type it used for wars and elections, which is to say it did not yet know how far down the story went.

The September high of 381.17 was not a number the market would see again for a very long time. The Dow kept falling until the summer of 1932, by which point it had lost nearly nine-tenths of its 1929 value. It did not climb back to the 381.17 of 3 September 1929 until 23 November 1954 — twenty-five years, two months and twenty days after the peak. An investor who bought at the top and held on, reinvesting nothing and selling nothing, would have waited a quarter of a century merely to break even in nominal terms. The plateau, in the end, was real; it simply turned out to be the one the market spent the next generation climbing back up to.

The plateau held for seven weeks. The climb back took a quarter of a century. TRANSMISSION · THE LONG READ

The deeper damage, though, was not to the people who owned shares. It was to the banks that had lent against them, and to the millions who had never bought a share in their lives. As loans went bad and depositors took fright, the American banking system began to fail in waves: 2,294 US banks collapsed in 1931 alone, each failure wiping out the savings behind it and pulling credit out of the towns that depended on it. The loss of those banks turned a market crash into a general contraction — the Great Depression — in which output fell, prices fell, and by the depth of it roughly a quarter of the American workforce had no job to go to.

FIG. 03 A march of the unemployed, 1930. The crash fell hardest not on shareholders but on the banks that had lent against shares — and on the towns those banks took down with them.Unknown author · public domain · via Wikimedia Commons

Out of that wreckage came the rules that still govern Wall Street. In 1933 the Glass-Steagall Act forced a separation between ordinary deposit-taking banks and the investment houses that traded on markets, so that a family's savings could no longer be gambled on the floor and lost there. In 1934 Congress created the Securities and Exchange Commission to police the markets, demand honest disclosure from the companies that sold shares, and curb the margin lending that had let $8.5 billion of borrowed money inflate the boom. Richard Whitney, the banker whose floor rally had briefly held the line on Black Thursday, later went to prison for embezzlement — a small, exact epilogue to the age of confidence he had tried to perform back into being.

THE CASE AGAINST · THE CRASH WAS A SYMPTOM, NOT THE CAUSE

Blame the banks of 1931, not the floor of 1929.

The popular memory makes the crash the hinge on which the Depression turned, but serious students of the period have long resisted that reading. In the Friedman–Schwartz account, the stock market fall of October 1929 was a severe shock, not a sentence: what turned a sharp recession into the Great Depression was the banking panics of 1930–33 and the authorities' failure to stop them, allowing the money supply to shrink by roughly a third. On that reading the crash exposed the fragility but did not have to produce the catastrophe. There is a narrower caution too. Only around sixteen per cent of American households owned stock in 1929, so the direct blow of the crash — fortunes lost on the floor — fell on a smaller slice of the country than the myth of a nation ruined overnight suggests. The crash made for the unforgettable image; it was the machinery behind it that did the lasting harm.

Which is why the anniversary is worth marking rather than merely observing. This issue goes out on 25 October, inside the crash's own calendar — Black Thursday fell on the 24th, Black Tuesday on the 29th — and it does so into a week that rhymes, quietly, with the thing being remembered. Britain's inflation figure for the year to September, published on 21 October, came in at 3.5 per cent, higher than the market expected and the last such reading before the Chancellor's Budget on 28 October. Ahead of that Budget the fiscal headroom against the government's own rules is reported to have roughly halved since the spring. None of this is a crash, and the comparison should not be stretched into one; the measures of the Budget itself are another section's business. But the rhyme is in the register, not the numbers. It is the gap between what a market, or a government, assumes about the ground it stands on and what the ground turns out to bear.

The surest sign a market has climbed too high is the moment everyone agrees it cannot come down. TRANSMISSION · THE LONG READ

Fisher's phrase survives because it names that gap precisely. A permanently high plateau is not a lie; it is a mood — the settled, reasonable-sounding conviction that the present level of things is the level they will stay at, that the rules have changed in one's own favour and will not change back. It is the most comfortable belief a market can hold and the most expensive one it can be wrong about. The crash of 1929 is remembered less for the two days of falling prices than for the seven weeks before them, when the most careful mind in American economics looked at the highest market in its history and saw not a peak but a floor. The lesson it left is not that markets fall. It is that the surest sign a market has climbed too high is the moment everyone agrees it cannot come down.

BAND 10 — THIS WEEK IN HISTORY HISTORY · DATED

On 25 October 1415 — St Crispin's Day, 611 years ago this week — a sick and outnumbered English army turned to face the flower of French chivalry across a narrow, rain-sodden field near the castle of Agincourt. Henry V had landed in Normandy that August, taken Harfleur after a costly siege, and was marching his depleted, dysentery-ridden army toward Calais when the French blocked the road and forced the fight he had hoped to avoid.

The ground decided it as much as the men did. Freshly ploughed farmland, soaked by days of October rain, lay between two woods that funnelled the French into a front far too narrow for their numbers. French men-at-arms in heavy plate advanced on foot and horse into that mud, where they bogged down, pressed together, and could not bring their weight or their weapons to bear. Momentum became a trap: the deeper ranks pushed the front ones into a crush from which many never rose.

Momentum became a trap: the deeper ranks pushed the front ones into a crush from which many never rose. TRANSMISSION · THIS WEEK IN HISTORY

Henry's strength was his archers. Thousands of longbowmen stood behind sharpened stakes, loosing volleys of bodkin-tipped arrows into the advancing column. When the French line stalled in the mire, the lightly armoured bowmen dropped their bows and waded in with mallets, hatchets and knives, killing heavily armoured knights who could barely move. The French nobility was gutted in a single afternoon; English losses were, by every account, comparatively slight. It remains one of the most lopsided victories in medieval warfare.

Its long afterlife, though, is literary. Nearly two centuries later Shakespeare set his St Crispin's Day speech at the heart of Henry V, Act IV — the king promising that he and his "band of brothers" would be remembered as long as the feast day came round. It is the speech, not the arrow-storm, that most people now carry of Agincourt: a battle survived chiefly as words that were almost certainly never spoken.

By a quirk of the calendar, 25 October is also the date of one of military history's most famous disasters. On that day in 1854, during the Crimean War, the British Light Brigade charged Russian guns in the valley below Balaclava. The order was garbled in the passing. Lord Raglan meant the cavalry to retake guns the Russians were hauling off from a captured redoubt; by the time the written command had travelled down the chain and been relayed by the men on the ridge, it had lost its object, and the brigade was instead sent straight down a long valley flanked by artillery on three sides.

Roughly 600 horsemen rode about a mile and a half under converging fire, reached the guns at the far end, and fell back — having lost a large part of their strength for nothing gained. Tennyson, then Poet Laureate, read the newspaper account and wrote "The Charge of the Light Brigade" within weeks. "Half a league, half a league, / Half a league onward," it begins, into "the valley of Death"; and, extraordinarily for a patriotic poem of its day, "Some one had blundered." Like Agincourt, Balaclava survives less as a battle than as a line of verse: one lopsided victory, one brave catastrophe, four and a half centuries apart on the same calendar day.

FITNESS · BLOCK 3, FREQUENCY & VOLUME

This week's question is frequency. Having settled last time that the hard sets taken near failure are what drive growth, the natural follow-up is how often to train each muscle — twice a week, or spread across four. The answer, stated plainly before the caveats: it mostly doesn't matter. A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis from Tao and colleagues, in the International Journal of Sports Science & Coaching, reinforces the equal-volume consensus — when total weekly volume is matched, training a muscle twice a week and training it four times produce similar hypertrophy and strength. How you slice the volume across the week matters far less than the weekly total itself.

How you slice the volume across the week matters far less than the weekly total itself. TRANSMISSION · THE SESSION

Now the qualifiers, because that's where the practical value sits. "Volume matched" is the whole trick: higher frequency helps only when it lets you accumulate more quality sets than you could cram into fewer sessions — a lifter who can't recover from twenty hard sets for legs in one day will land them better spread over three. Frequency, then, is a tool for fitting the volume you need into a week you can actually recover from, not a growth lever in its own right. Which fixes the planning order: settle the weekly set count per muscle first, then pick whatever split delivers it without junk sessions or sessions so long the last sets turn sloppy.

DO THIS WEEK · THE SESSION

Count your current weekly hard sets per muscle group before you touch the split — then distribute that number across the days you'll realistically train, rather than letting the schedule decide the volume.

The meta-analysis says the weekly total drives the result and the split is just logistics; deciding frequency first solves the wrong variable and usually means guessing at the number that actually matters.

This week's long read follows a market certain of its own permanence right up to the two days it was proved wrong — the kind of collapse for which English keeps one blunt, ancient word: bankrupt. Say it slowly and a picture surfaces that has nothing to do with ticker tape or margin calls. The word is Italian in its bones — banca rotta, a broken bench — and it was minted in the crowded market squares of Renaissance Italy, where modern finance was being assembled one coin at a time, centuries before anyone thought to raise an exchange.

The banca was a literal object: the bench or counter a money-changer sat behind to do his trade. Florence, Venice and Genoa ran on these men. They swapped one city's coin for another's, weighed and tested metal of uncertain purity, took deposits and advanced loans, and settled debts between merchants who never touched cash themselves. The bench was the business — the fixed, public spot in the square where a dealer's customers knew to find him day after day. It is the same banca that, worn down through French and Latin, hands modern English the word bank.

The bench was the business — the fixed, public spot in the square where a dealer's customers knew to find him day after day. TRANSMISSION · DOWN THE RABBIT HOLE

And rotta is simply "broken." A dealer who could not return his depositors' money, or cover the bills he had accepted, was finished — and the tradition that fastened itself to the phrase is that his bench was broken, snapped or smashed where it stood, so that everyone in the market could see he no longer traded. Whether the breaking was always literal is uncertain; what matters is what it signified. A broken bench meant a failed house, a man whose word on money could no longer be trusted. The ruin was deliberate and public, staged in the very square where the credit had been extended.

The ruin was deliberate and public, staged in the very square where the credit had been extended. TRANSMISSION · DOWN THE RABBIT HOLE

From banca rotta the term travelled outward along the same trade routes that carried Italian banking itself. Latinised forms and the French banqueroute moved it north, and by the 1560s English merchants and lawyers were writing bankrupt — the spelling bent toward the Latin ruptus, "broken" — to mean a debtor who had failed utterly. Four centuries of statute and stigma have grown around it since, but the image never quite left. When a brokerage folded in the panic of 1929 and its partners walked out for the last time, the word that followed them to the door still carried, buried a few layers down, the sound of a wooden bench being broken in a Florentine market.
