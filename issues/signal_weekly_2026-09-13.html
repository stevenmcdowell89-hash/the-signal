The machine and the hand on it.

An Italian threads Monza from nineteenth, a phone learns to fold, and eighty-six years on, the aircraft that held the line.

A week of machines, and the people who bend them to a will. Last Sunday Kimi Antonelli started the Italian Grand Prix nineteenth on his own home grid and won it — the first Italian to take his home race since Ludovico Scarfiotti in 1966, and from the second-lowest position anyone has ever won a Formula 1 race. Monza, of all places, went quietly delirious.

Two days later Apple folded the iPhone in half. The first foldable — the iPhone Duo — is a phone that opens like a book, a machine reimagined by the hands that build it rather than the ones that drive it. Different discipline, same instinct: take the thing everyone thought was finished and find one more gear in it.

And tomorrow, 15 September, is the 86th Battle of Britain Day. So, the day before the anniversary, a look back at the summer of 1940 — not the plucky-few legend but the real machinery of it: the fighters, the aircrew drawn from a dozen nations, and the radar-and-plotting system that quietly held the line.

The few, and the system behind them

In the summer of 1940 the Royal Air Force turned back the Luftwaffe over southern England and gave Britain the story it has told about itself ever since: the plucky few against the many. The pilots were real, and so was the danger. But the thing that actually won the battle was less romantic and more modern than the legend — a network of radar, telephones and plotting tables that told the few exactly where to be.

BY THE EDITOR · THE LONG READ · A STANDING STORY

The Battle of Britain was fought from 10 July to 31 October 1940, and its decisive day is commemorated every 15 September. It was the first battle in history fought entirely in the air. After the fall of France, Germany needed to neutralise the RAF before it could attempt Operation Sea Lion, the planned seaborne invasion of southern England; the Luftwaffe set out to destroy Fighter Command, and Fighter Command set out to survive. The fighting escalated in stages. The Luftwaffe's main assault, "Adlertag" — Eagle Day — opened on 13 August 1940. Five days later came "the Hardest Day," 18 August, which cost both sides so heavily that the slow, vulnerable Junkers Ju 87 Stuka dive-bomber was largely withdrawn from operations over England afterwards.

FIG. 01 A Spitfire and a Messerschmitt Bf 109 in flight in 1940 — the two fighters whose duel became the emblem of the battle, though the Hurricane, not the Spitfire, did most of the RAF's work.Royal Air Force / IWM · public domain · via Wikimedia Commons

The climax arrived on 15 September 1940, the date now marked as Battle of Britain Day. The Luftwaffe sent roughly 1,120 aircraft against London — about 620 fighters escorting some 500 bombers — in an effort to break Fighter Command by sheer weight. The RAF met them in strength across the whole approach. British claims that day were wildly optimistic: 81 German aircraft were reported shot down in the morning alone, when the true figure across the day was about 58 German aircraft downed for the loss of around 29 RAF fighters. The exact tally mattered less than what it signalled. The RAF had not been destroyed, was not going to be, and could still put a wall of fighters over the capital on demand. On 17 September, two days later, Hitler postponed Operation Sea Lion. The invasion never came.

Four weeks earlier, on 20 August 1940, Winston Churchill had already given the airmen their name. Reporting to the House of Commons on the fighting, he delivered the line that has outlived almost everything else about the battle, and gave Fighter Command the title it has carried since: "the Few."

The Few were fewer, and more varied, than the phrase suggests. Some 2,927 aircrew flew in the Battle of Britain, and 574 of them — about one in five — were not British. Poles, Czechs, Canadians, New Zealanders, Australians, South Africans, Belgians, Free French and others filled cockpits alongside the RAF's own. The highest-scoring unit of the whole battle was No. 303 (Polish) Squadron, credited with 126 kills in 42 days despite only joining the fighting halfway through. Its top pilot, and the top-scoring pilot of the entire battle, was Josef František — a Czech, flying with the Poles, credited with 17 kills. The purely-British legend of 1940 is, at the level of the roster, not quite true.

The Few were fewer, and more varied, than the phrase suggests — one in five of the 2,927 were not British at all. TRANSMISSION №025 · THE LONG READ

What the legend leaves out entirely is the machine. The pilots were the sharp end of something new, and it was the something new — not courage, which both sides had in abundance — that decided the battle. Britain in 1940 possessed the world's first integrated air-defence network, later called the Dowding System after Air Chief Marshal Hugh Dowding, who built it. It fused two streams of information into one picture. Chain Home, a chain of coastal radar masts, detected incoming raids while they were still forming up over France and the Channel. Once the enemy crossed the coast and passed beyond the radar's seaward gaze, the Observer Corps tracked them visually from the ground. Both streams flowed into the filter room at Bentley Priory, where they were reconciled into a single plotted picture of the air battle, then passed down through Group and Sector operations rooms that scrambled and vectored squadrons to exactly where they were needed.

FIG. 02 The Dowding System, schematically: radar and ground observers feeding one filtered picture, and orders flowing back down to the squadrons. It is a command-and-control diagram before the term existed — the real war-winner of 1940.CC0 · via Wikimedia Commons

This is why the Few could be so few and still win. Without the system, defending fighters had to fly standing patrols and hope to stumble on the enemy, or scramble too late to climb to altitude. With it, they could sit on the ground, conserve fuel and pilots, and be launched to intercept with enough warning to gain height and position. The Hurricane — which outnumbered the more famous Spitfire by roughly two to one — and the Spitfire were directed against the Messerschmitt Bf 109E fighters and the Heinkel He 111, Dornier Do 17 and Junkers Ju 88 bombers with an efficiency the Luftwaffe could not match, because the Luftwaffe was fighting blind by comparison. This is the argument Stephen Bungay makes in The Most Dangerous Enemy, widely regarded as the definitive single-volume account of the battle: the Luftwaffe's decisive error was that it never concentrated its attacks on destroying the radar and the command-and-control network. It attacked airfields, aircraft and cities, but not systematically the nervous system that made the whole defence work. Bungay's reading turns the battle from a contest of aces into a contest of organisation — one the Germans lost partly because they did not understand what they were up against.

FIG. 03 A preserved Spitfire and Hurricane flying together, photographed in 2007. The Hurricane did the heavier, less glamorous work in 1940 — the bomber-killer to the Spitfire's fighter-catcher.CC BY 2.0 · via Flickr

There is a second revisionist case, and it cuts deeper into the myth. The historian Richard Overy, in The Battle of Britain: Myth and Reality, argues that the picture of a razor-thin escape — a plucky few holding off overwhelming numbers by the narrowest of margins — does not survive contact with the figures. A German seaborne invasion of Britain in 1940, he contends, was never truly viable: Germany lacked the shipping and the logistics to land and supply an army across a contested Channel against the Royal Navy, whatever happened in the air. And the two air forces were far more evenly matched than the legend allows. Crucially, Britain out-produced Germany in fighters throughout the battle. Under Lord Beaverbrook's Ministry of Aircraft Production, British fighter output leapt from 141 aircraft in February 1940 to 496 in July — about two and a half times the German rate. The consequence was startling: RAF fighter strength actually rose, from 644 to 732 aircraft between July and October, even as the Luftwaffe's serviceable fighters fell from 725 to 275. On this reading Britain ended the battle stronger than it began it, and the "narrow-run thing" is partly a story the winners told afterwards.

Britain ended the battle stronger than it began it — fighter strength rose from 644 to 732 even as the Luftwaffe's fell to 275. TRANSMISSION №025 · THE LONG READ

Both accounts describe the same summer, and they do not fully reconcile. The traditional reading — the one carved on memorials and repeated every September — holds that a small band of pilots stood between Britain and defeat, and that their courage was the margin. The revisionist reading holds that the margin was wider than that, secured by radar, factories and the sheer impossibility of the invasion the RAF was supposedly the last line against. The honest position is that the two do not cancel out. The pilots did fly, and die, in numbers; the system did work; the production did hold; and the invasion was probably never on. That the outcome may have been more secure than it felt at the time does not make the feeling — or the flying — any less real. What changes is where the credit goes: less to a miracle, more to a country that had quietly built, in radar chains and aircraft plants and plotting rooms, the means to win.

The cost was not carried by the Few alone. Recorded losses across the whole battle ran to about 1,744 RAF aircraft and 1,542 aircrew killed, against some 1,977 Luftwaffe aircraft and 2,585 killed. And as the daylight fight over the airfields gave way to the night bombing of the cities — the Blitz — roughly 23,000 British civilians were killed. The battle for the aerodromes became a battle for the streets, and the machine that had protected the airfields could do far less for terraced housing in the dark.

FIG. 04 A preserved Hawker Hurricane, photographed in 2009. Rugged and numerous, it was the workhorse of Fighter Command in 1940 — guided to its targets by the network behind it as much as by the pilot in it.CC BY 2.0 · via Flickr

Eighty-six years on, the substance of 1940 has an uncomfortable habit of rhyming with the present, because Europe is once again arguing about how to defend itself. On 1–2 September 2026, Germany formally blamed Russia for a drone attack on Leipzig/Halle Airport — a hub for NATO airlift and support to Ukraine — in which an explosive-carrying drone struck a parked Antonov An-124 heavy transport. Berlin responded by ordering the closure of the Russian House in Berlin and the Russian consulate in Bonn. The particulars are wholly of this decade: a cargo drone, not a Dornier; a logistics hub, not an airfield. But the shape is old. It is again about early warning — knowing what is coming, and from where — and about the integrity of an alliance's ability to move men and materiel across a continent.

The political answer is old too. At his first Prime Minister's Questions in early September 2026, Britain's new prime minister, Andy Burnham, set out where the money would go, pledging to fully fund the Defence Investment Plan and to meet the country's NATO commitments — a path toward spending 3.5 percent of GDP on defence — by 2035.

Strip away the eighty-six years and the two moments are asking the same four questions. Can a country see the threat in time — the Chain Home question, now a question about radar, satellites and drone defence? Can it command what it has as one system rather than a scatter of parts — the Bentley Priory question, now about integrated air and missile defence across NATO? Can it out-produce the pressure — the Beaverbrook question, now about whether European factories can build fast enough? And can an alliance hold — the coalition of Poles and Czechs and Canadians in 1940, now the thirty-two flags over Leipzig? What 1940 was really about was never just the men in the cockpits. It was early warning, integrated command, industrial output and alliance. Those four things won the battle, and they are precisely the four things Europe is spending 2026 arguing about how to pay for.

FIG. 05 The Battle of Britain Memorial, photographed in 2011. The stone remembers the Few; the harder lesson is that they were the visible edge of a system — and that the system is what a country has to build before the battle, not during it.CC BY-SA 2.0 · via Flickr

The monuments, understandably, remember the pilots. They are the part of the story a country can put a face to, and with 1,542 RAF aircrew killed across the battle the remembering is owed. But the deeper lesson of the summer of 1940 is the one that does not fit on a plinth: that the battle was won on the ground before it was won in the air, by the unglamorous decision to build radar masts and plotting tables and aircraft factories in the years when it was possible to imagine not needing them. The Few were extraordinary. The system was decisive. And the argument now unfolding across Europe about drones over Leipzig and defence budgets by 2035 is, underneath the modern vocabulary, an argument about whether the continent will do the unglamorous building again — this time before the day it turns out to matter.

From nineteenth on the grid to the top step at home — Kimi Antonelli won the Italian Grand Prix at Monza from P19, a start line he only reached after a thirty-place drop for exceeding his engine allocation, and turned it into the most improbable win of the season. He is the first Italian to win his home Grand Prix since Ludovico Scarfiotti in 1966, it was his seventh victory of the year, and the championship lead is out to sixty-six points over George Russell. On a Sunday when the calendar was mostly still deciding things, this one had already been settled — and settled sensationally.

BAND 09 — BOOKMARK BOOKS · ON THE NIGHTSTAND

  • The Battle of Britain

    History · single-volume narrative

    James Holland's single-volume history is the fast, authoritative way into the summer this issue's Long Read revisits — the campaign fought from 10 July to 31 October 1940, still marked every 15 September as Battle of Britain Day. It moves at the pace of a thriller without cutting the scholarship, and it keeps the people in frame: the pilots, the plotters, and the factory floors that turned out fighters faster than Germany could. For anyone coming off the feature, it's the obvious next few evenings — the whole arc, told once and told well.

  • The Most Dangerous Enemy

    History · the definitive account

    Widely regarded as the definitive single-volume account, Stephen Bungay's book is the myth-busting deep read behind the Long Read's borrowed angle. Its argument is the one worth carrying away: the Luftwaffe's decisive error was failing to concentrate on Fighter Command's radar and command-and-control — the Dowding System — which was the real source of the RAF's strength. Bungay's 1940 is won less by lone courage than by an integrated air-defence network doing its job under pressure. Denser than Holland, and repaid in full for the extra weight.

  • The Will of the Many

    Fantasy · Hierarchy #1

    A change of altitude, and this issue's discovery pick: James Islington's propulsive, Roman-flavoured fantasy, the opening book of his Hierarchy sequence. It runs on a hard-driving plot and a world built around a sinister system of ceded will, and it has become one of the more talked-about fantasy openers of recent years. The peg is timing — Islington's new sequel, Scion, arrives this month — which makes the first book the natural place to start before the follow-up lands.

  • The Splendid and the Vile

    History · narrative nonfiction

    To widen the lens from the battle to the war around it: Erik Larson's narrative account of Churchill's first year in office and the Blitz that followed — the bombing campaign in which some 23,000 British civilians were killed. Larson works from diaries and official papers to keep the story at eye level, tracking a household and a government through the nights when the raids moved from the airfields to London. Narrative history at its most readable, and the gentlest on-ramp of the four.

BAND 10 — THIS WEEK IN HISTORY HISTORY · DATED

On 12 September 1940, near the town of Montignac in the Dordogne, an 18-year-old apprentice mechanic named Marcel Ravidat followed his dog, Robot, down a hole left by an uprooted tree — and found the mouth of a narrow shaft dropping into the hillside. He came back with three friends, Jacques Marsal, Georges Agniel and Simon Coencas, and over the following days they widened the gap and lowered themselves in. What their oil lamp lit up was a chamber whose walls moved with animals: horses, deer, and great long-horned cattle, painted and cut into the rock in colours that had not faded.

The images were between 15,000 and 17,000 years old — Upper Palaeolithic work, made while Europe was still under ice and long before anyone had written a word. The four had walked into one of the richest galleries of prehistoric art on Earth, sealed under a few metres of Dordogne limestone for the whole length of recorded human history, and a good deal more besides. They had gone looking, so the story goes, for a rumoured tunnel and some buried treasure. They found something older than treasure and stayed to guard it.

FIG.The painted galleries of Lascaux, in a modern (2021) photograph — horses and long-horned aurochs crowding the rock much as Ravidat's lamp first found them in 1940.CC BY 4.0 · via Wikimedia Commons

The scale is hard to hold in the head. The caves carry roughly 600 painted figures and about 1,500 engravings — herds of aurochs, horses, red deer and stags, worked in ochre, charcoal and manganese and often set in motion, one beast overlapping the next as if drawn in a hurry to keep up with them. The engravings alone outnumber the paintings more than two to one, many of them so fine they are almost invisible until the light rakes across the wall at the right angle. The centrepiece, the Hall of the Bulls, holds a single figure about 5.2 metres from nose to tail: the largest individual animal known anywhere in cave art. Whoever made it never saw the whole of it at once, working by lamplight on a curved and rising ceiling, judging the proportions of a five-metre bull from inches away.

Then comes the paradox that gives the story its point. The art survived seventeen millennia in the dark and very nearly died once it was seen. After the war Lascaux opened to the public and drew crowds — by the early 1960s some 1,200 people filed through each day, and the carbon dioxide from their breath, together with the warmth of their bodies and the spores they carried in, fed algae and a slow chemical bloom that began eating at the pigments. The threat was not vandalism or damp or time. It was attention. In 1963 France closed the cave to visitors altogether. A meticulous facsimile, Lascaux II, opened a couple of hundred metres away in 1983; a complete replica, Lascaux IV, followed in 2016; UNESCO had inscribed the site as World Heritage in 1979. The original is now entered only by a handful of conservators, and only rarely — the most famous cave in the world, kept dark so that it survives being famous. The replicas are not a consolation prize but the whole visitor experience now: Lascaux IV reproduces the entire cave, wall by painted wall, so that the crowds the real chamber cannot survive can stand in front of an exact copy a short walk away.

There is a smaller coincidence of timing worth marking. Lascaux was found in September 1940 — the same weeks the Battle of Britain was being fought out in the skies to the north, one of the century's hinge moments unfolding while four teenagers stood in torchlight before some of the oldest pictures the species has made. The bulls had waited out colder emergencies than that one, and would wait out this one too.

BAND 11 — THE DESK SERVICE

The Session

FITNESS · BLOCK 3, PROXIMITY TO FAILURE

Block 3 is a hypertrophy block run at maintenance-to-surplus, and the question it keeps posing is how hard each set really needs to be. A systematic review with meta-analysis lands the answer plainly: sets stopped one to three reps short of failure — RIR 1–3 — build muscle just as well as sets taken to momentary failure, but leave behind far less fatigue and muscle damage. That last part is where the free win lives. Because each hard set costs less to recover from, more quality sets fit into the week, and accumulated weekly volume is the real driver of growth, not the heroics of any single grinding set.

Sets stopped one to three reps short of failure build muscle as well as sets taken to failure — at a fraction of the fatigue. THE DESK · THE SESSION

Set against the wider evidence, it reads cleanly. Mechanical tension applied across a sufficient number of stimulating reps is the primary mechanism of hypertrophy; total volume then shows a dose-response with diminishing returns — more works, up to a point, and past it each added set returns less (Schoenfeld & Krieger). Taking every set to failure buys only a sliver of extra stimulus while charging a steep recovery tax: the systemic fatigue that quietly caps how much can be trained before form and output fall away. Leaving a rep or two in the tank keeps tension high where it counts and banks the recovery to do more good work across the block.

DO THIS WEEK · THE SESSION

On each main compound — squat, bench, row, press — this block, take only the last set to RIR 1–2 and leave two or three reps in reserve on the earlier sets.

The named proximity-to-failure meta-analysis puts RIR 1–3 level with training to failure for growth but at a fraction of the fatigue, so spending the hard effort only on the final set lifts total weekly quality volume — the thing that actually adds size.
