The oldest argument in the room.

A tax on imports came back at full volume this week — collapsed talks with Canada, a fresh move on China — while two crowns changed hands, in Oslo and at Zandvoort.

The week ran on handovers laid over an old quarrel. Trump's US–Canada trade talks collapsed and Washington reached for the tariff again; within days, a new move on China. Elsewhere a King died in Norway and his son acceded the same afternoon; the grid changed guard at Zandvoort. Different worlds, one rhythm — something long-settled being reopened.

Under all of it sits one question, older than anyone reading it: when a country taxes what it buys, who actually pays? A tariff is the rare tax that hides who it lands on. The daily carried the blow-by-blow. This week's Long Read does the quieter work — how a border tax really works, and the century-long argument these headlines are echoing. I've put it on the cover, 1908 cartoon and all, because the machinery explains the noise better than the noise ever explains itself.

The tariff, explained

A border tax is the oldest instrument in statecraft and the most misunderstood. What a tariff actually is, who really pays it, and how the post-war world spent forty-seven years lowering them — only to watch, this month, the trend snap back.

BY THE EDITOR · THE LONG READ · A STANDING STORY

The word arrived in English by way of arithmetic. "Tariff" comes from the Arabic ta'rif — a notification, a list of fees, from the root 'arafa, "to know" — which passed through the Italian tariffa and reached English in the late sixteenth century meaning, at first, not a customs duty at all but a table of numbers, a ready-reckoner. Only later did it settle onto the specific list it now names: the schedule of taxes a country charges on goods crossing its border. A tariff is, before it is anything else, a number on a list — a tax — and the whole argument about tariffs is really an argument about who ends up paying it.

FIG. Two centuries of the average US tariff rate. The Smoot-Hawley spike around 1930 is the last peak; the long slide after 1947 is the GATT era at work, pulling the rate into low single digits.Wikimedia Commons contributor (data: US Census/Historical Statistics) · CC-BY-SA-4.0 · via Wikimedia Commons

Start with the mechanics. A tariff is a tax on an imported good, and it is paid, as a matter of law, by the importer of record — the company that brings the goods through customs, not the foreign firm that made them. That legal fact is where most of the confusion begins: a leader can truthfully say the tariff is "charged to China" or "charged to Canada" while the cheque is written by a domestic importer. The importer, facing a higher cost, does one of three things: absorbs it and takes a thinner margin, squeezes its foreign supplier to cut prices, or passes it forward to whoever buys the product next. Recent research into the tariffs of 2025 — from the Peterson Institute, the New York Fed and the Tax Foundation — finds that, overwhelmingly, importers do the third. Studies estimate US consumers and firms bore about 90 percent of the 2025 tariff burden: the tax shows up as a higher price on the shelf, not as a cut in the exporter's profits abroad.

The effect has a shape. The direct hit — the sticker price of the taxed good — lands within weeks. A slower, indirect effect works its way through supply chains over the following nine to twelve months, as the taxed input raises the cost of everything built on top of it. And the burden does not fall evenly. Because tariffs land hardest on cheap, traded, physical goods — clothes, food, appliances, the things a lower-income household spends most of its money on — the tax is regressive: it takes a larger share from those who have least. A tariff is a tax, it is paid at home, and it is paid most by the people least able to spare it.

If that sounds like a modern finding, the argument is nearly a century old. On 17 June 1930, President Herbert Hoover signed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act, which raised already-high American import duties by roughly a fifth. He did so over an extraordinary protest: more than a thousand economists had signed a petition urging him to veto the bill, warning that walls invite walls. Canada, France and others retaliated, and countries that raised barriers against American goods cut their imports of them by 28 to 32 percent. World trade contracted, and Smoot-Hawley entered folk memory as the tariff that caused the Great Depression.

That last part is where the history needs correcting. Quantitative economic historians — the most careful recent work is an NBER study by Mitchener and colleagues — find that Smoot-Hawley's tariffs account for only about a quarter of the collapse in American imports between 1930 and 1932. Imports fell around 41 percent; the ordinary gravity of the recession, the study estimates, would have pulled them down about 32 percent on its own. The tariff made a bad situation worse; it did not create it. The Depression was overwhelmingly a monetary catastrophe — a collapse of banks, credit and the money supply — and the tariff was a self-inflicted wound laid on top of it, not the wound itself.

FIG. 01 Container ships and cranes at a port terminal, pictured in 2009 — the physical channel a tariff taxes. Every duty is ultimately a charge on the boxes coming off ships like these.Roger Jones / geograph.org.uk · CC-BY-SA-2.0 · via Wikimedia Commons

The lesson the post-war world drew was that tit-for-tat retaliation turns a downturn into a rout — and that the way to stop the 1930s repeating was to bind tariffs into rules, out of the hands of any single angry government. On 30 October 1947, twenty-three countries met in Geneva and signed the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade. Over the next forty-seven years, through eight successive rounds of negotiation ending in 1994, the members bargained their tariffs down together, each cut traded for a matching cut abroad. The average tariff among them fell from about 22 percent to roughly 5 percent. In 1995 the World Trade Organization succeeded GATT, turning the agreement into a permanent institution with a court to enforce it. This was deliberate: the architects had watched the 1930s and built a machine to make the reflex that produced Smoot-Hawley expensive and slow. That long descent is the story the chart above tells — the volatile nineteenth century, the last spike in 1930, then the steady fall to the low single-digit average a whole generation of shoppers has grown up assuming is simply how prices work.

That is the grain against which this week runs. On 22 August 2026, trade talks between the United States and Canada collapsed after last-minute pressure from Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, and Washington imposed 50 percent tariffs on about $20 billion of Canadian goods. Canada's prime minister, Mark Carney, called the American demands "uneconomic, unfair" and said they "undermined the net benefits for Canada." "They asked too much and offered too little," he said. Then he did the thing the men in Geneva built their machine to prevent: he matched. From 8 September, Canada will levy dollar-for-dollar tariffs on American steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, and electronics. Each is a tax paid, in the end, by Canadian buyers of American goods — retaliation as domestic as the tariff it answers.

The same week brought more of the pattern. Trump moved toward a new tariff on China — reported at around 7.5 percent — to punish it for flooding markets with underpriced goods, pitched carefully low so as not to endanger a one-year trade truce or a planned Trump-Xi summit expected late in September. And on 24 August the US Treasury warned of fresh secondary sanctions against countries still trading with Iran — a threat aimed squarely at China, which is Iran's largest trading partner, as the US-Israeli war on Iran neared its sixth month. Tariffs, sanctions, secondary sanctions: the border tax is back as a first-order instrument of statecraft, used less to raise revenue than to apply pressure.

The century of history offers not a verdict but a lens. A tariff is politically seductive because its costs are hidden and its targets foreign: the tax is invisible at the till, folded into a price the shopper blames on inflation, while the retaliation lands first on someone else's exporters. That asymmetry — pain easy to inflict and hard to trace — is exactly why the post-war order took the instrument away from national tempers and wrapped it in rules. When a government raises a tariff and promises a foreign country will pay, the research says its own citizens pay about 90 percent of it, the poorest most of all.

THE CASE AGAINST · THE OTHER READING

Not every tariff is Smoot-Hawley.

The honest counter-argument starts by conceding the history above. If Smoot-Hawley did not cause the Depression — if the damage was real but modest against an overwhelmingly monetary collapse — then "tariffs equal catastrophe" is itself a folk myth, only a more fashionable one. Trade scholarship makes a narrower, defensible case for the tool. A targeted tariff can be a legitimate response to genuine dumping, where a state-backed exporter sells below cost to capture a market and hollow out a domestic industry; the China measure this week is pitched, at least, in those terms. An infant industry may need temporary shelter to reach scale. A country that depends on rivals for its steel or its chips may decide some resilience is worth a price. The real risk the record identifies is not the border tax as such but the broad, retaliatory wall — the reflexive, across-the-board, matched-and-matched-again escalation that turned a recession into a trade war in 1930. The distinction the 2026 headlines blur: a scalpel and a sledgehammer are both called tariffs.

BAND 09 — BOOKMARK BOOKS · ON THE NIGHTSTAND

  • Clashing over Commerce

    Non-fiction · economic history · US trade policy

    If this week's Long Read left you wanting the full sweep, Douglas A. Irwin's history is the book to reach for. It walks the whole arc of American tariffs from the founding to the present, and it does the useful thing of showing that today's fights are old fights in new clothes — revenue, protection, and reciprocity have been pulling against each other for two centuries. Irwin writes as an economist but keeps the politics in view, so the numbers never float free of the people arguing over them. It is long and thorough rather than breezy, but it is the one book that turns a headline about tariffs into something you can actually reason about. The definitive account, and the calm counterweight to a noisy week.

  • The Will of the Many

    Fantasy · epic · series opener

    Big, propulsive epic fantasy for the reader who lives for a doorstop with a system to figure out. James Islington drops an orphaned outsider into an elite academy inside an empire built on a power its people can hand up the ranks like currency, and the plot moves fast enough that the world-building never stalls. It is plotted rather than plodding — mysteries stacked on mysteries, factions with real teeth, and a hero carrying a secret worth keeping. Islington earns his page count by paying off almost everything he sets up, which is rarer in the genre than it should be. The timing is the point: the sequel, Scion, lands on 1 September, so this is the week to start book one and arrive at the new release already hooked rather than playing catch-up.

  • The Almost Nearly Perfect People

    Non-fiction · travel · the Nordic countries

    With Norway in the news this week, here is the witty, sceptical tour of how the Nordic countries actually work. Michael Booth lives among the Danes, Swedes, Norwegians, Finns and Icelanders and pokes at the happiness-survey clichés until something more honest falls out — the taxes, the conformity, the quiet contradictions behind the miracle. It is affectionate rather than sour, and it makes the region legible without flattening it into a Scandi fantasy — you come away understanding why the model works and where it strains, not just that it exists. A brisk, funny read that pairs neatly with the week's succession headlines, and a good corrective to anyone who thinks the North has it all figured out.

BAND 10 — THIS WEEK IN HISTORY HISTORY · DATED

On 28 August 1963 — 63 years ago this week — Martin Luther King Jr stepped to a lectern on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial and spoke to a crowd of about 250,000 people. It was the closing address of the March on Washington, and the speech he gave that afternoon is the one the world now knows by four words from its refrain: "I Have a Dream." They had come on a hot Wednesday to press for civil rights, and King's was the last of the day's speeches; most of the crowd would remember only his.

What made it last was partly the size of the gathering — one of the largest political assemblies the country had ever seen — and partly that it was carried live on television, so the words reached far past the reflecting pool. The March itself had been months in the planning and drew people from every state, arriving by bus and train through the morning. He gave them a vision rather than a grievance, built to be remembered rather than argued with — and the phrase outlived the day, the decade, and nearly everyone who was there.

The same week carries two older events, accidents of nature and men. On 1 September 1859 the Earth was struck by the Carrington Event, the most intense geomagnetic storm on record. A vast eruption on the Sun swept over the planet and lit auroras so bright they were seen as far south as the Caribbean. The world of 1859 had almost nothing electrical to damage, but it had the telegraph, and the storm found it: lines threw sparks, operators took shocks, and some machines kept clattering out messages after their batteries were disconnected, running on the current the sky itself had induced. A storm of that scale today, on a planet wired end to end, is why space-weather forecasting exists.

And on 2 September 1666, a fire started in a baker's shop on Pudding Lane in the City of London. It was small at first, the kind of blaze a crowded medieval city saw often. But the summer had been dry, the houses were timber and packed tight, and a wind was up. The Great Fire of London burned for four days, gutting the old walled City and leaving tens of thousands homeless. What rose from the ash was a different London — brick where there had been wood, and, eventually, Wren's new St Paul's on the ruins of the old.

BAND 11 — THE DESK SERVICE

The Session

FITNESS · BLOCK 3, PROGRESSIVE OVERLOAD

Block 3 is a hypertrophy block run at maintenance-to-surplus — the job is to rebuild training volume after the holiday week and put size back on. A 2026 progressive-overload study found the answer plainly: lifters who kept adding stress produced +22.9% hypertrophy against +11.6% for those who held load and reps fixed — roughly double the growth from the same time under the bar. Turning up is not the variable. Progression is.

The free win: you don't have to chase heavier plates every session to get it. It keeps you inside the ~10–20 hard-sets per muscle per week band the hypertrophy literature converges on, and it lets a surplus block add stimulus without the joint cost of always going heavier. Full range of motion and controlled eccentrics stack more stimulus on top, for nothing.

The Word's Descent · Notice to Levy

late C16enters English — first, an arithmetic tableTARIFF

The Long Read this week is about the tariff — the return of a border tax to the front rank of statecraft, with 50% US duties on Canadian goods and dollar-for-dollar retaliation to follow. But the word for the thing has a quieter history. Pull the thread on tariff and it runs backward out of English, through Italian, and into Arabic, where it started life meaning something almost sweet: to make known.

The root is the Arabic verb 'arafa, "to know." From it comes ta'rif — a notification, a making-known, and by extension a list of fees, the posted schedule that tells you what a thing will cost. It is a merchant's word before it is a government's. From Arabic it was borrowed into Italian as tariffa, the Mediterranean traders' language, and from Italian it crossed into English in the late 16th century.

When it first arrived, tariff in English did not mean a customs duty at all. It meant an arithmetic table — a ready-reckoner, a printed grid of figures you consulted to look up a number without doing the sum yourself. Only later did the schedule of numbers narrow to one particular kind of schedule, the one nailed up at the port listing what the crown would take on incoming goods. The list of fees swallowed all the other lists.

So the word that now headlines a trade war between neighbours began as a note pinned up for everyone to read, from a verb that means "to know." There is a small irony in that, given how much of the modern argument turns on what is hidden — who actually pays, how much of the cost quietly passes to the shopper. The word, if you trust its origins, was built to be the opposite. If you want to keep tumbling, the entry at Etymonline lays out the descent in full, one borrowing at a time.
