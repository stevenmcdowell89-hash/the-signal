II

A World Walks Out for a Walk

On the last Sunday of July 1914, the people of London queued for the bank holiday at Waterloo and Liverpool Street. The papers were full of Ireland — the country had been on the edge of a four-province civil war since spring, and the Home Rule crisis was the lead. The Austrian declaration of war on Serbia, three days old, ran on the foreign pages. The cricket scores were larger.

One week later, by Tuesday 4 August, Britain was at war with Germany, the German Army was inside Belgium, Russia had begun mobilising more than four million men, and Paris was preparing to be evacuated. The change took twelve days from the assassination of an Austrian archduke in a town most British readers could not have placed on a map. None of the leaders who issued the declarations expected what they were starting. Most thought their armies would be home by Christmas. None of them were right.

Four years and three months later, when the last guns went silent in a railway carriage at Compiègne on the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month, around seventeen million people were dead. Four imperial dynasties had been dissolved or destroyed — the Hohenzollerns of Germany, the Habsburgs of Austria-Hungary, the Romanovs of Russia, the Ottomans. The world's largest creditor nation in 1914 was the United Kingdom. By 1918 it was the United States. The map of Europe east of the Rhine bore almost no resemblance to the map of Europe in July 1914. The map of the Middle East had been drawn from scratch.

This is the story of how a continental quarrel about Balkan succession became, in the space of fifty-one months, the event that ended the long European nineteenth century and started everything we now think of as the twentieth.

It is told here in three parts. The first describes the world that walked into the war: the six great powers as they actually were in summer 1914, the rising states around them, the colonial empires whose subjects would do much of the fighting, and the long fuses that had been smouldering for a generation before Sarajevo lit them. The second follows the war itself — not battle by battle, but as the six powers experienced it, and as the war shaped them in return. The third looks at what remained: the treaties, the new countries, what became of each of the original players, and the consequences that ran far past 1945.

The Great War has had a hundred and ten years of historians writing about it now. The orthodox British reading has been Germany-did-it (the Fischer Thesis of 1961, still the dominant view in most popular accounts). The most influential recent revision — Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers (2012) — argues responsibility was diffuse and the European order was always fragile. Niall Ferguson has argued Britain should have stayed out and let Germany win a continental war. This issue holds those debates lightly. The story of the war's causes matters; the magazine's interest is the story of what it actually did to the world that fought it.

Which brings us to the editor's actual take.

The Magazine's Take
III

It Did Not Change the World. It Ended One.

The magazine's take

The First World War did not modify the Europe of 1914. It abolished it — root and dynasty, map and idea — and everything the twentieth century became was a working-out of that destruction.

The standard reading of the war is a chapter in the history of conflict — bigger than 1870, smaller than 1939, the first of two world wars. This issue treats it differently. The war's most important achievement was the demolition of the political world that fought it: four hereditary empires gone, the world's leading creditor relegated, the white colonial certainty cracked, the moral case for monarchy emptied, and an entire intellectual confidence in linear human progress permanently broken. Bolshevism, fascism, the partition of the Middle East along borders that still hold, the slow death of European empires, the American century, the very idea of the nation-state as the default unit of sovereignty — these are not consequences of the war so much as expressions of its negative space. The 1914 world simply stopped being there. The rest is what filled the room.

The chapters that follow argue this by showing it. Act I describes the six powers as they actually were when they walked in. Act II watches the war eat the societies that started it. Act III walks back through the same six countries, names what they became — and traces the long shadow into a present that, more than a century on, still lives in the cathedral the war left behind.

Act I · The World in 1914
IV

The Six

In Vienna, in May 1914, the Emperor Franz Joseph turned eighty-three. He had ruled in unbroken succession since 1848 — sixty-six years. His personal valet had served him for fifty-five of those years. His desk routine — papers brought in at 5 a.m., signed by candlelight, returned by 7 — had not changed since the year Britain and France went to war in the Crimea. When ambassadors arrived to present credentials, the Emperor stood; foreign diplomats sometimes noticed that the chair behind him remained pushed in not by the staff but by a small mechanical brake fitted in the 1860s, because the chair was older than most countries represented in his court.

This was the world that walked into the war. Six great powers, almost all of them ruled by hereditary monarchies that traced their right to govern to a God whose decisions, by 1914, very few educated Europeans still believed in. Behind the dynasties stood elaborate machineries — armies, navies, foreign offices, bond markets, mass-circulation newspapers, electric trams, transatlantic cables — that the dynasties had not built and increasingly did not understand. They were the figureheads on a continent in violent industrial transformation; the figureheads still genuinely thought they were the captains.

Great Britain — the global landlord, distracted at home

The British Empire in 1914 was the largest political entity ever assembled by human beings. It governed around 412 million people across a quarter of the planet's land area — India alone with a population three times Britain's own. The Royal Navy was the unchallenged master of the world's oceans, and had been since Trafalgar in 1805. The pound sterling was the world's reserve currency; the City of London ran the global financial system; British coaling stations and submarine telegraph cables wrapped the globe.

And in the summer of 1914 the British political class was thinking about almost none of this. Asquith's Liberal government had spent the spring on the edge of armed conflict in Ireland — the Home Rule Bill had triggered the Curragh Mutiny in March, when British army officers in Ireland declared they would resign rather than coerce Ulster Unionists into a Dublin parliament. The Commons in late July was debating the partition of Ireland, not the partition of Europe. Edward Grey, the Foreign Secretary, had spent the previous fortnight trying to mediate between Vienna and Belgrade; he did not have a Cabinet majority for war until the German army crossed into Belgium on the morning of 4 August.

France — the wounded republic

France in 1914 was the world's third-largest economy and home to the largest empire after Britain. It was also a country that had not won a major European war since 1815 and carried the unhealed scar of the Franco-Prussian War of 1870-71 — when Bismarck's armies had defeated France in six weeks, marched through Paris, and detached Alsace and the German-speaking portion of Lorraine. Forty-three years on, the lost provinces still had their own statue on the Place de la Concorde, draped in black. Schoolchildren in the Third Republic learned a French geography that included two departments under foreign occupation.

Politically, the Republic was unstable in the manner that had become its custom — Viviani became Prime Minister in June 1914, the seventh man to hold the post since 1910. Raymond Poincaré, a Lorrainer by birth, was president; he had visited St Petersburg in mid-July to reaffirm the Franco-Russian alliance and was on a battleship in the Baltic when the Austrian ultimatum to Serbia arrived. The army was France's pride and political project — universal conscription, three-year service, the doctrine of l'offensive à outrance, the symbolic recapture of Alsace as the founding war aim.

Germany — newest, fastest, most resentful

The German Empire was forty-three years old in 1914. It was the youngest of the great powers, founded in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles in January 1871 after the same war that took Alsace-Lorraine from France. In the four decades since, it had become the fastest-growing industrial economy in the world; German steel output had overtaken British by 1893, German chemical and electrical industries dominated Europe, German universities were the global benchmark for research. Walther Rathenau ran AEG; Fritz Haber had synthesised ammonia from air the year before. In every measurable economic respect, Germany was a coming hegemon.

Politically the Reich was led by Kaiser Wilhelm II — grandson of Queen Victoria, personally erratic, fond of uniforms, prone to writing furious marginalia on diplomatic despatches at three in the morning. The Chancellor since 1909 was Theobald von Bethmann-Hollweg, a serious civilian who had inherited a state in which the General Staff under Helmuth von Moltke (nephew of the elder Moltke who had won 1870) increasingly set the agenda. The Reichstag had real powers over the budget but no power over the army; constitutionally, the Kaiser was supreme commander and the General Staff reported to him alone. The civilian government found out about the final mobilisation timetable in late July at roughly the same time as the rest of Europe.

Russia — the contradictory colossus

The Russian Empire in 1914 was vast almost beyond comprehension — 167 million people, eight time zones, the largest landmass under a single sovereignty since Genghis Khan. Its standing army of 1.4 million men was the largest in Europe; on full mobilisation it could put roughly five million troops in the field. It had the largest agricultural output on earth and was, in pre-war years, the world's largest grain exporter.

It was also the most internally fragile of the great powers. The Tsar, Nicholas II, had survived the 1905 revolution by conceding a parliament (the Duma) and then progressively neutering it. He ruled personally — refused to delegate, made appointments based on whom his German-born wife Alexandra trusted, and after 1912 increasingly took counsel from Grigori Rasputin, a Siberian holy man whose hold over the imperial family was already a national scandal. The peasantry had been emancipated from serfdom only in 1861. Industrial workers in St Petersburg and Moscow had been on the edge of mass strike action all spring. The Russian general staff knew it could mobilise the largest army in Europe; it also knew that another revolution would bring down the dynasty, and that defeat in a major war would probably trigger one.

Austria-Hungary — the museum that thought it was a country

The Habsburg dual monarchy was eleven nations governed as one. Austrians and Hungarians, Czechs and Slovaks, Poles and Ukrainians, Romanians, Croats, Slovenes, Serbs, Italians and Bosnian Muslims — fifty million subjects with no common language, divided between Vienna and Budapest by the 1867 Ausgleich compromise that had stabilised the empire for half a century by giving the Hungarians their own parliament and almost everything else they wanted. The army drilled in German; orders to the Hungarian regiments were translated. The currency was the gulden until 1892 and the krone thereafter; reform happened slowly, when it happened at all.

Franz Joseph's heir presumptive was his nephew, Franz Ferdinand — a complicated, prickly man who had nearly been removed from the succession for marrying a Czech countess of insufficient rank, and whose actual political project was a Triple Monarchy that would add a South Slav crown to the Austrian and Hungarian ones, defusing the dangerous Slav nationalism on the empire's southern edge. He was killed in Sarajevo on 28 June by a nineteen-year-old Bosnian Serb who saw the Triple Monarchy plan, correctly, as a fatal threat to the project of a greater Serbia. The empire's response — to use the assassination as pretext for the punitive war on Serbia it had been wanting since the Balkan Wars — was endorsed by a Vienna establishment that believed, again correctly, that the empire's days were numbered if Serbian agitation in Bosnia continued unanswered. They were not wrong about the threat. They were catastrophically wrong about the answer.

The Ottoman Empire — the sick man, looking for a doctor

Of the six powers, the Ottomans were the only one already in visible territorial decline. The empire had governed the heart of the eastern Mediterranean and the Arab world for four centuries; by 1914 it had lost Greece (1830), Egypt in all but name (1882), Bulgaria, Bosnia, and almost all its European territory in the Balkan Wars of 1912-13. Salonika, where Atatürk had been born, had been Ottoman in 1912 and was Greek by the time he came of fighting age.

Power in Constantinople sat with the Committee of Union and Progress — the Young Turks who had taken effective control in 1908 by forcing the restoration of the constitution. By 1914 the CUP triumvirate of Enver Pasha (war), Talaat Pasha (interior), and Cemal Pasha (navy) ran the empire; Sultan Mehmed V was a figurehead. Enver in particular had been impressed by Germany during a tour of duty in Berlin. He had been pushing for years for an alliance with one of the great powers — anyone who would help the empire modernise its army before the next set of vultures arrived. London had refused; Paris was uninterested. Berlin, in August 1914, said yes.

The Others

In Tokyo in early August 1914, the Japanese Foreign Minister, Katō Takaaki, summoned the British ambassador to ask what Britain would like Japan to do under the terms of the Anglo-Japanese Alliance signed twelve years earlier. The ambassador, slightly startled, suggested limited naval action against German raiders in the Pacific. Katō took the request, expanded it considerably, and within ten days Japan had declared war on Germany, dispatched a fleet to bottle up the German East Asia Squadron, and begun the siege of the German colony at Tsingtao on the Chinese coast.

The Tsingtao operation was over by November. Japan had taken its first piece of foreign-occupied territory in the Pacific and, more quietly, an excellent collection of German Pacific islands — the Marshalls, the Carolines, the Marianas. The war was eleven weeks old. Japan, the only non-European great power, had become one of the war's first net gainers without having to fight a European on European soil.

This is the second face of 1914 that gets less attention than the great-power story. Beyond the six dynasties there was a second tier of states whose decisions over the next four years would matter as much as anyone's — and beyond them, the colonial world, whose subjects would do roughly a quarter of the fighting and pay roughly none of the political price for which.

Italy — the alliance partner who wandered off

Italy was technically a member of the Triple Alliance with Germany and Austria-Hungary, a treaty signed in 1882 and renewed at intervals ever since. In practice it was the alliance member everyone knew would not show up. Italian and Austrian interests collided directly in the Adriatic: Italy claimed Trento and Trieste (still Austrian, still ethnically mixed but heavily Italian-speaking), and the Italian state since unification had defined itself partly against the Habsburgs whose armies it had spent the 1850s and 60s fighting. When Germany and Austria went to war in August 1914, the Italian government — led by the conservative Salandra — declared neutrality on the grounds that the alliance was defensive and Austria had not been attacked.

The Italians then spent nine months auctioning themselves to the highest bidder. The Triple Entente offered, in the secret Treaty of London signed 26 April 1915, Trento and Trieste, Dalmatia, parts of the Albanian coast, colonial concessions in Africa, and a share of Asia Minor. Italy declared war on Austria-Hungary in May 1915 — but, characteristically, did not declare war on Germany until August 1916. The Italian war was its own thing, fought in the Alps and the Isonzo Valley against an Austrian army that knew the ground; the Treaty of London promises would mostly be honoured at Versailles, but not the ones the Italians had wanted most.

The United States — the giant that wanted to be left alone

The United States in 1914 had the world's largest economy, the world's third-largest navy after Britain and Germany, a standing army of about 98,000 men (smaller than Belgium's), and a settled political tradition of refusing to entangle itself in European affairs. President Woodrow Wilson — a former Princeton professor in his second year of office — had inherited this isolationism and intended to maintain it. On 19 August 1914, two weeks into the war, he issued a formal proclamation of neutrality and asked Americans to be "neutral in fact as well as in name… impartial in thought as well as in action."

The neutrality was real but skewed. American factories supplied both sides — but the Royal Navy's blockade of Germany meant that, in practice, the supplies flowed overwhelmingly to Britain and France. By 1916 American banks had extended credit to the Allies on a scale (~$2 billion) that made an Allied defeat a direct American financial catastrophe. The Allies needed American materiel and money; the Germans needed to interdict it; the question of what the United States would do about that became, by early 1917, the question on which the war turned.

Serbia, Belgium, Bulgaria, Romania, Greece — the small fates

The countries the war would actually be fought through were small, predominantly agrarian, and almost entirely incapable of determining their own fate. Serbia had emerged from two Balkan Wars in 1912-13 doubled in size, exhausted, militarily exhausted, and ruled by Peter I — a king brought back to the throne by the same officers who had assassinated his predecessor in 1903. Its army of around 360,000 was good for its size, battle-hardened, and outnumbered roughly three to one along its northern border by the Austro-Hungarian forces that crossed the Danube in August.

Belgium was a constitutional monarchy of seven and a half million people whose perpetual neutrality had been guaranteed by the Treaty of London in 1839 — a document Bethmann-Hollweg famously called "a scrap of paper" to the British ambassador in the early hours of 4 August, as German troops were already crossing the frontier. King Albert I led the Belgian army personally, refused to surrender, and held the western strip of his country — the Yser sector, near the coast — for the entire war.

Bulgaria, Romania and Greece all began the war as neutrals. All three would be pulled in by 1916-17, drawn by territorial promises from whichever side they suspected of winning — Bulgaria with the Central Powers in 1915, Romania with the Entente in 1916, Greece with the Entente in 1917 after a constitutional crisis that effectively split the country. None of them got what they were promised.

The colonial world — most of the planet, almost none of the agency

In 1914, European powers governed approximately 84 per cent of the world's land surface. Britain alone ruled around 412 million colonial subjects; France ruled around 62 million; Belgium controlled the Congo, where the Leopoldian rubber economy had — by direct massacre, forced labour, and induced famine — killed perhaps ten million people in the previous three decades; Germany held Tanganyika, Namibia, Cameroon, Togo and the Pacific islands; the Netherlands held the Dutch East Indies; Portugal held Angola and Mozambique. The Spanish empire was effectively reduced to a coastal strip of Morocco. The Russian Empire was, on the Asian land mass, the largest colonial enterprise of all.

The war was a world war from week one because the empires were worldwide. German colonies in Africa were invaded by British, French, Belgian, South African and Portuguese forces; the East African campaign under the brilliant German commander Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck would tie down a quarter of a million Allied troops for the entire war and only end in November 1918, when his unbeaten army of mostly African Askari learned of the European armistice and laid down arms in Northern Rhodesia. Indian troops fought at Mons in October 1914, in Mesopotamia, in East Africa, in Gallipoli. Senegalese tirailleurs fought on the Western Front. Chinese labour battalions — perhaps 140,000 men — dug trenches and cleared the dead behind the British and French lines. Vietnamese conscripts served in France.

None of these soldiers, labourers or subject peoples voted on entering the war. Most of their countries would not be free to vote on much of anything until well after 1945. But the experience of dispatch, of seeing the white man bleed and die at the same rate as everyone else, of the French and British promising self-determination to Czechs and Poles while withholding it from Egyptians and Indians — this was an experience the colonial world would carry into the long process by which empires came apart. The war did not invent decolonisation. It planted the moral case in the lungs of a generation that would do most of the work.

Long Fuse, Short Spark

On the morning of Sunday 28 June 1914, Gavrilo Princip — nineteen years old, tubercular, a Bosnian Serb high-school student who had been turned away from the Serbian army the year before for being too short — was eating a sandwich outside Schiller's delicatessen on Franz-Josef Street in Sarajevo. The motorcade carrying Archduke Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie had passed by half an hour earlier; one of Princip's six co-conspirators had thrown a grenade, which had bounced off the back of the Archduke's car and detonated under the following vehicle. The Archduke had insisted on visiting the wounded in hospital. The new route should have taken the motorcade down the Appel Quay; the lead driver took the wrong turn into Franz-Josef Street and stopped to reverse, directly in front of Princip's sandwich.

The pistol was a Belgian-made Browning Model 1910. Princip fired twice from a distance of about five feet. The first bullet hit Sophie in the abdomen; the second hit the Archduke in the neck, severing the jugular. Both were dead within fifteen minutes. The assassin was wrestled to the ground, swallowed a cyanide capsule that failed to kill him, and was taken into custody. He would die in a Habsburg prison in April 1918, weighing eighty-eight pounds, of tuberculosis, two months before the empire he had helped to destroy began its own collapse.

The spark was Sarajevo. The fuse was thirty years long.

The long fuse — what had been waiting to burn

The structural pressures had been building since at least 1871. The unification of Germany under Prussian leadership had created a state at the heart of Europe larger, richer and more populous than France, and over the next four decades that state's industrial weight had reshaped every calculation in every European capital. Bismarck — Germany's first Chancellor, in power from 1871 to 1890 — had managed the imbalance through an elaborate alliance system designed to keep France isolated and Russia friendly. His successors, beginning with Wilhelm II's dismissal of him in 1890, dismantled that system within a decade. By 1907 the Triple Entente of Britain, France and Russia had formed in direct response.

Three specific pressure points kept the Entente tight. The first was the Anglo-German naval race. In 1906 the Royal Navy launched HMS Dreadnought, a battleship whose all-big-gun armament and steam-turbine speed made every prior capital ship obsolete overnight. Germany under Admiral Tirpitz had been building a fleet specifically to challenge Britain in the North Sea; the dreadnought reset reset the contest to zero. Britain ran the race harder, and by 1914 had twenty-two dreadnoughts to Germany's fifteen. The race had been the single largest source of Anglo-German hostility for a decade — and Britain, the historical balancer who had usually leaned against France, had been pushed into a de facto alliance with Paris precisely by the German threat at sea.

The second pressure point was Morocco. Twice — in 1905 and 1911 — Germany had tested the Anglo-French entente by manufacturing a colonial crisis in Morocco, gambling that London would not back Paris when nothing British was directly at stake. Both times London did. The 1911 Agadir Crisis ended with a French protectorate over Morocco and a German consolation prize in Cameroon; what mattered more was that Britain and France emerged with their entente hardened from a paper agreement into something approaching a war plan. The Anglo-French naval conversations of 1912 — never quite a treaty — committed the Royal Navy to the North Sea and the French Mediterranean fleet to the Mediterranean, on the implicit understanding that the other would cover one's own coast.

The third pressure point was the Balkans. The Ottoman retreat from Europe across the nineteenth century had left a tier of new nation-states — Greece, Serbia, Bulgaria, Romania, Montenegro — competing for the dying empire's last European provinces. The Balkan Wars of 1912-13 had been the next-to-last act: in October 1912 the Balkan League (Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece, Montenegro) attacked Ottoman Europe and stripped it of almost everything west of Constantinople. In June 1913 the winners turned on each other; Serbia doubled in size, Bulgaria came out worst, and Austria-Hungary watched its great southern rival become twice as dangerous overnight. Vienna's reaction was hardening across 1913: when the next chance came to crush Serbia, Vienna would take it.

The war plans — promised solutions, designed to misfire

Every great power's general staff entered 1914 with a war plan. None of these plans worked as designed; the planners' assumptions about speed, distance, and the durability of the opposition were systematically wrong; and the plans, taken together, made any local crisis a continental one because each plan's success required the others to fail.

The German plan was the most ambitious. Originally drafted by the chief of the General Staff Count Alfred von Schlieffen and modified by Moltke the Younger after 1906, it tried to solve Germany's geographic problem — a two-front war against France in the west and Russia in the east — by knocking out France in six weeks and then transferring the army by rail to face Russia. The mechanism was a vast right-wheel through neutral Belgium and Luxembourg, enveloping Paris from the north-west, and ending the war in the west by Day 42. The plan's first sentence — "The whole of Germany must throw itself on France" — was its operating assumption. It required Belgian non-resistance, French passivity in Lorraine, and Russian slowness to mobilise. None of these would hold.

France's Plan XVII — adopted in 1913 — was the mirror image: at the first sign of German mobilisation, every available French division would attack into Alsace-Lorraine to recover the lost provinces. The doctrine was l'offensive à outrance: élan and bayonet over firepower and entrenchment. In the first weeks of August 1914, twenty-seven thousand French soldiers would die in a single day on the Lorraine frontier — the bloodiest day in French military history before the Somme. Russia's Plan 19 anticipated a slow mobilisation and a defensive war in the east; in the event it would be French pressure that forced Russia into the disastrous early invasion of East Prussia that ended at Tannenberg. Britain had no continental war plan at all — the Expeditionary Force was an instrument of imperial defence, not European intervention. Its commitment to deploy to France in support of the Belgian frontier was a Cabinet decision made on 6 August, two days after the war began.

Who is to blame?

The question has been argued for a century and a hundred and ten years, with the centre of gravity moving back and forth across each generation of historians. The 1919 Versailles consensus, encoded in Article 231 of the treaty, placed all responsibility on Germany. After the Second World War a generation of "shared blame" historians — Sidney Fay, Luigi Albertini — moved the responsibility around, weighing all six powers' mobilisation decisions and finding fault widely distributed. In 1961 the German historian Fritz Fischer published Griff nach der Weltmacht, arguing on the basis of newly opened German archives that the imperial government had pursued a programme of continental hegemony for years and had used the July Crisis to launch it. The Fischer Thesis dominated for fifty years. In 2012, Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers swung the consensus back: Clark argued that the European order in 1914 was structurally unstable and that all six great powers walked into the war through a thicket of perceived threats they were each incapable of stepping back from. Niall Ferguson's The Pity of War (1998) added a separate counter-position: that British intervention was the catastrophic choice, that a German victory over France would have been bad but recoverable, and that a continental war Britain stayed out of would have been a vastly smaller event.

This issue's view: Germany's decision to issue the blank cheque on 5 July, knowing that doing so almost certainly meant a continental war, and the German General Staff's decision to make Russian mobilisation rather than direct hostility the casus belli, are the decisions that turned an Austro-Serbian war into a world one. That is not the same as saying Germany alone bore the cost of being right or wrong about it; the war's destruction was authored collectively by everyone who failed to take the off-ramps that were briefly available between 28 July and 4 August. But of the powers who could have stopped it, Berlin's was the cheapest "no" and the most consequential "yes."

Act II · The Conflict
VII

The War Each Country Wanted

In the second week of September 1914, a German cavalry patrol on the Marne river — about thirty miles north-east of Paris — was startled to find itself being shelled by French artillery fired from the windows of taxis. A few hours later one of its officers was captured. He was carrying maps, marked with intended billets, that placed the German command in the Élysée Palace by the end of the month. The Schlieffen Plan's clock had been running thirty-eight days, and the German right wing was still pressing forward; one more push and the war in the west would be effectively over.

The push didn't come. The French Sixth Army, commanded by Joseph Gallieni, attacked the exposed German flank from Paris using whatever transport was available — including six hundred Renault taxis requisitioned from the Paris ranks and driven up to the line of battle with their meters running. The German First and Second Armies, exhausted and increasingly out of contact with each other, began to fall back. Within a week the line had stabilised along the Aisne river. By Christmas it would run from the Channel coast to the Swiss frontier — 475 miles of trenches that would barely move for three and a half years.

This is the war as everyone discovered it, rather than the war as each side had designed it. Across 1914 and 1915 the original war goals — clean, ambitious, victorious — survived contact with reality for about six weeks. After that, every belligerent had to invent a new war.

Germany had drafted a continental hegemony in September 1914 — a private war-aims memorandum from Chancellor Bethmann-Hollweg's office known to history as the September Programme, written when victory still seemed nine weeks away. Belgium would become a vassal state; France would lose the iron and coal fields of Briey and Longwy and accept a punitive indemnity; a German-led customs union would absorb the Low Countries, Scandinavia, Austria-Hungary, Poland and possibly France itself; central Europe would be reorganised as Mitteleuropa under Berlin's economic direction. The September Programme is the clearest single window into what Germany was actually fighting for; the Fischer Thesis cited it heavily, because nothing else in the early war so explicitly states the imperial ambition.

France's war aim was geographically modest and emotionally enormous: recover Alsace-Lorraine, contain Germany permanently, never again face a Franco-Prussian War. Britain's war aims were the oldest in European history: preserve Belgian neutrality, prevent any single power from dominating the Channel coast, protect imperial communications. Russia's were imperial — control of the Dardanelles to give the Russian Black Sea fleet access to the Mediterranean, an Anglo-Russian agreement (the Constantinople Agreement of March 1915) explicitly promised the Russians Constantinople and the Straits at the war's end. Austria-Hungary's war aim was the punitive destruction of Serbia and the preservation of the dual monarchy. The Ottoman war aim — once Enver Pasha had committed to the Central Powers in November 1914 — was to recover Egypt, push back the Russian advance in the Caucasus, and reassert Ottoman influence over the Arab and Persian worlds.

By spring 1916 not one of these aims looked recoverable. Belgium was occupied; Alsace-Lorraine was unreached; Egypt remained British; Constantinople was still Ottoman; Serbia had been overrun; and most of the war's surface had become a static trench line in which any territorial gain cost more lives than the land was worth. The war became, in industrial language, a contest of attrition: not who could win, but who could outlast.

The Eastern Front — fluid, vast, ruinous

The war in the east was a different war. Distances were too large for continuous trench lines; the armies were too big for any one front to absorb them all; the front line moved hundreds of miles a year. In late August 1914, two Russian armies under Samsonov and Rennenkampf invaded East Prussia faster than the Schlieffen Plan had assumed Russia could mobilise. They were destroyed at Tannenberg (26-30 August 1914) by a smaller German force under Hindenburg and Ludendorff in one of the most complete operational annihilations in modern military history — around 92,000 Russians captured, perhaps 30,000 more killed or wounded. Samsonov shot himself in the forest after the battle. Tannenberg made Hindenburg the most famous soldier in Germany; by 1916 he and Ludendorff would effectively be running the war.

The east see-sawed for the next two years. Russia did badly against Germany, well against Austria-Hungary, devastatingly to its own infrastructure on both fronts. The Brusilov Offensive of June 1916 — the most successful Russian operation of the war — nearly broke Austria-Hungary entirely and forced the Germans to divert troops east, weakening their position at Verdun and the Somme. It also cost the Russian army around 500,000 casualties, accelerating the collapse of morale that would feed into the revolution of the following March.

Gallipoli — the war's first imperial graveyard

In early 1915 Winston Churchill, then First Lord of the Admiralty, persuaded the War Council to attempt the forcing of the Dardanelles — the narrow waterway between the Aegean and the Sea of Marmara. The strategic logic was elegant: open the straits, take Constantinople, knock the Ottomans out of the war, restore the Russian supply route through the Black Sea. The execution was the war's most thorough catastrophe of operational ambition outrunning logistical reality.

The naval attempt failed in March when mines sank three Allied battleships in a morning. The land attempt — beginning with the landings of 25 April 1915 — pitted under-resourced British, French, Australian, New Zealand and Indian forces against an Ottoman army that, once committed, fought as well as any in the war and was commanded at corps level by a divisional colonel named Mustafa Kemal. Kemal's tactical defence of the Sari Bair ridges and his rallying counter-attacks made his career and saved the campaign for the Ottomans. The Allied evacuation in December 1915 and January 1916 — coordinated by Charles Monro and General Birdwood — was the only part of the operation that went genuinely well. Casualties: about 46,000 Allied dead, around 56,000 Ottoman. Russia's isolation from Allied supply was sealed. The political career of Mustafa Kemal began.

Verdun and the Somme — the war's centre of gravity

From February to December 1916 the Western Front had two defining battles fought at the same time. At Verdun, the German Chief of Staff Erich von Falkenhayn launched an offensive specifically designed not to capture territory but to "bleed France white" — to draw the French army into a defensive battle for a symbolically irreplaceable fortress city and inflict casualties at a ratio that would eventually exhaust French manpower. The French commander Philippe Pétain, on taking over in late February, instituted the Voie Sacrée — a continuous one-way road that funnelled around three-quarters of the French army through Verdun on rotation, so that no division spent more than two weeks in the inferno at a stretch. The battle ran for 303 days. French casualties were around 377,000; German around 337,000. Falkenhayn lost his command in August.

The Somme, beginning 1 July 1916, was the British attempt to relieve Verdun by drawing German forces north — a plan that worked at appalling cost. On the first day the British army suffered around 57,000 casualties including 19,240 killed: the worst single day in British military history before or since. The battle ran until November; the maximum advance was about seven miles. British and Commonwealth casualties were around 420,000; French around 200,000; German around 465,000. The Somme is the battle that fixed the British cultural memory of the war into a fixed picture of futility, mass death, and prose-poetic protest — the work of Sassoon, Owen and Graves came directly out of it.

"We shall not flag or fail. We shall go on to the end. We shall fight in France."

— Lloyd George, December 1916, on taking office as Prime Minister to replace Asquith

By the end of 1916, every belligerent had quietly buried its August 1914 war aims and was fighting for survival, for the morale of its own population, and for the post-war territorial settlement it could still extract from the wreckage. The war had become the war it would end as.

Everything on the Altar

In Berlin in the winter of 1916-17, the ration of fresh meat for a working-class adult had dropped to about a quarter of pre-war consumption. The potato crop had failed; the Allied blockade had cut off imported fats and protein for two years; the substitute carbohydrate was the turnip — a crop normally grown for cattle. Across the city, sausages were being made from sawdust binder and turnip pulp. The winter became known in German memory as the Steckrübenwinter — the turnip winter. The civilian death toll attributable to malnutrition over the war would eventually rise to perhaps 750,000.

In London at the same moment, the Defence of the Realm Act — passed in August 1914 and steadily expanded — had grown into a system of state control unimaginable in the pre-war Liberal mind. The government could now requisition factories, set wages, ban whistling for taxis after dark, and shorten pub opening hours. By the spring of 1917 it was rationing bread, sugar and meat. The Treasury had effectively suspended the gold standard; the railway companies had been taken into government control; the munitions factories of the Midlands were running three shifts of women on lathes producing shells in numbers that would have seemed industrially impossible three years earlier.

This is what total war looked like once it stopped being a theory. Every belligerent had to mobilise resources at a scale no peacetime state had ever attempted; the war ate the societies that started it, and the societies came out the other end with different state-citizen contracts than the ones they had entered with.

Britain — the Liberal state remakes itself

Pre-war Britain had a tradition of voluntary military service unique among the European great powers; the new armies of 1914-15 were raised on Kitchener's appeal to volunteers, who came forward in numbers that made the British army of 1915 the largest in British history. The volunteer mechanism broke under the Somme. The Military Service Act of January 1916 introduced conscription for the first time in British history (Ireland was exempted, a fact that would matter); a second Act in May extended it to married men; by 1918 the upper age limit had been raised to fifty.

Women entered the workforce in unprecedented numbers — perhaps a million additional women in industrial employment by 1918, with the iconic case the munitions factories where the "munitionettes" worked twelve-hour shifts handling TNT that turned their skin yellow. The political case for women's suffrage, which the suffragettes had been making with increasing violence before 1914, became unanswerable once the war made women indispensable to military production. The Representation of the People Act 1918 enfranchised women over thirty who met a property qualification — partial, conditional, but the constitutional dam had broken.

The political crisis came in December 1916. Asquith's coalition government — formed in May 1915 to replace the dying Liberal-only ministry — had lost the confidence of Conservative backbenchers, the Northcliffe press, and finally of Lloyd George himself, then Munitions Secretary. On 7 December 1916 Lloyd George became Prime Minister of a coalition dominated by Conservatives and his own Liberals, with Labour participating; the Liberal Party split that began that week would prove permanent, and Britain's two-party system was structurally reshaped — Liberals slowly displaced as the main opposition by Labour across the 1920s. The wartime coalition would govern Britain until 1922.

Germany — the silent dictatorship

In August 1916, after a series of disasters that culminated in Romania's entry into the war on the Allied side, Kaiser Wilhelm II appointed Paul von Hindenburg as Chief of the General Staff and Erich Ludendorff as his First Quartermaster-General. The two had been the heroes of Tannenberg; they now became the effective rulers of Germany. The civilian Chancellor — Bethmann-Hollweg until July 1917, then a succession of weaker men — retained titular authority but conceded all major strategic and economic decisions to the army.

The Hindenburg Programme of late 1916 attempted to triple munitions output through a state-directed war economy of unprecedented coercion — civilian labour conscription under the Auxiliary Service Law of December 1916, requisition of raw materials, fixed prices, the conversion of consumer industries to military production. It succeeded in increasing output, but at the cost of accelerated inflation, severe shortages of consumer goods, and a deepening alienation of the urban working class from a regime they had not chosen.

The submarine question was decided in early 1917 by Hindenburg and Ludendorff over Bethmann-Hollweg's objections. The German High Seas Fleet had spent the war bottled up by the British blockade; submarines were the only weapon capable of cutting British supply. The decision to resume unrestricted submarine warfare — sinking neutral merchant ships, including American ones, without warning — was made in the conviction that Britain could be starved into surrender within six months. American intervention would, the German naval staff calculated, come too late to matter. They were wrong about how long it would take to starve Britain. They were wrong about how fast the United States could mobilise.

France — mutiny and Pétain

In April 1917 the new French Commander-in-Chief Robert Nivelle launched an offensive on the Chemin des Dames ridge that had been promised as the war-winning breakthrough. It was a catastrophe of advertising — the French army had been told the breakthrough was certain; instead it suffered around 187,000 casualties for an advance measured in yards. The reaction was the most serious mutiny in modern French military history. Across May and June 1917, mutinous demonstrations affected somewhere between half and two-thirds of all French divisions; soldiers refused to attack but agreed to hold their positions, expressing a clear war-weariness through specific operational refusal rather than desertion.

The man brought in to restore the army was Philippe Pétain — the hero of Verdun, methodical, cynical, deeply suspicious of grand offensives. Pétain conducted a series of trials (about 3,400 men convicted, 554 sentenced to death, around 49 actually executed) but he combined the punishment with reforms: longer leave, better food, regular rotation out of the line. Most importantly, he simply stopped attacking. From mid-1917 to early 1918 the French army's role on the Western Front was largely defensive while it rebuilt. The mutinies were a near-disaster the German army did not detect in time to exploit; they were also the moment the French state learned what its army would and would not tolerate.

Russia — the empire that left the war

The Tsarist regime did not survive 1917. The February Revolution — actually March in the Western calendar, the Russians still using the Julian — began with food riots in Petrograd that the garrison was ordered to suppress and refused. Nicholas II abdicated on 15 March, ending three hundred and four years of Romanov rule. The Provisional Government that followed — under successively Prince Lvov and Alexander Kerensky — made the fatal decision to continue the war, on the dual grounds of Allied obligation and national honour. The Kerensky Offensive of July 1917 was the last attempt to remake the Russian army; it collapsed within days.

Lenin returned to Russia in April 1917 aboard a sealed German train — Berlin's calculation being that introducing a militant defeatist into Russian politics would accelerate the collapse of the eastern front. (This was, from a strictly military perspective, one of the most successful covert operations in history. It was also why Stalin would spend the next thirty years denying the entire transit.) The Bolshevik seizure of power on 7 November 1917 was tactical opportunism, ideological commitment, and the first viable peace platform a Russian government had presented since 1914. The new regime's first foreign policy act was the unilateral armistice with the Central Powers.

The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk signed on 3 March 1918 was the largest territorial transfer in European history to that point. Russia ceded Poland, the Baltic states, Finland, Ukraine and parts of the Caucasus — roughly a third of its European population, half its industry, three-quarters of its iron ore. Lenin accepted these terms because the Bolshevik calculation was that the German revolution was imminent and the terms would not matter; he was wrong about the timing, eventually right that the terms would not be enforced. Russia was out of the war.

America — the giant arrives, slowly, and then all at once

The United States declared war on Germany on 6 April 1917. The proximate causes were two: the German resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare on 1 February (with the Lusitania still recent memory from May 1915), and the Zimmermann Telegram — a German Foreign Office cable intercepted by British naval intelligence in January 1917 in which Foreign Secretary Arthur Zimmermann offered Mexico the recovery of Texas, New Mexico and Arizona in exchange for joining a war against the United States. The telegram's publication on 1 March collapsed the remaining American case for neutrality. Congress voted 82-6 in the Senate and 373-50 in the House for war on 6 April.

The American Expeditionary Force under John "Black Jack" Pershing landed in France in June 1917 — fewer than 15,000 troops in the initial deployment, against a German army of over four million. By December the AEF was about 175,000 strong. By March 1918, when the German Spring Offensive began, the AEF was finally large enough to matter — and after that, the build-up accelerated to about 10,000 fresh American troops arriving in France every day. By November 1918 there were two million Americans in France. The war had been turning, in terms of materiel and finance, since 1916; it now began turning in manpower as well. President Wilson's Fourteen Points speech of 8 January 1918 — the framework for what he would call "peace without victory" — set the moral terms on which Berlin would eventually try to negotiate. The Allies tolerated the speech because they needed the American army.

"The world must be made safe for democracy."

— Woodrow Wilson, address to Congress requesting a declaration of war on Germany, 2 April 1917

Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman empire — the empires the war was eating

Franz Joseph died on 21 November 1916, aged eighty-six, after a reign of sixty-eight years and his successor — his great-nephew, Charles — inherited an empire that was running out of options on every dimension. Across 1917 Charles attempted secret peace negotiations with the French via his brother-in-law Prince Sixtus of Bourbon-Parma — the Sixtus Affair — offering the return of Alsace-Lorraine in a kind of side-deal that would have ended the Habsburgs' war without Berlin's knowledge. The negotiations leaked in April 1918; Charles was publicly humiliated; Germany imposed political and military control over what was left of Habsburg agency.

Inside the Ottoman empire, the same year saw the first industrially organised genocide of the modern era. The Armenian population of eastern Anatolia — perhaps 1.5-2 million people in 1914 — was systematically deported, starved, shot and forced on death marches across 1915-16 by direct order of the CUP government in Constantinople, with estimates of the dead ranging from 600,000 to 1.5 million. The genocide was reported in real time by American, German and Allied diplomats; it was condemned by the Allies in a joint declaration of May 1915 that used, for the first time in diplomatic history, the phrase "crimes against humanity." It was also the empire's most thorough single act of self-destruction, hollowing out a population that had been central to Ottoman trade, finance and industry. The Republic of Turkey would inherit the genocide as a foundational silence; the historical reckoning has been a century in coming and is incomplete still.

The Arab Revolt began in June 1916. Hussein bin Ali, the Sharif of Mecca, declared independence from Ottoman rule with British encouragement and modest British support. The revolt was militarily peripheral — the Hejaz Railway was the principal target — but politically immense: it gave Britain a pretext for the post-war settlement that would dissolve four hundred years of Ottoman rule over the Arab world. The young British liaison officer T.E. Lawrence wrote his account of the revolt across the 1920s, eventually as Seven Pillars of Wisdom; the historical reality was both more squalid and more consequential than the romance.

By the autumn of 1917 every belligerent except the United States was either visibly cracking, exhausted, or — in the case of Russia and Austria-Hungary — close to dissolution. The question for 1918 would not be whether the war could end, but in what order each side's house would fall down.

1918: The House Falls

On the morning of 21 March 1918, the German army on the Western Front began the largest barrage in the history of land warfare. Six thousand guns opened fire along a fifty-mile front in Picardy at 4:40 a.m. and continued without pause for five hours. The shells included around two and a half million rounds of gas — phosgene, chlorine and the new mustard agent — designed to incapacitate British defenders before the infantry assault. When the storm troops moved forward at 9:40 a.m., they advanced four and a half miles by nightfall, taking ground the Allied armies had not held since the first weeks of the war. By the end of the first week the Germans had pushed the British Fifth Army back forty miles and were closer to Paris than at any point since 1914.

This was Operation Michael — the first and largest of five great German offensives planned across the spring and summer of 1918. The strategic calculation was straightforward: defeat Britain and France before American weight could become decisive. With Russia knocked out of the war, around fifty German divisions had been transferred from east to west; Ludendorff had a numerical superiority on the Western Front for the first and last time in the war. The question was whether the German army could end the war in 1918 before the AEF arrived in force.

The answer was no, but it was very close to yes. Across March, April, May, June and July 1918, the German army inflicted around 700,000 Allied casualties and advanced more ground than at any point in the previous three years — and they did all of this with a strategy that, on close examination, contained no actual war-winning objective. Ludendorff's reply to questions about what each offensive's strategic aim was — "We chop a hole. The rest follows" — captured the problem. The German army was no longer fighting for something; it was fighting against time. And it ran out.

The Spring Offensives — gambling and losing

Operation Michael ground to a halt by 5 April, having advanced about forty miles but failed to take Amiens or split the British and French armies. Operation Georgette in Flanders, launched 9 April, similarly threatened the Channel ports without taking them. Operation Blücher-Yorck on the Aisne in late May reached the Marne again — the closest German troops had been to Paris since 1914, and triggered the deployment of the first major American formation, the 2nd Division, to halt the advance at Belleau Wood and Château-Thierry across June. Operation Gneisenau in early June and the second Battle of the Marne in mid-July were progressively smaller and less successful.

The cumulative German casualty count was around 688,000. The reserves were gone. The horses that pulled the artillery — already a chronic shortage — were dying of malnutrition. Most importantly, the offensives had reduced the German army's elite storm-trooper formations to skeletons; the men who had broken the Allied line in March were dead or wounded by July, and there was no second wave of equivalent training. When the Allied counter-attack began on 18 July at Villers-Cotterêts — supported by Renault FT tanks, a new generation of light, fast, two-man tanks built in numbers Germany could not match — the German army began a retreat that would not stop until 11 November.

The Hundred Days — the Allied counter-attack that ended the war

On 8 August 1918, the British Fourth Army under Henry Rawlinson launched the Battle of Amiens. Four hundred and fifty Mark V tanks, surprise, and a smoothly coordinated combined-arms attack pushed the German line back seven miles in a single day and captured around 13,000 prisoners. Ludendorff called 8 August "the black day of the German army" — and he wrote, in his post-war memoirs, that it was on that day he realised the war could no longer be won. Five thousand German troops surrendered to the British during the morning, an unprecedented number. The Allied counter-offensive, which would become known as the Hundred Days, was now under way.

Marshal Ferdinand Foch — who had been appointed Allied Supreme Commander on 26 March in the panic of the Spring Offensives — orchestrated a sequence of rolling attacks that gave the Germans no opportunity to consolidate. The British armies under Haig (Plumer at Second, Horne at First, Byng at Third, Rawlinson at Fourth) pushed methodically through the Hindenburg Line in late September; the French and Americans drove east through the Meuse-Argonne; the Belgians under King Albert advanced along the coast. The German army was retreating in good order, but it was retreating, and behind it the political structure of Germany was beginning to crack.

The collapse — Bulgaria, the Ottomans, Austria-Hungary, Germany

The first imperial pillar to fall was the weakest. Bulgaria — which had joined the Central Powers in October 1915 — collapsed first under Allied pressure from Salonika. The Vardar Offensive, launched 15 September 1918 by a Franco-Serbian force under Louis Franchet d'Espèrey, broke through the Bulgarian line in two days. King Ferdinand abdicated; the new government signed the Armistice of Salonika on 29 September 1918. Bulgaria was out, leaving the Central Powers without a southern flank.

The Ottoman Empire followed within weeks. British and Arab forces under Edmund Allenby and Faisal had taken Damascus on 1 October; Ottoman armies in Mesopotamia were collapsing under British pressure; the empire's only ally was now isolated. The Armistice of Mudros — signed on 30 October 1918 aboard HMS Agamemnon in the harbour at Lemnos — surrendered the Ottoman armed forces, opened the Dardanelles to Allied warships, and allowed Allied occupation of any "strategic point" the Allies designated. Constantinople was occupied within weeks.

Austria-Hungary disintegrated from within rather than from defeat in the field. Across mid-to-late October the constituent nationalities simply walked out. Czechoslovakia declared independence on 28 October. The South Slav state that would become Yugoslavia was proclaimed on 29 October. Hungary declared independence on 31 October. The Italian army won the Battle of Vittorio Veneto in early November against an army that was already disintegrating; the Armistice of Villa Giusti was signed on 3 November 1918. The Habsburg empire, having survived six and a half centuries, ceased to exist as a political entity within five weeks.

Germany was the last to fall. The end began on 28 October at the naval base of Wilhelmshaven, where sailors of the High Seas Fleet — ordered to put to sea for a final glorious engagement with the British Grand Fleet they knew would be the death of them — refused to weigh anchor. The mutiny spread to Kiel within forty-eight hours; from Kiel it spread inland; within a week revolutionary councils — workers' and soldiers' soviets on the Russian model — controlled most major German cities. On 9 November Prince Max von Baden, the last Imperial Chancellor, announced the abdication of the Kaiser before the Kaiser had actually agreed to it. Wilhelm II fled to the Netherlands that night and would never see Germany again. A German Republic was proclaimed in Berlin the same afternoon — twice, separately, by two different revolutionary factions standing fifty metres apart on the Reichstag balcony.

The armistice — 11 November 1918, 11 a.m.

The German armistice delegation crossed the line under white flags on 7 November near La Capelle. They were taken by car to Compiègne, north of Paris, where Marshal Foch had set up a temporary headquarters in a railway carriage on a siding in the forest. The negotiations were brief — Foch presented terms (immediate evacuation of all occupied territory, surrender of the High Seas Fleet to internment, withdrawal behind the Rhine, surrender of artillery and railway rolling stock, abrogation of the Brest-Litovsk treaty, continuation of the Allied blockade) and the German delegation, led by the civilian Matthias Erzberger, had no authority to negotiate them. They signed at 5:10 a.m. on 11 November 1918; the armistice took effect six hours later, at 11 a.m.

The fighting continued in places to the last minute — the AEF in particular suffered around 11,000 casualties on the final day, attacking right up to the deadline under orders that have been argued about by historians ever since. Then the guns stopped. At Mons, where the British army had fired its first shots of the war in August 1914, the British were back, and a Canadian division was in the suburbs at 11 a.m. The line in November 1918 was almost exactly where the line had been in November 1914. Around ten million soldiers had died in the four years it took to put it back.

Russia was not at Compiègne. The Bolshevik government had been at peace with the Central Powers since March; it was now fighting a civil war of its own against White Russian forces, Czech Legion troops, and Allied expeditionary contingents from Britain, France, the United States, Japan and a half-dozen other countries. The Russian Civil War would run until 1922 and would kill, by famine and battle and Red and White terror, somewhere between seven and twelve million more people. The Bolshevik state that emerged from it would be the war's most consequential survivor.

"This is not peace; this is an armistice for twenty years."

— Marshal Ferdinand Foch, on first reading the terms of the Treaty of Versailles, June 1919

Foch's line was off by sixty-five days. Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939 — twenty years, nine months, and twenty-one days after the armistice his railway carriage had ended the previous war. The world he had said could not last lasted exactly as long as he had said it would.

Five Treaties, One New Map

On 28 June 1919 — five years to the day after Gavrilo Princip shot Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo — two German civilians named Hermann Müller and Johannes Bell signed the Treaty of Versailles in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles. The same hall, in January 1871, had hosted the proclamation of the German Empire after Bismarck's defeat of France. The selection of the venue was Georges Clemenceau's; the symmetry was deliberate; nothing about the choice was lost on anyone present. Müller would die of natural causes in 1931; Bell would survive to be arrested by the SS in 1933 and die in 1949. The treaty they signed would be repudiated by Adolf Hitler within fourteen years.

The Paris Peace Conference had opened on 18 January 1919 — also chosen for symbolism, the forty-eighth anniversary of the proclamation of the German Empire — with thirty-two nations attending. None of the defeated were present. Russia, which had lost more men than any other belligerent, was absent: the Bolshevik government had not been recognised. The German colonies were not represented at all. The peoples whose territory was being redrawn — the Czechs and Slovaks, the Poles, the South Slavs, the Arabs — were present mostly as petitioners. The conference was, at its working centre, an Anglo-French-American negotiation about what would happen to the world.

The Big Four, the four cities, the five treaties

The decisions ran through four men: Woodrow Wilson for the United States, David Lloyd George for Britain, Georges Clemenceau for France, Vittorio Emanuele Orlando for Italy. Wilson came with his Fourteen Points, with his moral certainty, and with his fatal political weakness — a Republican-controlled Senate at home who would not ratify what he was negotiating in Paris. Lloyd George had won the December 1918 "khaki election" on a platform that included "Hang the Kaiser" and "Make Germany pay" but understood the British strategic interest in not breaking Germany so thoroughly that France gained continental hegemony. Clemenceau wanted, with a clarity uncomplicated by any other consideration, French security against any future German aggression: dismemberment of Germany if possible, isolation and disarmament if not. Orlando wanted the Adriatic — the territory Italy had been promised in the secret Treaty of London — and walked out of the conference on 24 April 1919 when he could not have Fiume, an issue the Big Four would never resolve before the war's end.

Five treaties closed the war, one per defeated power. Each is named for a Paris suburb.

Versailles — the treaty everyone has heard of

The Treaty of Versailles was the long one and the one that mattered to subsequent history. Its territorial terms returned Alsace-Lorraine to France, transferred the Saar coal basin to French economic exploitation for fifteen years, gave Belgium the small districts of Eupen and Malmedy, gave Denmark northern Schleswig after a plebiscite, gave Poland West Prussia and Posen (the "Polish Corridor" that separated East Prussia from the rest of Germany), and made Danzig — overwhelmingly German-speaking — a Free City under League of Nations administration. Germany lost about thirteen per cent of its pre-war land area and about ten per cent of its population.

The military terms reduced the German army to 100,000 men, abolished the General Staff, banned conscription, banned an air force, banned submarines and capital ships, and demilitarised the Rhineland — German territory but no military presence permitted within fifty kilometres of the Rhine's east bank. The colonial empire was dissolved entirely, the German colonies divided among the victors as League of Nations "mandates" (a polite formula for transfer of sovereignty).

The two terms that became politically explosive were Article 231 and the reparations question. Article 231 — known to history as the "war guilt clause" — stated that Germany "accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage" of the war. The clause was technically a legal device required to underpin reparations liability; politically, in Germany, it was read as a moral verdict and rejected by every German government across the 1920s. The actual reparations figure was deferred — the conference could not agree on a number — and finalised by the Reparations Commission in May 1921 at 132 billion gold marks (about $33 billion at 1921 exchange rates, or about $500 billion in 2026 money). The figure was renegotiated downward by the Dawes Plan (1924) and the Young Plan (1929), suspended by the Hoover Moratorium (1931), and effectively abandoned at Lausanne in 1932. Germany paid, in net real terms, around an eighth of the original demand. The political damage of the demand itself proved far greater than the financial damage of the payments.

John Maynard Keynes — who had attended the conference as a British Treasury official and resigned in protest at the terms — published The Economic Consequences of the Peace in December 1919, arguing that the reparations were economically impossible, that the territorial settlement was politically unsustainable, and that the treaty was "an outrage against the spirit of the modern world." The book became a bestseller and became the orthodox British and American reading of the treaty for the next two decades. The orthodox French reading was almost the opposite — that the treaty had been far too soft because the French security demands of 1919 (a permanent military occupation of the Rhineland, the detachment of the Rhenish provinces, an Anglo-American military guarantee against future German aggression) had been negotiated away by Wilson and Lloyd George. Both readings turned out to contain a great deal of truth.

Saint-Germain and Trianon — the dismemberment of Austria-Hungary

The dual monarchy had ceased to exist before the treaties — the constituent nationalities had declared independence in late October 1918. The treaties of Saint-Germain (with the new German-Austrian republic) and Trianon (with Hungary) ratified facts on the ground that had been settled by the new nation-states themselves. Austria, the new German-speaking rump of the empire, lost approximately sixty per cent of its pre-war territory and was forbidden by treaty from union (Anschluss) with Germany. The country that remained — about six and a half million people centred on Vienna, a metropolitan capital built for an empire of fifty million — would spend the interwar years in chronic economic and political crisis.

Hungary's reduction was more severe. The Treaty of Trianon, signed under coercion after the brief Hungarian Soviet Republic of 1919 had been suppressed, cost Hungary approximately seventy-two per cent of its pre-war territory and around sixty-four per cent of its population. Transylvania, with its sizeable Hungarian minority, went to Romania; Slovakia and Carpathian Ruthenia went to the new state of Czechoslovakia; Croatia, Slovenia and parts of southern Hungary went to the new Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (later Yugoslavia). The Hungarian state that emerged was about a third of its pre-war size with millions of ethnic Hungarians stranded outside its new borders — a wound that would drive Hungarian foreign policy for the next twenty-five years and that has still not psychologically closed.

The new successor states — Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, the reborn Poland, an enlarged Romania, the three Baltic republics — were the structural fact that emerged from the dissolution of the German, Habsburg and Russian empires in central and eastern Europe. They were created on the principle of national self-determination but they were not, in practice, nation-states; almost every one of them contained substantial minorities (Germans in Czechoslovakia and Poland, Hungarians in Romania and Czechoslovakia, multiple nationalities in Yugoslavia) who had not chosen the new arrangements and would, across the 1930s, be progressively weaponised by Nazi Germany as pretexts for territorial revision.

Sèvres, Lausanne, and the Middle East

The Treaty of Sèvres, signed with the Ottoman government on 10 August 1920, was the most ambitious territorial settlement of the entire post-war process. It dismembered the empire entirely — Constantinople reduced to an international zone, eastern Anatolia divided into Armenian and Kurdish states, southern Anatolia under Italian and French spheres of influence, the Aegean coast around Smyrna under Greek administration, the Arab provinces transferred to British and French "mandates" under the League of Nations. The treaty was signed by an Ottoman government in occupied Constantinople that no longer represented any real authority on the ground.

The Turkish War of Independence — fought between 1919 and 1923 by a nationalist movement based in Ankara under Mustafa Kemal — made the Sèvres terms unenforceable. Kemal defeated the Armenians in the east (1920), the French in Cilicia (1921), and the Greeks in western Anatolia (1922) in the most consequential reversal of a post-war settlement of the entire interwar period. The Treaty of Lausanne, signed on 24 July 1923, recognised the Republic of Turkey within roughly the modern borders, abolished the foreign capitulations, returned eastern Thrace, and imposed a massive population exchange between Greece and Turkey that displaced around 1.5 million Greeks and around 500,000 Turks. Lausanne is the only treaty of the post-war settlement that was negotiated, signed, and held — and it held because it had been written by the victors of a war that the European powers had not realised they were also losing.

The Arab world's settlement was the legacy of the Sykes-Picot Agreement — a secret deal between Britain and France signed in May 1916 and revealed by the Bolsheviks in November 1917 from the Tsarist Foreign Ministry archives. Britain took Iraq, Palestine and Transjordan as League mandates; France took Syria and Lebanon. The Hashemite princes whom Britain had encouraged to lead the Arab Revolt — promised an Arab kingdom in the McMahon-Hussein correspondence of 1915-16 — received consolation thrones: Faisal in Iraq, Abdullah in Transjordan, with Hussein himself eventually expelled from the Hejaz by Ibn Saud in 1925. The Balfour Declaration of November 1917 — committing Britain to a "national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine — added a third, irreconcilable promise to the contradictory commitments of the war years.

The borders the British and French foreign ministries drew on maps in Paris between 1919 and 1923 are still the borders that exist today, with very few alterations. The Iraq-Syria border that ISIS bulldozed in 2014 was a Sykes-Picot line. Lebanon's borders are the borders Henri Gouraud carved out of French Syria in 1920. Israel and Jordan emerged from the Palestine mandate. The hundred-year history of Western intervention, civil war, refugee crisis and political failure that has run across the Middle East since the early 1920s has been working through the consequences of a peace settlement that imposed European nation-state geometry on a region the Europeans did not understand and to which they made promises they could not all keep.

The League of Nations — Wilson's victory, Wilson's defeat

The League of Nations — established by the Treaty of Versailles and convened on 10 January 1920 — was Wilson's most significant achievement at Paris and the centrepiece of his Fourteen Points. It was designed as a permanent international institution to prevent future war through collective security, arbitration, and disarmament. It was, in important ways, the institutional ancestor of the United Nations and the entire postwar international order.

It was also the treaty's most visible failure. The United States — whose President had championed the League and whose participation was meant to anchor it — never joined. The US Senate, under Republican leader Henry Cabot Lodge, voted down ratification of the Treaty of Versailles in November 1919 and again in March 1920. Wilson had suffered a debilitating stroke in October 1919 while touring the country in support of the treaty; the negotiating position from the White House had hardened rather than softened in his illness; the Senate Republicans were determined that the League's Article 10 — committing members to defend each other against aggression — would not become an American constitutional obligation. The United States made a separate peace with Germany by treaty in August 1921. The League opened for business in Geneva without the country whose President had invented it. Across the 1930s — Manchuria, Abyssinia, the Rhineland, Spain, Czechoslovakia — its inability to constrain great-power aggression demonstrated, repeatedly, what a security architecture without American participation could and could not do.

Six Countries, After

It is worth, before the long-shadow chapter, going back to the six powers we met in Chapter IV and seeing what each of them became. The dynasties at the start of this issue had run for between four decades (Germany) and six and a half centuries (the Ottomans). Four were gone within four years. Of the four that survived as continuous political entities — Britain, France, Italy, and the smaller powers — only Britain and France had really won the war they had fought, and the cost of winning was substantial enough that even the victory is a complicated word to use.

Britain — the victor who could not afford the victory

Britain came out of the war the holder of the largest empire in history. The mandates of Iraq, Palestine, Transjordan, Tanganyika and the Cameroons added directly to British rule; Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and Canada were now self-governing dominions in everything but the foreign-policy details that the 1931 Statute of Westminster would shortly tidy up; the Royal Navy remained the world's first navy.

It was also bankrupt. The war had cost approximately £9 billion — about $40 billion at 1918 exchange rates — and around £1 billion of that had been borrowed from the United States, leaving Britain a net debtor for the first time since the Napoleonic Wars. Sterling had lost its position as the unchallenged world reserve currency to the dollar; the gold standard, abandoned in practice during the war, was restored at the pre-war parity in 1925 in a doomed attempt to recover the City of London's pre-war role — Keynes argued at the time the parity was overvalued and would crush British exports. He was correct, and the return to gold contributed substantially to the General Strike of 1926 and the long industrial depression of the late 1920s. Around 880,000 British soldiers and sailors had been killed; one in seven of those who served; the loss across the generation born between roughly 1885 and 1900 was permanent and the social pattern of the interwar British educated classes — the "lost generation" of Vera Brittain, Robert Graves, Wilfred Owen's "doomed youth" — was structurally determined by it.

The Irish question, which had been about to detonate in summer 1914 and which had been suspended for the duration, returned. The Easter Rising of 1916, the Conscription Crisis of 1918, and the Sinn Féin sweep of the December 1918 general election produced a unilateral declaration of Irish independence in January 1919 and the Anglo-Irish War of 1919-21. The Anglo-Irish Treaty of December 1921 created the Irish Free State (twenty-six counties) and partitioned the six counties of the north as Northern Ireland — the partition the European Union, the Good Friday Agreement of 1998, and the post-Brexit Northern Ireland Protocol have all subsequently inherited.

France — the victor who was already losing

France had recovered Alsace-Lorraine, had been on the winning side, had hosted the peace conference, had received the largest single share of German reparations. It had also lost around 1.4 million soldiers killed — about one in five of all mobilised men, the highest proportional military loss of any major belligerent. Around four million more were wounded, of whom around a million were permanently disabled. The northeast of France — Picardy, Champagne, the Ardennes — had been the war's main industrial battlefield and had been physically destroyed at a scale not seen in western Europe since the Thirty Years' War.

The demographic damage shaped French history for thirty years. The number of male births had collapsed during the war, producing a "hollow generation" coming of age in the late 1930s — exactly the cohort that would have been called up to face Germany in 1940. The political consequence was a defensive military doctrine that produced the Maginot Line, a foreign-policy orientation toward containing Germany by alliance with the new central European states, and an internal political fragility that saw an average French cabinet survive about eight months across the 1919-39 period. France in 1919 was the only country in continental Europe with an army still capable of beating Germany. By 1936 the demographic and political conditions for that capability had already eroded; by 1940 it was gone.

Germany — the Weimar Republic and the long defeat

The German Republic that had been hastily proclaimed in Berlin on 9 November 1918 inherited a state in physical collapse, an economy already deeply distorted by four years of war and blockade, a political culture in which the new democratic institutions were rejected by the parties of the right (as creatures of the Treaty of Versailles) and the parties of the far left (as a betrayal of the workers' revolution that had been within reach in winter 1918-19). The constitution drafted at Weimar in summer 1919 was technically the most advanced democratic instrument of its era — universal suffrage including women, proportional representation, federal structure — and provided the procedural cover for political violence the new state could not contain.

The 1923 inflation was the war's longest economic shadow on the Republic. Germany had financed the war primarily by borrowing — domestic war loans plus money printing rather than the British and French model of higher taxation — and had emerged with a currency already substantially debased. The French occupation of the Ruhr in January 1923 (to enforce reparation deliveries) triggered a German policy of passive resistance financed by the printing press. The mark — which had stood at 4.2 to the US dollar in July 1914 — reached 4.2 trillion to the dollar in November 1923. Middle-class savings in life insurance, war bonds and pensions were destroyed within a few months. The currency was stabilised by the introduction of the Rentenmark in late 1923 and replaced by the Reichsmark in 1924, but the social memory of the inflation became one of the most powerful political facts in Weimar Germany — and the political beneficiaries, eventually, were the parties of the radical right.

The Dolchstoßlegende — the "stab in the back" — was the most consequential political myth of the interwar period. Spread from late 1918 onward by Hindenburg, Ludendorff, and successively by the parties of the German right, it held that the German army of 1918 had been undefeated in the field and had been betrayed by socialists, Jews and democratic politicians on the home front. It was false in every particular — the army had been defeated, was retreating in good order but defeated, and the November Revolution had followed military collapse rather than caused it. It was politically irresistible. It explained why the new Republic was illegitimate from the moment of its birth; it provided the moral architecture for everything that would follow.

Adolf Hitler — an Austrian-born corporal in the Bavarian army during the war, who had been temporarily blinded by mustard gas at the front in October 1918 and who heard about the armistice from a hospital chaplain — joined the German Workers' Party (the DAP, soon renamed NSDAP) in Munich in September 1919. His failed Beer Hall Putsch of November 1923 — a Bavarian attempt to march on Berlin in deliberate imitation of Mussolini's March on Rome the year before — collapsed within a day. He served thirteen months in Landsberg prison and used the time to dictate the first volume of Mein Kampf. The party he led was a fringe annoyance until the 1929 Depression and the systematic political crisis of 1930-32. The fact that it eventually came to power constitutionally, through electoral mechanisms designed at Weimar to make extremism harder, is one of the central political tragedies of the European twentieth century. None of it was inevitable. All of it was downstream of the war and the peace.

Russia — the empire that became the Soviet Union

The Russia that had walked out of the war in March 1918 spent the next four years in civil war. Between roughly 1918 and 1922, Red and White armies fought across European Russia, Siberia and the Caucasus while Allied expeditionary forces (British in Murmansk, Japanese and American in Vladivostok, French in the Crimea, the Czech Legion stranded along the Trans-Siberian Railway) intervened in modest but not negligible numbers on the White side. The Red Army under Trotsky won. The cost — military deaths, famine deaths (the 1921-22 Volga famine killed perhaps five million on its own), Red and White terror, displacement, disease — has been variously estimated at between seven and twelve million people. The Romanov family, including the four daughters and the haemophiliac heir Alexei, were murdered by Bolshevik militiamen in the cellar of a Yekaterinburg merchant's house on the night of 16-17 July 1918.

The Soviet Union was formally constituted on 30 December 1922 from the wreckage. Lenin died in January 1924 after a series of strokes. The succession struggle that followed — Stalin against Trotsky, against Bukharin, against Zinoviev, against everyone — was concluded by 1929 with Stalin in unchallenged control of a regime that would, across the first Five-Year Plan (1928-32) and the Great Terror (1936-38), industrialise the country at a pace and human cost without precedent. The country that emerged was a great power on a scale the Tsarist empire had never been — by 1939 the Soviet Union was the world's second-largest industrial economy, with an army that would, after very nearly being destroyed in summer 1941, eventually take Berlin. The Russia of 1917 had been a backward European empire. The Soviet Union of 1945 was a global superpower. The transformation is one of the war's largest unintended consequences.

Austria-Hungary — the empire that vanished

Of the six pre-war powers, the Habsburg empire is the one that simply ceased to exist. There is no successor state that claims continuity with Franz Joseph's empire the way the Soviet Union claimed continuity with Tsarist Russia or the Republic of Turkey claimed continuity (selectively) with the Ottomans. The Austria of the interwar period was a German-speaking rump of about six and a half million people with a capital built for an empire of fifty million — Vienna in the 1920s had unfinished government buildings designed for ministries it no longer had, palaces whose owners had been dispossessed, and an Austrian self-image that oscillated between calling itself "the rest of Austria" (Restösterreich) and quietly seeking the union with Germany the treaty forbade.

The Anschluss came in March 1938 — Hitler driving in triumphantly through the same Vienna where he had failed to enter the art academy thirty years earlier. The Austria-Hungary that the Sarajevo bullet had been pointed at, in the strict national sense, was already long dead by then. What had been lost was not the empire's territory — that had been redistributed among the successor states — but a particular kind of multi-national, multi-confessional, partly liberal polity that had absorbed Galician Jews and Slovak peasants and Italian Trieste merchants and Czech industrialists and Croat priests under a single dynastic loyalty. Whatever else the post-war central European order produced, it did not produce a viable replacement for that.

The Ottoman Empire — the most thorough reinvention

The Ottoman Empire's reinvention as the Republic of Turkey is, on its own terms, the most successful state transformation produced by the war. Mustafa Kemal — the divisional colonel who had saved the Gallipoli line in 1915 — led the nationalist movement that defeated the Greeks in western Anatolia (1922), forced the renegotiation of Sèvres into Lausanne (1923), abolished the sultanate (November 1922) and the caliphate (March 1924), declared the Republic (29 October 1923), replaced the Arabic script with the Latin alphabet (1928), gave women the vote (1934) ahead of France or Italy, secularised the constitution, and adopted in 1934 the surname Atatürk — "Father of the Turks" — under a law he had personally written requiring all Turkish citizens to adopt surnames.

The cost of the transformation included the Armenian genocide of 1915-16, which the new Republic inherited and refused to acknowledge; the population exchange with Greece in 1923 that displaced two million people; the systematic suppression of Kurdish identity that has produced a low-level civil war running from the 1920s to the present; the dissolution of the multinational character of Ottoman governance into a Turkish ethnic state. The republic that emerged was modern, secular, militarily competent, and Turkish in a way the Ottoman empire had emphatically not been. The Arab provinces — Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Palestine, the Hejaz — became, by treaty, the responsibility of others. The hundred-year history of those provinces has been a different story.

Six countries walked into the war. Two were destroyed (the Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires); two were transformed beyond recognition (Russia into the Soviet Union, Germany into a Republic and then, fourteen years later, into the Third Reich); two won the war and emerged less powerful than they had begun (Britain and France). No belligerent emerged stronger than it had been in 1914 except, by a margin growing rapidly through the 1920s, the United States — which had spent the war on the sidelines, lent the others most of the money, and discovered itself, in 1919, as the world's leading economy and political force in a way it had not previously appreciated. The American century began at Versailles.

The Long Shadow

In a graveyard near Broodseinde Ridge in Belgian Flanders, around twelve thousand identical white limestone headstones stand in arcs along a low rise of ground. Tyne Cot is the largest Commonwealth War Graves cemetery in the world; it holds the burials of soldiers killed in the third Battle of Ypres in autumn 1917, plus the names of around thirty-five thousand more who have no known grave at all and whose names are inscribed on the rear memorial wall. The cemetery was designed by Sir Herbert Baker in the 1920s; the headstones were carved to a single specification by Sir Edwin Lutyens and Sir Reginald Blomfield; the rule that every man buried in a Commonwealth war grave gets the same headstone regardless of rank — private next to brigadier — was set down by Fabian Ware of the Imperial War Graves Commission in 1917 and has held ever since.

It is one of the most architecturally complete monuments anyone has ever built to the dead of a single event. There are, by the Commonwealth War Graves Commission's count, around 956 such cemeteries in France and Belgium alone. Walking through them is one of the few ways still available to take the war's actual shape physically. They are everywhere. They are unreasonable.

This is the chapter for what came afterward, beyond the immediate aftermath. The war is everywhere in the twentieth century that followed; the closing chapters of this issue have already named most of the headline consequences. What remains is to draw out, in summary, the shadows that ran past 1945 and into the century we still live in.

The Soviet Union — and the Cold War that came out of it

The Soviet Union existed from 30 December 1922 to 26 December 1991 — sixty-nine years. Without the First World War, there is no Bolshevik Russia in 1917, no civil-war victory for Lenin and Trotsky, no industrial superpower under Stalin to face Hitler in 1941, no Red Army in Berlin in 1945, no Iron Curtain across central Europe from 1945, no Korea, no Vietnam (in the form we got it), no Cuban Missile Crisis, no Berlin Wall, no Reagan-Gorbachev summit, no 1989, no fifteen post-Soviet republics adjusting to independence in the 1990s.

The Cold War is a direct lineal descendant of the Russian Civil War of 1918-22 and the Allied intervention against the Bolsheviks. Stalin's foreign policy was shaped throughout by the conviction — well-documented in Soviet archives — that the capitalist powers would eventually invade the USSR a second time, as they had invaded the first time in 1918. Western Cold War policy was shaped by the conviction that the Soviet Union, having absorbed eastern Europe in 1945, would push further west. Both convictions were partly correct, mostly self-fulfilling, and entirely a working-out of an antagonism whose original detonation was the Bolshevik consolidation of power in 1917-22. The Russia of the Tsars had been a European great power in dialogue with the rest. The Soviet Union was a hostile counter-system. The transformation is one of the war's largest unintended consequences.

The Middle East — borders we are still living inside

The political map of the Middle East that exists in 2026 is in almost every detail the map that was drawn by Sykes, Picot, Balfour, the San Remo conference of 1920, and the British and French mandate administrators of the 1920s. Iraq's borders are Anglo-French borders. Syria and Lebanon are French divisions of what had been Ottoman Syria. Jordan is the territory the British carved out of the Palestine mandate for the Hashemite prince Abdullah in 1921. The state of Israel was made constitutionally possible by the Balfour Declaration of 1917 and politically inevitable by the post-Holocaust refugee crisis a generation later. Saudi Arabia was assembled by Ibn Saud out of the Arabian Peninsula across the 1920s in the absence of the Ottoman political order that had previously held the holy cities. Egypt became nominally independent in 1922 — but the British military presence on the canal continued until 1956. The Iraq-Syria border that ISIS bulldozed in 2014 was a Sykes-Picot line.

The hundred-year history of Western intervention in the region — the British in Iraq, the Suez Crisis, the Yom Kippur War, the Iran-Iraq War, the Gulf War, the post-2003 Iraq War, the Syrian civil war, the rise and fall of ISIS, the Israeli-Palestinian peace process — has been a process of repeatedly running into the contradictions of the post-1918 settlement and being unable to escape them. The First World War created the Middle East as a coherent political object, gave it borders that had no organic relation to the lived life inside them, and made promises to multiple constituencies that no settlement could simultaneously honour. We are still working through that.

The end of European supremacy

In 1914, European powers governed roughly eighty-four per cent of the world's land surface. By 1965 — fifty years later — the figure was below ten per cent. The end of European empire was the work of two generations, accelerated by the Second World War, completed across the 1950s and 1960s. But the moral and material conditions for that end were established by the First World War.

Materially, the European empires were financially exhausted by 1918. The colonial relationships had relied on a perceived metropolitan strength that the war's debts, casualties and demonstrated mutual destruction had undermined. The white European certainty — the assumption that European peoples were biologically, intellectually and morally entitled to rule the rest of the planet — could not survive the experience of having Europeans slaughter each other at industrial scale for four years over what colonial subjects had every right to call white tribal warfare. The 1919 Versailles application of national self-determination to the Czechs, Poles and South Slavs but not to Egyptians, Indians, Vietnamese or Koreans was registered by educated colonial intelligentsias as a moral failure that delegitimised the empires going forward.

Mahatma Gandhi returned from South Africa to India in 1915 and launched his first non-cooperation campaign in 1920; Egyptian protests against British rule began in 1919; Korean independence demonstrations against Japanese occupation began in March 1919, directly invoking Wilson's Fourteen Points; Ho Chi Minh — then a young Vietnamese cook working in Paris — submitted a petition to the Versailles peacemakers demanding Vietnamese rights, was ignored, and concluded that nationalist liberation would have to be sought through the Soviet route. Vietnam, India, Korea, Egypt and most of the rest of the colonial world would not be free until after the Second World War; but the political movements that secured their freedom mostly date their organised modern beginnings to the years immediately after 1918.

The American century

In 1914 the United States was a wealthy regional power that exported food and raw materials, ran a substantial trade surplus, and held very little foreign debt because the international financial system was sterling-denominated and ran through London. By 1918 the Allies owed the United States approximately $10 billion in war debt; the dollar had replaced sterling as the principal reserve currency for the next century; Wall Street had replaced the City of London as the world's leading capital market; and the President of the United States had set the agenda of European negotiations at Versailles in a way no previous American president had ever attempted.

The trajectory from there to the Marshall Plan, NATO, Bretton Woods, the dollar's role at the centre of the post-1945 international economic order, the Cold War contest between the two superpowers that came out of 1917-22, the American military bases that have ringed Eurasia for eighty years — all of this begins in 1917 with the American decision to enter the war and runs forward through Wilson's failed Versailles, the interwar isolation, and the second war's confirmation of what the first had begun. The American century — Henry Luce's phrase from a February 1941 Life magazine editorial — was given its budget in 1918 and its political mandate in 1945. The down-payment was the war.

Women's suffrage

Britain (women over thirty with a property qualification, Representation of the People Act 1918, extended to full equality in 1928); Germany and Austria (1918, under the new republics); the Soviet Union (1917, under the Bolsheviks); the United States (1920, Nineteenth Amendment); the Netherlands (1919); Sweden (1921). The political case for women's suffrage had been argued for half a century and had been making slow legislative progress before 1914. The war made the argument unanswerable: women had run the munitions factories, kept the railways, staffed the hospitals, taken over the agricultural and industrial labour of the absent men, and had thereby made the war effort possible. The case for excluding them from the franchise that had sent their husbands and sons to die in industrial trenches was politically defunct by 1918. The constitutional changes followed within months of the armistice in most western European countries.

Medicine, technology, cultural rupture

Medicine was transformed by industrial-scale battlefield trauma. Blood transfusion went from experimental procedure to standard surgical protocol; Harold Gillies at Sidcup pioneered the techniques of modern plastic and reconstructive surgery on facial wounds caused by shrapnel; Marie Curie ran a fleet of mobile X-ray units (the "petites Curies") along the French front, training over a hundred women radiographers; the diagnostic category of "shell shock" (later post-traumatic stress disorder) was named and recognised, with neither the diagnosis nor the treatment yet capable of doing much for the men who suffered from it. Aviation was transformed from novelty to industry — the Sopwith Camel, the Fokker Triplane, the SPAD XIII, the strategic bombing of London by Zeppelins and Gothas, the first aircraft carrier (HMS Argus, 1918). Radio went from naval-communications tool to mass medium across the 1920s in part because of the wartime industrial capacity that had been built around it.

The 1918-19 "Spanish" influenza pandemic killed an estimated fifty million people worldwide — more than the war itself — and was accelerated by the troop movements and demobilisation that ended the war. It belongs in the war's death toll though it never appears in the standard military figures. It hit the populations weakened by four years of food shortages, blockade, and industrial mobilisation; it killed disproportionately young adults of military age; it shaped post-war demographics in ways still being computed. The Covid-19 pandemic of 2020-22 was the next time a pandemic of comparable scale reached western consciousness, and the comparisons were drawn at the time precisely because the cultural memory of 1918-19 had so largely receded that the comparison felt alien.

Culturally, the war produced one of the most decisive ruptures in the history of the European intellectual tradition. T.S. Eliot's The Waste Land was published in 1922; Ezra Pound and Eliot together remade the technical and emotional grammar of English-language poetry around an aesthetic of fragmentation that the war's experience seemed to have authorised. Wilfred Owen and Siegfried Sassoon had created a body of English war poetry — almost all of it published posthumously or after the armistice — that became the central cultural memory of the war for British readers and that argued, against the propaganda of the home front, that the war had been a crime against the men who fought it. All Quiet on the Western Front — Erich Maria Remarque's novel of 1929 — sold a million and a half copies in its first year and made the same case for German readers. The German Expressionists, the surrealists in Paris, the Dadaists in Zurich, the Bauhaus in Weimar — all of them came out of the assertion that the pre-1914 European cultural confidence in linear progress, rational improvement, and the moral authority of the educated classes had been demonstrated to be fraudulent and had to be replaced.

The Christian churches lost moral authority across the same period that they could not entirely recover. The mainstream Protestant churches in Germany and Britain had blessed the war on both sides; the Pope had condemned it but had been unable to broker a settlement; the experience of clergy in chaplaincy services to massed industrial trench warfare produced, on both sides, a generation of theologians for whom the optimistic liberal Christianity of the late nineteenth century had to be substantially rebuilt or abandoned. The secularisation of European public life across the twentieth century has many causes; the war is one of the largest.

A coda

The First World War abolished the political world that had fought it. It destroyed four empires, redrew the map of two continents, killed an industrial generation, broke the European intellectual confidence in linear human progress, and bequeathed the next century an entirely different set of problems from the ones the pre-war world had been working on. The Cold War was the war's child. The modern Middle East is the war's drafting. The American superpower is the war's tenant. Decolonisation began in earnest in the war's aftermath. The Second World War was the consolidating sequel to the Treaty of Versailles. The European Union, founded in 1957 specifically to make another Franco-German war impossible, was the long penance.

And on a low rise in Belgian Flanders, beside a railway cutting where a generation of Australian, British, Canadian and New Zealand soldiers were buried in autumn 1917, the twelve thousand identical white headstones still stand in the arcs Sir Herbert Baker laid out for them in the 1920s — a piece of architecture against forgetting that has held against eleven decades of weather and that will, in all probability, hold for several more. It is one of the very few places in the developed world where the actual physical size of the catastrophe is still legible. Most of the rest of the war has subsided into the architecture of the century that came out of it. That is what this issue has been about.

Where the War Lives in Other Media

Eight ways to keep going — chosen because each makes the case for the war's continuing presence in a register this issue could not fully reach. Not a bibliography. A working list.

Podcast

Hardcore History — Blueprint for Armageddon

Dan Carlin · Episodes 50-55 (2013-14)

Six episodes, around twenty-five hours, dramatising the entire arc Sarajevo to armistice. Carlin is a populariser not a historian, and the show works because of the breathlessness of his delivery — he treats the war as the human disaster the documentary tradition has been trying for a hundred years to make us feel and almost never manages to.

TV

The Great War

BBC · 26 episodes · 1964

Made for the fiftieth anniversary, with extensive interviews of surviving veterans — many in their seventies, many never recorded again — and full access to the Imperial War Museum's photographic and film archive. The voice of the narration is Sir Michael Redgrave; the music is by Wilfred Josephs. Still the deepest single moving-image source on the war that exists, available in fragments on the BBC archive site.

Film

They Shall Not Grow Old

Peter Jackson · 2018 · 99 min

Jackson took black-and-white IWM combat footage, restored it frame by frame, colourised it, slowed the unnaturally accelerated frame rate to normal speed, and dubbed in lip-read dialogue performed by actors from the regions whose regiments are shown. The technical effect is shocking — the British infantry on the Somme suddenly look like young men rather than ghosts. The argument the film does not make explicitly but enacts physically: the war was not long ago. It happened to people who looked like us, and it happened recently enough that the photographs can still be brought back.

Film

All Quiet on the Western Front

Edward Berger · 2022 · 148 min (Netflix)

The first major German-language adaptation of Erich Maria Remarque's 1929 novel — the original 1930 Lewis Milestone version was an American production. Berger's film is the war from inside the army that lost it, and the final fifteen minutes — the armistice approaching in real time at the front while the negotiations close in the railway carriage at Compiègne — are one of the most devastating cinematic sequences on the war's end. Won Best International Feature at the 95th Academy Awards.

Film

1917

Sam Mendes · 2019 · 119 min

Composed as a single continuous shot following two British soldiers through a day of trench warfare in April 1917. The conceit is gimmick on the page and revelation on the screen — the film makes the trench landscape and the time-pressure of an actual operational mission tactile in a way no third-person war film can. Roger Deakins won the Oscar for cinematography; the script (co-written with Mendes) draws on Mendes's grandfather's wartime service as a runner.

Game

Battlefield 1

DICE / EA · 2016 · PC and consoles

A first-person shooter that surprised everyone by treating its subject matter with substantial dignity — a series of single-player "War Stories" that move between the Italian front, the Lawrence-of-Arabia theatre, an American tank crew at Cambrai, an Australian pilot in the Royal Flying Corps. The multiplayer is the usual blockbuster shooter; the campaign opens with a sequence in which the player is told, every few minutes, that the soldier they were controlling has died, and the perspective shifts to another. It is the closest the medium has come to acknowledging that the war's scale was not heroic.

Book

The Sleepwalkers: How Europe Went to War in 1914

Christopher Clark · 2012 · Allen Lane

The most important revisionist account of the war's outbreak published this century — the anchor for the counter-frame this issue gestured at but did not adopt. Clark argues that responsibility was diffuse and the European order in 1914 was structurally unstable in ways that made the war substantially over-determined. The issue's own frame — Germany made the decisive call — disagrees with Clark on emphasis but takes his structural argument seriously enough to find his book the place to start the disagreement.

Birdsong

Sebastian Faulks · 1993 · Hutchinson

The English novel that, more than any other late-twentieth-century work, made the trenches imaginable to a generation that did not know anyone who had been there. The middle section follows the British tunnellers under the Somme — the men who dug below the German lines to lay mines, working in clay, by candlelight, listening for German counter-tunnellers in the dark. The book is sometimes accused of sentimentality; the tunnels chapter is one of the most claustrophobic things a contemporary novel has done with the war.

Of the four imperial dynasties that fell in the war, only one — the Romanovs — was extinguished by violence. The Hohenzollerns, Habsburgs and Ottomans all abdicated. Kaiser Wilhelm II lived in Dutch exile until 1941; his death was reported by the Associated Press as "the passing of the last Hohenzollern emperor", a detail almost no obituary today would think to include.
