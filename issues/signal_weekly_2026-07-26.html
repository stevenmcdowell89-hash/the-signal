Andy Burnham has run the country for a week now. Nobody voted for him to — and the machinery ran anyway.

Britain's seventh leader in a decade took office on 20 July without a general election, and spent this week doing the plainest things a government does. The cabinet was finished on 23 July — John Healey to the Treasury, Ed Miliband to the Foreign Office, Rachel Reeves sacked. The ordinary machinery of government, running smoothly, under a man no ballot chose.

The rest of the week was the one everyone else lived. Formula 1 rolled into Budapest with the championship all but Kimi Antonelli's — he leads by 45 points, and the race is still to run this afternoon. Halo arrives on PlayStation on Tuesday, twenty-five years after it was built to sell a rival console. And a corroded lump of bronze pulled from a Greek shipwreck turned out, once the X-rays got into it, to have been a computer.

BAND 05 — THE LONG READ SIGNAL STRONG
TWO THOUSAND YEARS, ONE CRANK

The Greeks who built a cosmos in bronze

A lump of corroded metal hauled from a shipwreck in 1901 turned out to be a hand-cranked model of the sky — the oldest known computer in the world. It took the rest of us a century, and a particle-fine X-ray beam, to read a machine its makers could operate by turning a knob.

BY THE EDITOR · THE LONG READ · 26 JUL 2026

In 1901, sponge divers working a wreck off the small Greek island of Antikythera brought up a lump of corroded bronze about the size of a thick book. It had been on the seabed since a Roman-era cargo ship went down there, some time around the turn of the first century BCE. For years it sat in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens as a green, calcified puzzle. Then people worked out that the shapes fused inside it were gears — dozens of them — and that the object was a machine for modelling the heavens. It is now regarded as the oldest known analogue computer in the world, built roughly 150 to 100 BCE, more than a thousand years before anything of comparable complexity appears again anywhere on Earth.

FIG. 01 Fragment A, the largest surviving piece of the mechanism — most of the machine's gear-train, crushed and fused by two thousand years in seawater.National Archaeological Museum, Athens · via Wikimedia Commons

Strip away the corrosion and what the Greeks made becomes clear. The Antikythera Mechanism was a hand-cranked calculator built from interlocking bronze gearwheels. You turned a knob on the side, and the gear train did the arithmetic of the sky: pointers on its dials moved to show where the Sun and the Moon stood against the stars, and where the five planets known in antiquity — Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn — would be found. One turn of the handle advanced the whole model by a fixed span of time, and each body moved at its own correct rate, because the ratio of teeth between two meshing gears encoded the ratio between two astronomical cycles. The mathematics of the heavens was carried, quite literally, in the count of the teeth.

The cleverest single part answered a problem the Greeks understood well: the Moon does not travel at a steady pace. It runs faster at some points in its orbit and slower at others. The makers reproduced that variation with a pin-and-slot mechanism — one gear carrying a small pin that rode in a slot cut into a second gear, the two mounted slightly off-centre from one another, so that as the pair turned the output sped up and slowed down exactly as the real Moon does across a month. It is a piece of astronomy solved in metal, a physical device standing in for a mathematical wobble that would not be written down as an equation for many centuries.

The front face was not the whole machine. Where that display showed the cosmos, other dials tracked the long calendar cycles. The mechanism followed the Metonic cycle — the nineteen-year span over which the Moon's phases fall back into step with the solar year — and the Saros cycle, the roughly eighteen-year rhythm on which eclipses repeat. Read the Saros dial and you could say, years in advance, when the Sun or the Moon would be eclipsed. A device you could hold in two hands told you the state of the sky on a date that had not yet arrived. Nothing else from the ancient world does anything close to this, which is exactly why it has been so hard to believe.

What survives is only a fraction of it. The mechanism comes down to us as 82 fragments — about a third of the original object — including some thirty corroded bronze gearwheels. The largest piece, known as Fragment A, holds most of the surviving gear-train, its wheels crushed and welded into a solid mass by twenty centuries underwater. For most of the twentieth century that damage kept the machine unreadable. You could see that there were gears; you could not see how many there were, how they meshed, or what the assembly as a whole was built to compute. The object was plainly extraordinary and almost entirely mute.

FIG. 02 The reverse of Fragment A — the corroded bronze gearing that stayed unreadable until X-rays powerful enough to see through solid metal arrived.National Archaeological Museum, Athens · via Wikimedia Commons

Reading it took a century and a technology its makers could never have imagined. The internal structure was only decoded after 2005, when researchers turned microfocus X-ray computed tomography on the fragments — CT scanning fine enough to see straight through the bronze and map every buried tooth in three dimensions. The scans let scholars count the gears, measure their teeth one by one, and rebuild the trains that had been fused into a single block of metal. In March 2021 a team at University College London, led by the engineer and mathematician Tony Freeth, published the first complete model of the machine's front display — the "Cosmos" face that carried the Sun, the Moon and the planets together on one set of dials.

That test matters, because the mechanism came with its own instructions. Its plates are covered in thousands of tiny Greek letters — a description and a user's guide, engraved straight into the bronze. Any proposed reconstruction is only convincing if the gears it invents actually produce the astronomy the inscriptions promise. Freeth's model was the first to reconcile the two at once: the surviving hardware, and the words the makers themselves wrote across it. The machine and its manual finally agreed.

This is where the popular version of the story goes wrong. The Antikythera Mechanism is usually sold as an impossible object — a computer that fell out of the sky into the ancient world, a genius's leap so far ahead of its era that it has to be explained away. Alexander Jones, a historian of ancient science at New York University, argues the opposite in his book A Portable Cosmos. The device is not an anomaly. It is the one physical survivor of a Hellenistic tradition of geared instruments that ancient writers mention but that otherwise rotted away, melted down or was lost. Bronze corrodes, and bronze gets recycled. That we happen to have one such machine, dragged up by luck from a shipwreck, is no reason to think there was only ever one.

Jones reads the object's purpose more soberly than the legend does, too. He takes it to be a display and teaching instrument for an astronomically literate public — a way to show an educated layman how the cosmos ran — rather than a navigation aid for sailors or a working observatory. It modelled the sky; it did not steer ships. Seen that way, the mechanism becomes less a miracle and more a measure of how much ordinary Greek technical culture we have simply lost. The astonishing thing is not that one workshop could build this. It is that we have almost nothing else from all the workshops that plainly could.

THE CASE AGAINST · THE IMPOSSIBLE MACHINE

The wonder survives the myth being punctured.

The romantic reading insists the mechanism is out of time — proof that someone, somehow, vaulted a thousand years past everyone around them. The evidence points the other way. Its gearing is sophisticated but fully explicable: the product of a real Greek tradition of precision instrument-making, working on mathematical principles, and nothing inside it demands knowledge the Hellenistic world did not already have. It is true that nothing of comparable geared complexity appears again until the astronomical clocks of the medieval Islamic and European worlds, over a millennium later — but that gap is a story about what was lost and never rebuilt, not about ancient magic. Take the mysticism out and the real marvel gets sharper. Nothing impossible happened here. A Greek workshop two thousand years ago built this within the ordinary reach of its craft, and we have only just become clever enough to read what they made.

That is the quiet joke of the Antikythera Mechanism. Its makers could work it with one hand, turning a crank to run the heavens forward or back and read the answer off a dial. It took the rest of us two thousand years, a shipwreck, and a beam of X-rays fine enough to count teeth inside solid metal to work out what the crank actually did. The machine was never waiting to be understood by the future. It was already understood, completely, by the people who built it. We are the ones who had to catch up.

BAND 10 — THE DESK SERVICE

The Session

FITNESS · BLOCK 3, HYPERTROPHY

Two weeks off and the instinct is to pick up exactly where you left the barbell — same weights, same set counts, same session. Resist it, but not out of fear: the good news first. You have not lost what you think you have. Training builds extra nuclei inside muscle fibres, and those myonuclei stick around long after the fibre itself shrinks — which is why size and strength come back far faster the second time than they took to earn in the first place. That is the plain mechanism to hang the whole ramp on. You are reconditioning, not rebuilding, and your muscle remembers.

The catch is that tendons, ligaments and connective tissue detrain quicker and re-adapt slower than muscle does. After a layoff your muscles are ready to pull harder than your joints are ready to be pulled — and that gap is exactly where a tweak happens. So a sensible ramp protects the slow tissue while the fast tissue catches up: start about two sets below your pre-break working volume on each lift, then add roughly one set per movement per week back toward where you were. Keep a couple of reps in reserve rather than chasing old maxes in week one. Spend your first week's attention on the lifts that regressed most — usually the big compounds and anything single-leg — not the ones that held. And because Block 3 runs at a small surplus, the surplus only pays off if recovery keeps up: land the roughly 1.6g of protein per kg of bodyweight the muscle-building research settles on, and treat sleep as non-negotiable — most of the repair you buy in the kitchen is banked overnight.

DO THIS WEEK · THE SESSION

Cap every lift two sets under your pre-break working volume and log it. Next week, add one set per movement — no more — and hold the same restraint on load.

Muscle comes back fast because the nuclei never left; tendons and connective tissue re-adapt slower, so easing volume up week by week keeps the injury off the tissue that hasn't caught up yet.

The Ledger

MONEY · MORTGAGE RATES

The Rate War · As of 26 Jul

Next decision30 JUL

The Bank of England meets on Thursday 30 July, and the near-certain outcome is nothing. The base rate has sat at 3.75% since the 18 June vote — itself a tight 7–2 hold — and markets price roughly an 86% chance it stays there. Yet the deals landing in front of remortgagers have kept getting cheaper anyway. If you have been waiting for the Bank to cut before you move, you may be watching the wrong scoreboard.

Here is the mechanism. A lender's fixed-rate price is not set by today's base rate — it is set by where the money markets expect rates to sit over the next few years (the swap rates lenders pay to fund fixed loans) and, on top of that, by how hard each lender is fighting for business. When the big lenders decide they want more of the market, they trim margins and undercut each other, and fixed rates drift down independently of anything the committee does on the day. Competition, not the Bank, is doing most of the work right now. There is no single announcement to wait for — the good rate is the one on the table today, and a better one may or may not follow.

That turns the move from "wait and see" into "lock and watch." Most lenders let you reserve a new fixed rate up to about six months ahead of your current deal ending, and — the part that matters — if a cheaper rate appears before you complete, you can usually switch onto it. You bank a secured rate as your floor and keep the upside if the price war keeps running. It is about as close to a free option as this market offers.

The Word's Descent · Thread to Hint
OEOld English cliewen — a ball of thread or yarn, later clewa ball of thread
mythAriadne hands Theseus a ball of thread to unwind through the Labyrinththe way out

C19the detective story fixes the sense; the spelling settles clew → clueCLUE

To read the Antikythera Mechanism, the scientists at UCL had to trace a single thread of logic through a labyrinth of corroded bronze — following the teeth of one gearwheel to the next until the whole machine made sense. There is a word for exactly that act of following a thread out of a maze, and it turns out to be the most ordinary word we have for a hint. A clue. The word is a fossil of the very thing the Antikythera team were doing, and to open it up is to walk straight back into ancient Greek myth.

Start with the spelling that got sanded away. "Clue" was once "clew," and before that the Old English cliewen — a ball of thread or yarn, the kind you would wind for weaving. That is the whole original meaning: not a hint, not a sign, just a physical ball of wool. For most of the word's life it had nothing to do with mysteries at all. It meant thread, and only thread.

The leap from a ball of yarn to the thing that solves a puzzle runs through one story — the oldest maze in the Western imagination. Theseus goes into the Labyrinth on Crete to kill the Minotaur, and the real danger is not the monster but the maze: even if he wins, he will never find the way back out. Ariadne gives him a ball of thread — a clew — to tie at the entrance and unwind as he goes. He kills the Minotaur, then follows the thread back to the door. To "follow the clue," in the plainest and most literal sense, was to follow that unspooling line of yarn out of a place designed to trap you.

From there the meaning does what live metaphors do: it loosens. By around the 1620s a clew had become a guiding thread through any difficulty — any problem you could get lost inside — and the ball of wool started to fade behind the idea of guidance. The detective story finished the job, narrowing "a guiding thread through a mystery" down to the single hard fact that leads you to the answer: the fingerprint, the misplaced train ticket, the thing that solves the case. Somewhere in that shift the spelling settled too, clew hardening into clue. The Antikythera scholars followed a thread of gearing out of a labyrinth of rust — which is, etymologically, the only kind of clue there has ever been.
