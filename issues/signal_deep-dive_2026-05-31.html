The First Roman Emperors

How Rome Got an Emperor

Augustus won a civil war and turned himself into a monarchy that never admitted it was one. The story from Caesar's murder to Nero's suicide — and how a job with no name became an inheritance.

Rome never created an office called emperor. That, in the end, was the whole problem.

This issue answers one question. How did a man who won a civil war turn himself into a permanent ruler without ever calling himself a king — and how did that arrangement harden, within a single century, into a monarchy passed down inside one family?

The man is Augustus. The century belongs to his family, the Julio-Claudians: Augustus, then Tiberius, Caligula, Claudius and Nero, from 27 BC to AD 68. The issue follows the machinery of the change, not just the lives of the men who sat at its centre. Each emperor is here as evidence of how the system was working — and of how the polite fiction Augustus built was wearing thin.

That fiction is the spine of the whole story. Augustus did not abolish the Republic; he kept its offices, its Senate, its language, and quietly gathered the powers that mattered into his own hands. He called himself princeps — first citizen — and let everyone go on speaking as though the Republic had simply been repaired. It is a useful warning that almost everything lurid we think we know about his successors comes from later writers with senatorial sympathies and scores to settle. Where the ancient portrait and the modern reading diverge, this issue says so.

The magazine's take

Augustus dressed one-man rule as a restored Republic, and because the ruler was legally just a private citizen holding a stack of ordinary Republican powers, there was no lawful way to inherit a job that officially did not exist. So every reign after him improvised, the mask slipped, and by AD 68 Rome had a hereditary monarchy it had never admitted creating.

The disguise is the whole point. Augustus never called himself king. He called himself princeps, first citizen, and built his power out of pieces any Roman magistrate might hold: a tribune's power to propose and block laws, a proconsul's command over armies. Held permanently and all at once, those pieces added up to sole rule. That is what the frame buys us. It explains why the system worked and why it could not pass itself on. An office never legally created cannot be legally bequeathed. Each successor had to fake a transfer of powers that were, on paper, personal to one dead man, and each fake exposed a little more of the monarchy underneath.

This is also where the real argument among historians sits. Theodor Mommsen read the settlement as a genuine sharing of power between the princeps and the Senate, a new constitution with two partners. Ronald Syme read the Republican restoration as "never anything but a screen and a sham," a coup wearing the costume of a rescue. This issue sides with Syme, but it takes the screen seriously rather than waving it away, because the disguise is exactly what made one-man rule survive its inventor. A naked monarchy in 27 BC would have ended the way Caesar did. A monarchy that swore it was the Republic lasted, mutated, and finally dropped the costume only when an army went looking for the next emperor and found there were no rules about where to look.

IV

The Republic That Killed Itself

By the time Augustus appeared, one man holding all the power had become the only thing that could stop Romans from killing each other. The Roman Republic did not fall to a foreign army or a single ambitious tyrant. It broke along a fault line built into how its armies worked, and once that fault opened, every attempt to close it by force made the next round of force more likely. The story runs from a change in military pay through three civil wars to a single man left standing over the whole Roman world.

THE FAULT LINE

For most of the Republic, a Roman soldier was a property-owning citizen who served for a campaign and went home to his farm. He fought for Rome because Rome was his. That changed in the decades around 100 BC. The general Gaius Marius opened the legions to the poorest citizens, men with no land to return to. They served for years, and they looked to their commander, not the state, to reward them with pay and a plot of land at the end. The general became the paymaster. Loyalty followed the money.

That single shift turned armies into private followings with public weapons. The Republic had been built on the assumption that no individual could ever command enough loyal force to overawe the rest. Its offices were short, shared between colleagues, and held in check by the threat of prosecution once a man stepped down. All of that depended on soldiers who served the state and then went home. Once soldiers served a man and depended on him for their reward, the checks still existed on paper but had nothing real behind them.

The first man to show what this meant was Lucius Cornelius Sulla, who in 88 BC did something no Roman had done before: he marched his own legions on the city of Rome itself. His soldiers followed him against the government because their futures were tied to his. Sulla took the city, made himself dictator, posted lists of enemies to be hunted down, and then, strangely, retired. The precedent did not retire with him. After Sulla, every Roman commander knew the same thing his troops knew. An army loyal to its general could take Rome. The only question was who would do it next, and whether he would walk away as Sulla had.

CAESAR'S WAGER

Julius Caesar inherited that logic and pushed it to its end. He spent the 50s BC conquering Gaul at the head of legions that were devoted to him personally and made rich by his victories. When his political enemies in the Senate ordered him to give up his command and come home as a private citizen, he understood what that meant: prosecution, ruin, and the loss of everything his soldiers had won. In 49 BC he chose the other road and led his army across the Rubicon, the small river marking the boundary a general was forbidden to cross under arms. It was Sulla's march again, and it started a civil war that Caesar won.

Victory left Caesar with a problem the Republic had no answer for. He held the loyalty of the armies and the personal authority of a conqueror, but the Republic's offices were designed to be shared and temporary, exactly so that no one man could keep this kind of power. Caesar's solution was to make the temporary permanent. In early 44 BC he was named dictator perpetuo, dictator in perpetuity. The emergency office that was supposed to last six months was now his for life. To the men who still believed in the Republic, a lifetime dictatorship was a king in everything but the word.

So roughly sixty senators, led by Marcus Junius Brutus and Gaius Cassius Longinus, decided to kill him. On 15 March 44 BC, the Ides of March, they surrounded Caesar at a meeting of the Senate in the Theatre of Pompey and stabbed him about twenty-three times. They believed they were saving the Republic. They thought that removing the one man who had concentrated all power would let the old shared system breathe again.

THE MURDER BACKFIRES

They were wrong, and the way they were wrong is the heart of the matter. Killing the strongman did not bring back the Republic because the fault line was still there. The armies were still loyal to their paymasters, and Caesar's death simply meant new strongmen would now compete to inherit his soldiers, his money, and his name. The assassination did not end the danger. It auctioned it off. The conspirators had treated Caesar as the disease, when he was only the most successful symptom. Remove him, and the conditions that produced him produced more like him, faster and with fewer scruples.

Three men moved into the gap. Caesar's veteran lieutenant Mark Antony, his teenage grand-nephew and adopted heir Octavian, and his cavalry commander Lepidus formed an alliance to hunt down the assassins and divide the Roman world among themselves. This time the arrangement was not hidden behind Republican forms. On 27 November 43 BC a law, the Lex Titia, gave the three of them near-total power for five years. It was open, legal, joint dictatorship by committee, the Second Triumvirate.

Their first act was to pay for the coming war and remove their enemies in one stroke through proscription, the same tool Sulla had used. They posted lists of the condemned, confiscated their property, and paid bounties for their heads. Around three hundred senators and two thousand equestrians were marked for death. The triumvirs even traded their own friends and relatives onto the lists. Among the killed was Cicero, the Republic's most famous voice, who had attacked Antony in a series of speeches. On Antony's orders his hands and head were cut off and displayed in Rome. The man who had argued the Republic's case in public was silenced in the most literal way, and the gesture told everyone watching that argument was no longer how Rome settled anything.

In 42 BC the triumvirs hunted down the assassins themselves. At Philippi in northern Greece, the armies of Antony and Octavian defeated Brutus and Cassius, both of whom killed themselves. The cause Brutus and Cassius had murdered for, the restored Republic, died with them. What remained was three warlords and their legions, and three was an unstable number.

DOWN TO ONE

The triumvirate did what such arrangements do. It broke apart. Lepidus was the weakest and was pushed aside, stripped of his power and left with only a priesthood. That left Antony and Octavian, and they split the Roman world between them, Octavian holding the west from Italy, Antony holding the rich east, where he allied himself with Cleopatra, the queen of Egypt. For a decade they circled each other in a contest of propaganda and maneuver, each presenting the other as the threat to Rome, until the contest became a war.

It was settled at sea. On 2 September 31 BC, off the western coast of Greece near a promontory called Actium, Octavian's fleet faced the combined fleets of Antony and Cleopatra. Octavian himself was no admiral, but his friend Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa was, and Agrippa's smaller, faster ships outmaneuvered Antony's heavy warships. Cleopatra's squadron broke for open water and Antony followed her, abandoning the fight. The rest of his fleet surrendered, and his land army gave up about a week later. Antony and Cleopatra fled to Egypt and killed themselves there the following year. By 30 BC there was no rival left anywhere. Octavian controlled the entire Roman world, from Spain to Syria, with something on the order of fifty to sixty legions under arms, all of them ultimately answering to him.

He never let anyone forget how completely he had won. Decades later, in the account of his own deeds that he had inscribed on bronze and copied across the empire, he opened with the claim that he had made the world obey.

Below: The achievements of the deified Augustus, by which he made the whole world subject to the rule of the Roman people... At the age of nineteen, on my own initiative and at my own expense, I raised an army by means of which I restored liberty to the republic, which had been oppressed by the tyranny of a faction.

— Augustus, Res Gestae Divi Augusti

THE TRAP HE WON

Read that last line carefully, because it states the trap Octavian was now caught in. He says he restored liberty to the Republic. He had in fact done the opposite. He had ended a century of civil war by winning it, which meant he held the kind of total power that the Republic existed to prevent. And he had a fresh, recent lesson in what Rome did to a man who held that power and looked like a king. His own adoptive father had been the most successful Roman who ever lived, had concentrated everything in his own hands, had been declared dictator for life, and had been stabbed to death on the Senate floor by men who told themselves they were patriots. The corpse was barely fourteen years cold.

This was the real problem facing the master of the Roman world in 30 BC. The armies would not go back to being the state's. The fault line that had destroyed the Republic was still open, and only one man holding the whole army could hold it shut. But that man could not call himself what he was. Power won by the sword could only be kept by a ruler who did not look like a ruler. Octavian had won everything Caesar had won, and he had watched exactly how that winning got Caesar killed.

V

Inventing the Princeps

In 30 BC Octavian held the Roman world by force. Antony and Cleopatra were dead, his rivals were gone, and the legions answered to him alone. The problem was that everyone could see this, and recent history told him exactly where naked power led. His adoptive father, Julius Caesar, had taken a lifetime dictatorship and the public manners of a king, and a group of senators had stabbed him to death for it. Octavian had won the war. Now he had to win the peace, which meant solving a harder puzzle: how to keep everything while appearing to give it back. The system he built to do that is the Principate, and it is the most successful political disguise in Western history.

THE FIRST SETTLEMENT, 27 BC

On 13 January 27 BC, Octavian stood before the Senate and handed the Republic back. He gave up his extraordinary powers and announced that he was returning the state to the Senate and the Roman people. This was the performance, and it was meant to be seen as one. The Senate, in turn, begged him to stay on and pressed an enormous package of authority on him: command of a single vast province covering most of the frontier, granted for ten years, and a new honorific name, Augustus, a word with religious weight meaning something like "the revered one." He had been Octavian, the warlord; now he was Augustus, the man who had saved the state and given it back.

The trick lay in which provinces he kept. The empire was split into two kinds. The imperial provinces — the ones with the armies on them, on the Rhine, in Syria, in Spain — stayed under Augustus, governed by deputies he chose. The peaceful, long-settled provinces, where no legions were needed, stayed with the Senate, governed in the old way. So the restoration was real on paper and hollow in fact. The Senate got the quiet places. Augustus kept every province where soldiers stood. Whoever commands the army commands Rome, and the army was his.

THE SECOND SETTLEMENT, 23 BC

The first arrangement still left a clumsy seam. To stay at the center of government, Augustus had been getting himself elected consul, the top magistracy, year after year. There were only two consulships a year, so holding one permanently blocked an ambitious senator from a prize the elite expected to take turns at, and it made his dominance look too much like an office he simply never vacated. In 23 BC he fixed this with a second settlement that traded the consulship for two permanent powers he could hold without holding any office at all.

The first was imperium proconsulare maius, "greater proconsular command." A proconsul was a provincial governor with military authority. Augustus took a version of that authority defined as maius, greater, meaning it outranked the command of every other governor in every province, including the senatorial ones he had publicly handed back. He could now give orders anywhere in the empire and be obeyed, without being the local governor, because his command was simply larger than anyone else's.

The second was tribunicia potestas, the tribune's power. In the old Republic the tribunes were officials who protected ordinary citizens, and the office carried three sharp tools: the right to propose laws, the right to veto the acts of other officials, and personal sacrosanctity, meaning it was a religious crime to lay a hand on a tribune. Augustus took all three of those powers as a permanent grant — but not the office. He was not a tribune. He held the tribune's power without being a tribune, which let him steer legislation, block anything he disliked, and be legally untouchable, all while leaving the actual magistracy open for other men to fill. These two ordinary Republican powers, held together and for life, were the real legal foundation of his rule. He counted his reign by them: each year he renewed the tribunician power and numbered it, so that the renewals became, in effect, the regnal years of a king who insisted he was not one.

THE THREE THINGS THAT ACTUALLY MATTERED

Strip away the legal language and the regime rested on three foundations. The first was the army. Augustus commanded the legions and the frontier provinces where they were stationed, so the only organized force in the Roman world looked to him. The second was the tribune's power, which gave him control over civilian Rome — the laws, the vetoes, the protected person at the center of the city. The third was money. He controlled the state treasury, and he held Egypt as a personal possession rather than a normal province, governing it through an officer of his own and forbidding senators even to enter it without his leave. Egypt's grain fed Rome, which meant the bread supply of the capital ran through Augustus. Armies, laws, and money: hold all three and the constitutional theater can say whatever it likes.

AUCTORITAS AND POTESTAS

Augustus described his own position with surprising honesty in the Res Gestae, the first-person record of his deeds that he had inscribed on bronze and copied in temples across the empire. He claimed that he held no more potestas — formal legal power — than any of his colleagues in office, but that he surpassed them all in auctoritas. The two words are the whole secret. Potestas is the power that comes with an office: a consul has it, a tribune has it, and it ends when the term ends. Auctoritas is prestige, weight, the kind of standing that makes people defer to you before you have said anything. Augustus was claiming that on paper he was just one magistrate among others, and that what set him above everyone was not extra power but extra authority. This was both a boast and a fairly accurate description of how the system worked. The legal pieces were ordinary; the man holding them was not. His prestige was the glue that held the whole arrangement together, and it was the one thing no successor could simply be voted.

I surpassed all in influence, although I possessed no more official power than the others who were my colleagues.

— Augustus, Res Gestae 34

That sentence opens with a plain claim and a quiet contradiction. He had no more power than his colleagues; he stood above all of them. Both halves were true at once, and the gap between them was exactly the space in which one man ruled an empire while swearing he had restored a republic.

THE ARMY MADE PERMANENT

The civil wars had left Rome with a swollen, improvised military — somewhere around fifty to sixty legions raised by rival warlords, paid by plunder and loyal to whoever led them. A standing force that large was both unaffordable and dangerous, since every general with legions was a potential rival. Augustus cut and standardized it. He reduced the legions to roughly twenty-eight permanent units, settled the discharged veterans in colonies, and turned soldiering into a profession with fixed terms of service rather than a temporary muster for a war.

The harder problem was pay. A soldier serves for years and then expects a reward at discharge, and in the late Republic that reward came from the general personally, which is precisely why armies followed generals against the state. Augustus broke that link. In AD 6 he founded the aerarium militare, a military treasury funded by new taxes, whose job was to pay the discharge bonuses of retiring soldiers. The state now paid the men off — but the state, in practice, was Augustus. Soldiers came to look to the regime, and to the man at its head, for their pensions. He also created the Praetorian Guard, a standing body of troops stationed in and around Italy as his personal force. No one else in the Roman world commanded soldiers near the capital. The Guard would, within a few decades, become the body that made and unmade emperors, but in Augustus's hands it was simply the armed proof that the center of power had a bodyguard and the Senate did not.

THE LIMIT OF THE SYSTEM

The army was an instrument of expansion as well as control, and there Augustus found his hard edge. In September AD 9, in the forests of Germany, the chieftain Arminius ambushed and destroyed three legions — the 17th, 18th, and 19th — under the governor Publius Quinctilius Varus. The defeat was total. The legion numbers were never used again, and the dream of conquering Germania died with them. Suetonius records that the aged emperor let his hair and beard grow for months and would sometimes beat his head against a door, crying out the name of his lost commander.

Quintilius Varus, give me back my legions!

— Suetonius, Divus Augustus 23

Teutoburg fixed the frontiers. After it, the Rhine became a permanent border rather than a line to advance past, and Augustus advised his successors to keep the empire within its existing bounds. The system that had made the army safe at home had also made it cautious abroad. A professional standing army with fixed pay and fixed terms was an instrument for holding ground, not endlessly taking more.

RELIGION AND IMAGE

Force and law held the empire; image made it feel natural. Augustus built his authority into religion, art, and the streets of Rome. He was already divi filius, "son of the deified one," because the Senate had declared the murdered Julius Caesar a god, which made his adopted heir the son of a god. In 12 BC he became pontifex maximus, the chief priest of the Roman state, putting the official religion under his control. In 2 BC he accepted the title Pater Patriae, "Father of the Fatherland," which cast him as the head of the whole Roman family. In the provinces, an imperial cult grew up around him during his lifetime; a temple to Roma and Augustus went up at Pergamum, and in the east the living emperor was worshipped, while at Rome the formal step of deification was held back until after his death.

He rebuilt the city to match. Augustus is supposed to have boasted that he found Rome a city of brick and left it a city of marble, and the building program behind the boast was real: new forums, restored temples, and monuments such as the Ara Pacis, the Altar of Augustan Peace, consecrated in 9 BC, whose carved processions of the imperial family turned dynastic politics into serene public art.

I found Rome a city of bricks and left it a city of marble.

— Suetonius, Divus Augustus 28

The message reached people who never saw a monument. His profile on the coinage carried his face and his titles into every market and barracks in the empire. Poets did the same work in words: Virgil's Aeneid, written by a member of the literary circle around Augustus's friend Maecenas, gave Rome a national epic that traced the emperor's family back to the gods and presented his peace as destiny fulfilled. Augustus reinforced the image with legislation as well, passing laws meant to revive marriage, punish adultery, and restore the moral order he claimed to embody. The statue, the coin, the poem, and the law all said the same thing: this was not a new regime but old Rome restored, with one revered man at its head.

The achievement, stated plainly, is this. Augustus made one-man rule feel like the Republic simply continuing. That is why it lasted, and why no one was ever forced to admit that a monarchy had been founded. He held the armies, the laws, and the money; he wrapped all three in Republican titles and religious prestige; and he ruled for more than forty years without once being called a king. But every one of these powers was personal. The greater command, the tribune's power, the chief priesthood, the control of Egypt, the loyalty of the soldiers who looked to him for their pay — all of it had been voted to Augustus, attached to Augustus, built on the auctoritas of Augustus. There was no office of emperor, because pretending there was none had been the entire point. Which left one question the genius of the design could not answer: when this man died, how did any of it pass to the next?

VI

The Unsolved Problem

There was something strange about the powers Augustus held: they were personal. The tribunician power and the great imperium over the provinces and armies were not an office that came with a building, a title-deed, or a line of succession. They were grants made by the Senate and people to one man, Gaius Julius Caesar Octavianus, for as long as he lived. When he died, they died with him. There was no crown to take off his head and place on another. There was no chair that the next ruler could sit in and thereby become ruler. The thing he had built had no slot for an heir, because in law it was not a thing at all. It was a description of what one citizen happened to be allowed to do. This chapter explains how Augustus turned that un-inheritable arrangement into an inheritance anyway, and how the method he improvised created a dynasty without ever creating a law that said dynasties were allowed.

THE TRAP

Start with the size of the problem, because it is easy to miss. A king solves succession with a rule: the crown passes to the eldest son, or to whoever the old king names, and the rule is the thing that carries authority across the gap left by a death. Rome had spent its whole history making sure it had no king and no such rule. Augustus kept it that way on purpose, because the word rex, king, was what had gotten his adoptive father Julius Caesar stabbed to death. So Augustus ruled as princeps, first citizen, a man who was formally just one senator among others, distinguished only by the powers the state had voted him. The disguise was the whole genius of the system. It also made succession impossible. You cannot hand down powers that were granted to you personally. The moment Augustus died, every legal basis for his rule would simply stop existing, and Rome would be left with a Senate, two consuls, and no one in charge of the armies. The system was designed to pretend that the position did not exist, which meant the position could not be passed on, which meant that on the founder's death the whole structure should have collapsed back into a leaderless Republic.

It did not collapse. The reason it did not is that Augustus found a way to build a successor by hand, out of the only materials he had.

THE METHOD

His method can be written as a recipe, because that is what it was: a sequence of steps he repeated whenever a candidate died and he had to start over. The steps are these.

First, pick a male relative. The pool was the extended family, by blood or by marriage, because a relative could be presented to Rome as a natural continuation of the founder rather than a rival to him. Second, marry that man into the bloodline, usually by marrying him to Augustus's daughter Julia, so that any children would carry Augustus's own descent. Third, adopt him as a son. Adoption in Rome was a formal, legally binding act that made an adult man a son completely, carrying the adopter's name and standing, as real in law as any birth. This is the step that turned a relative into an heir. Fourth, and this is the engine of the whole thing, have the Senate vote that man the same powers Augustus held, the tribunician power and a great imperium, while Augustus was still alive. The heir would hold these powers jointly with the founder for years before the founder died.

That fourth step is what made succession work, so it is worth being precise about why. If the heir already holds the powers when the old man dies, then nothing has to be transferred at the moment of death. There is no dangerous gap, no need for a new vote in the panic after a funeral, no question of whether a leaderless Senate would even agree to recreate the position. The handover is finished before it is needed. The heir does not become ruler when Augustus dies; he is already co-holder of every power that rule consists of, and he simply continues holding it. Augustus had taken an arrangement that legally could not be inherited and arranged for the inheritance to happen in advance, while he was alive to enforce it. The powers were still personal grants. He just made sure the next person already had his own set.

Look closely at the bloodline this produced and you find that it is engineered, not natural. Not one clean step from father to son exists anywhere in the chain. Every link is an adoption, a marriage, or both. The family that ruled Rome for almost a century called itself a dynasty and looked like one in its monuments and on its coins, but it was held together by legal acts and arranged marriages rather than by simple descent. The recipe manufactured the appearance of a bloodline out of relatives who were chosen, married in, and adopted into place.

THE SEARCH AND ITS HEARTBREAK

Augustus had to run the recipe again and again, because his chosen heirs kept dying before he did. The pattern is grim, and it is the best evidence that none of this was a settled plan. It was improvisation forced by repeated loss.

The first candidate was Marcellus, his nephew, married to his daughter Julia. Marcellus died in 23 BC, young, before he could be more than a hope. Augustus turned next to Marcus Agrippa, his oldest friend and the general who had won his wars at sea, and married Agrippa to the now-widowed Julia. Agrippa held real power alongside Augustus and looked like the answer. He died in 12 BC. Augustus then adopted the two sons Agrippa and Julia had produced, his own grandsons Gaius and Lucius Caesar, and groomed them as the heirs the bloodline had finally supplied. Both boys died within two years of each other, Lucius in AD 2 and Gaius in AD 4, and with them the direct line of descent that Augustus had spent decades engineering simply ran out.

Only after all of them were dead did Augustus fall back on the man he had not wanted, his stepson Tiberius. Tiberius came from Livia's first marriage. He was a Claudian by blood, not a descendant of Augustus at all, and the connection to the founder ran only through Livia's remarriage. Augustus adopted him in AD 4 and voted him the powers. On the same day he forced Tiberius to adopt his own nephew Germanicus, trying to steer the next succession after this one back toward the family he actually preferred. The recipe was now being applied to a man Augustus had reached for last, which is the clearest possible sign that the dynasty was built out of whoever was left alive, not out of anyone's design.

THE CONSEQUENCE

Repeating the recipe had an effect that no single use of it could have. Each time Augustus picked a relative, adopted him, and had the powers voted to him in advance, he taught Rome a lesson about where the next ruler comes from. The lesson was that the ruler's family supplies the ruler. Nobody passed a law saying this. There was no statute that named an order of succession, because to write one would have admitted that there was a throne to succeed to, which was the one admission the whole disguise existed to avoid. What there was instead was an expectation, built up by repetition, that the man in charge would arrange for a relative to hold his powers and that this relative would carry on after him. An expectation is not a law. It cannot be enforced in a court. But once everyone holds it, it does the work of a law: it tells the Senate whom to vote the powers to, it tells the armies whom to obey, and it tells rivals that they are challenging a settled order rather than competing in an open field. That expectation, and nothing written down, is what turned a personal arrangement into a dynasty.

No law made the Principate hereditary. A habit did. By granting his powers to a chosen relative again and again, Augustus taught Rome to expect that the ruler's family would supply the next ruler.

A WARNING ABOUT THE WITNESSES

Before following this machine into its first real test, a caution about the sources for everything that comes next. Almost everything later chapters will say about Tiberius, Caligula, Claudius and Nero comes from three writers who lived long after the events and disliked the system they described. Tacitus wrote his account around AD 110 to 120, Suetonius around AD 121, and Cassius Dio around AD 220. All three came from or wrote for the senatorial elite, the class that the emperors had overshadowed, frightened, and sometimes killed. None of them is a neutral witness, and the most lurid stories in the chapters ahead, the cruelties and the supposed madnesses, often come from exactly the writers with the strongest reason to make these men look monstrous. Modern historians do not throw the sources out, because they are most of what survives, but they read past the caricature, weigh the hostile color against coins, inscriptions, and administrative records, and treat the famous set-pieces as claims to be tested rather than facts to be repeated.

The machine was now built. Augustus had taken an office that could not exist and an inheritance that could not happen and engineered both into being through adoption, marriage, and the early grant of powers. Whether the machine actually worked could only be known when the man who built it was no longer there to make it work. That test came in AD 14, when Augustus died and Tiberius, the heir he had reached for last, had to find out whether powers arranged in advance would hold an empire together once their author was gone.

VII

Tiberius: The Reluctant Heir

Tiberius was the first man to test whether the Principate could survive its founder, and the test began the moment Augustus died in AD 14. The question was concrete. Augustus had ruled through a bundle of personal powers that, in law, could not be inherited. Could a successor actually step into that bundle when the man who held it was gone? The answer, it turned out, was already settled before anyone asked it. Tiberius had been voted the tribunician power and a great military command years earlier, by the method Augustus had perfected. He did not need to inherit anything at the funeral. He already held, in his own name, the exact powers that made a man ruler of Rome. When Augustus stopped breathing, Tiberius was simply, quietly, already in charge.

That is the first thing his reign proves about the system. The handover machine worked. The thing Augustus had built to make an un-inheritable position inheritable functioned exactly as designed: there was no vacuum, no scramble, no moment when Rome lacked a first citizen. The powers had changed hands long before, while the old man still lived, and the change took effect without a law of succession ever being written. The disguised monarchy passed from one man to the next on the first try.

Have I played the part well? Then applaud as I exit.

— Augustus, reported last words (Suetonius, Divus Augustus 99)

Augustus had treated his whole life as a performance, a role played out behind a Republican mask, and his reported deathbed line acknowledged it openly. The mask was the point. The man who took it over now had to wear it too, and the awkward part was that everyone could see the wearing.

THE CHARADE IN THE SENATE

The clearest evidence that the position had become real appeared in the very ritual meant to deny it. When the Senate met to confirm Tiberius, he refused. He spoke at length about the burden being too great for one man, about wishing to share the work, about not wanting sole power. The Senate, in turn, begged him to take it. The members pressed, he demurred, they pressed harder, and at last he allowed himself to be persuaded into a role he insisted he did not want. The whole scene was a performance, and both sides knew it was a performance.

What that performance reveals is worth stating plainly, because the theatre carried the real information. There was, in law, no office of emperor to accept or refuse. Tiberius held a collection of separate Republican powers that happened, in combination, to add up to sole rule. Yet the Senate did not treat them as separate. It treated them as a single thing, one transferable position, which Tiberius could take up or decline as a whole. The ritual of refusal only makes sense if everyone in the room already understood that a job existed to be refused. By acting out the reluctance, Tiberius and the Senate together confirmed the fact the entire system was designed to hide: that the bundle of powers had become one inheritable position, an office in everything but name.

The refusal also exposed how much the disguise now depended on shared pretending. Augustus had been able to make the Republican fiction look almost natural, because he had invented it and played it for forty years with extraordinary care. Tiberius could only repeat the lines, and the repetition made the artifice visible. The historian Tacitus, writing a century later, built his portrait of Tiberius around exactly this gap between word and intention.

He so thoroughly mastered the arts of dissimulation that men did not know whether he was speaking truth or falsehood.

— Tacitus, on Tiberius (Annals)

THE ABLE MAN AND THE TREASON TRIALS

Tiberius governed competently for years. He had been one of Rome's best generals, he understood the provinces and the armies, and he managed the finances of the empire with care. By most administrative measures he was a capable ruler. He was also widely resented, partly because of his cold manner and partly because he was a Claudian who had reached the position only after every Julian heir had died. Competence did not buy him affection, and his reign curdled around a single instrument that did more damage to the Roman political class than any battle: the treason trial.

The charge was maiestas, the crime of diminishing the majesty of the Roman state, and increasingly of the emperor personally. It was an old offence, but under Tiberius it became something new. Words could now be treason. A careless remark, an unflattering joke, a will that failed to honour the emperor, could be brought before the Senate by an informer hoping for a share of the condemned man's confiscated property. The accused were senators and knights, tried by their own peers, who learned that the safest course was to convict. The Senate kept its forms. It still met, still debated, still voted. But a body that condemns its own members for their words to please the ruler is no longer a free deliberative chamber. The maiestas trials hollowed out the Senate from the inside, leaving the shell of the Republic standing over an institution that had stopped functioning as one.

The fear reached into the imperial house itself. In AD 19 Germanicus, the popular heir whom Augustus had forced Tiberius to adopt, died in the eastern provinces. Rumour blamed poison, and suspicion ran toward Tiberius and his circle. Whether the rumour was true cannot be known, but its effect is clear enough: the death of the dynasty's most beloved figure, surrounded by whispers of murder, deepened the atmosphere of suspicion that the treason trials had already created. The system designed to make succession smooth was now consuming the very people it depended on.

SEJANUS AND THE GUARD

The next thing Tiberius's reign exposed was the latent power of the Praetorian Guard. Augustus had created the Guard as the emperor's bodyguard, the only troops stationed in and near Italy, but he had kept its cohorts scattered. Under Tiberius, the Guard's commander, the prefect Sejanus, gathered all of them into a single barracks just outside Rome, the Castra Praetoria. With one stroke he turned a dispersed bodyguard into a concentrated armed force sitting permanently at the door of the city, and he commanded it.

Sejanus used that force to make himself, for several years, the effective ruler of Rome. He controlled access to the emperor, steered the treason trials against his rivals, eliminated members of the imperial family who stood between him and power, and accumulated honours that pointed toward the succession itself. He was a knight, not a senator, with no blood tie to the dynasty, yet he ran the state. That a man could do this reveals something the disguise had obscured: real power in the Principate did not rest on family or office. It rested on control of armed men close to the centre. Sejanus held the Guard, and so for a while he held Rome.

NOTE

Sejanus fell as fast as he had risen. In AD 31 he came to a Senate meeting expecting to be granted yet more honours. Instead a letter from Tiberius was read aloud, condemning him. He was arrested and executed within hours, his body thrown down the Gemonian Steps. A wave of further treason trials followed against his associates.

His fall in AD 31 makes the same point from the other side. The Guard that had raised Sejanus did not save him; a single letter from the emperor was enough to destroy him in an afternoon. The Praetorians would follow whoever could plausibly command them, and Tiberius, even absent, still could. The Guard was a weapon. It mattered enormously who held the handle, and that question had no legal answer at all.

GOVERNMENT BY LETTER

In AD 26 Tiberius left Rome for the island of Capri, and he never returned. For the last eleven years of his reign the ruler of the Roman world was not in his capital. He governed from an island by correspondence, sending instructions to the Senate and the magistrates and receiving reports in return. The empire ran on.

This is perhaps the strangest proof of all about what Augustus had built. The princeps did not even need to be present. The machine of provinces, armies, taxes and courts had become regular enough that it could be directed by letter from a distance, with the emperor's authority traveling on paper. The position had become so detached from the man that the man could remove himself entirely and remain the man in charge. It was the letter from Capri, read aloud in the Senate, that destroyed Sejanus. An institution that can be commanded by post from an island is an institution, not a personal arrangement. The disguise said Tiberius was merely the first among citizens; the reality was a ruler whose power reached the whole empire while he sat on a cliff far from the city.

His self-exile carried a darker meaning too. A first citizen who leaves the city of the citizens he supposedly leads has stopped pretending to lead them in the old way. Tiberius's reported maxim captured the shift.

Let them hate me, so long as they approve my conduct.

— Tiberius, reported maxim (Suetonius, Tiberius 59)

It is a tyrant's line, softened. The original ran "let them hate, so long as they fear," and Tiberius had pulled it back toward "approve." The structure of the thought was still new in a ruler who claimed to be only first among equals. He no longer needed to be loved. He needed only to be obeyed and to govern well enough that the obedience could be justified. The Republican idea that a leader led by consent had quietly drained out of the words.

Tiberius died in March AD 37, aged seventy-seven, with ancient sources hinting that he was smothered as he lingered. He left behind a reign that had tested the Augustan system and found it durable in the one way that mattered. The first succession had worked. A successor had stepped into a position that legally did not exist, had held it for twenty-three years, had even run the empire from an island, and had passed it on in turn. That success carried a lesson that undid the founder's whole project. By working, the handover taught Rome that the position was real, that it was a single thing, and that it stayed in one family. Augustus had spent a lifetime insisting he had created no such office and founded no such dynasty. His heir's reign proved he had done exactly both. The next man to receive the position would inherit it with that lesson already learned. He was the surviving son of the murdered Germanicus, a young man Rome greeted with relief and hope, and he would be the first to stop pretending the office was anything but what it was.

VIII

Caligula: The Mask Comes Off

Caligula's reign is the moment the Republican disguise was dropped on purpose. His given name was Gaius, and he came to power in AD 37 as the dynasty's golden boy. He was the son of Germanicus, the popular heir whose death had darkened Tiberius's reign, and Rome had loved the father enough to transfer the affection to the son. After the cold, absent, suspicious Tiberius, the young man arrived on a wave of goodwill. Rome expected the return of Germanicus in his child. What it got was the first emperor to rule openly as a monarch, and that is why his short reign matters to the mechanism: it tested what happened when a ruler stopped pretending.

The succession itself ran on the same logic Tiberius's had. Caligula held no legal office of emperor, because none existed, but he was the obvious dynastic heir, and the Senate confirmed him with relief rather than reluctance. The handover worked again. The interesting thing this time was not the transfer of power but what the new holder chose to do with it.

The name

"Caligula" was a soldiers' pet name — little boots, after the miniature army sandals he wore as a child in his father's camps on the Rhine. He grew to hate it.

THE TURN

The good start did not last. Ancient sources place the change after a serious illness in late AD 37, which Caligula barely survived. Whatever the cause, the reign that followed broke with everything Augustus had built. Caligula showed open contempt for the Senate. He asserted his own personal supremacy without the careful Republican language that had clothed it for sixty years. He moved toward divine kingship, demanding to be treated as a living god rather than as the first among citizens. This was the line Augustus had never crossed and Tiberius had only edged toward. Augustus had let the provinces worship him while keeping the fiction intact at Rome, and full godhood came only after death. Caligula wanted it now, while alive, in the capital, from the senators themselves.

That demand is the heart of why his reign matters. For two generations the Principate had run on a shared pretence: the emperor pretended to be a citizen, the Senate pretended to be free, and both sides maintained the performance because the performance kept the peace. Caligula refused to perform. He treated the position as what it actually was, a monarchy, and required everyone else to treat it the same way. The Republican fiction was dropped openly for the first time, and the political class that had built its safety on the fiction had nowhere to stand.

The break was not only a matter of manner. Caligula's spending drove the treasury into crisis, and a ruler short of money who also despises the men with money is a dangerous combination for the rich. He demanded extravagant honours and took the trappings of divinity as his due rather than as gifts the Senate chose to bestow. The distinction sounds small but it was everything. Augustus had accepted honours as though reluctantly, always letting the Senate appear to give them freely, because the appearance of giving preserved the appearance of a free state. Caligula simply required the honours, and in requiring them he told the senators what they already feared: that their votes were not consent but obedience, and that the difference no longer mattered to the man they obeyed.

THE MADNESS, AND THE DOUBT ABOUT IT

The stories about Caligula's insanity are the most famous things about him. He is said to have planned to make his horse, Incitatus, a consul. He is said to have declared war on the sea and ordered his soldiers to gather seashells as spoils. These set-pieces have fixed him in memory as the mad emperor, the tyrant whose mind had broken. They deserve a caution, because the caution applies to this whole part of the story.

Everything known about Caligula comes from later writers with reasons to make him a monster. Suetonius, Tacitus and Cassius Dio all wrote decades or centuries afterward, all were men of senatorial sympathy, and all were describing an emperor who had humiliated the Senate. Modern scholarship reads the lurid stories with suspicion. The historian Aloys Winterling has argued that much of the "madness" is better understood as deliberate political theatre, or as later senatorial slander, rather than clinical insanity. On this reading, the threat to make Incitatus a consul was not the raving of a broken mind. It was a calculated insult, a way of telling the senators that he could elevate a horse to their highest honour and they would have to applaud. The point of the joke was their humiliation, and the joke worked because they could not stop him.

This reinterpretation is worth presenting plainly, because it changes what the reign means. If Caligula was simply insane, his reign is a freak accident, a story about one damaged man. If his cruelty was rational, the savage response of a ruler caught in an impossible game, then his reign is evidence about the system itself. The Principate forced the emperor to pretend the Senate was free while the Senate pretended to love him. Caligula refused both halves of the pretence and turned the contempt into open display. The "madness," on this view, is partly what a monarchy looks like when it stops disguising itself, and partly what hostile survivors wrote afterward to explain why they had killed him.

The watchword marks the distance from his predecessor exactly. Tiberius had softened the old tyrant's line into "let them hate, so long as they approve." Caligula reverted to the original and harsher version. He did not ask for approval. He asked for fear. The same source records an even colder saying, the wish that the Roman people had a single neck so that he could cut through it at one stroke.

Would that the Roman people had but a single neck!

— Caligula, reported saying (Suetonius, Caligula 30)

Whether or not he ever said those words, they capture how the political class came to understand the new kind of ruler it faced: a man who held the whole machinery of the state and felt no need to hide what holding it meant.

The reign ended on 24 January AD 41, when officers of the Praetorian Guard murdered Caligula in a passage beneath the Palatine during the games. The conspiracy was led by a tribune of the Guard named Cassius Chaerea, a man Caligula had repeatedly taunted over his voice. The emperor's wife and infant daughter were killed as well. Caligula was the first Roman emperor to be assassinated.

The lesson for the mechanism is sharp, and it follows directly from the dropped mask. The Praetorian Guard existed to protect the emperor. It was the armed force at the centre of the state, the weapon whose latent power Sejanus had already revealed under Tiberius. Caligula had ruled as a naked autocrat, demanding fear, dispensing with the disguise that had made the position tolerable to those around it. And the very soldiers meant to guard such a man were the ones who killed him. A ruler who governs by open terror gives the men closest to him the strongest reason to strike first. The mask, it turned out, had not only protected the Senate's dignity. It had protected the emperor. Without it, the position was lethally exposed.

The men who killed Caligula thought they were doing more than removing a tyrant. Some of the conspirators, and a number of senators who met in the hours after the murder, meant to restore the Republic itself, to abolish the Principate and return Rome to the government it had pretended all along still to have. For a few hours it seemed possible. The emperor was dead, the Senate was meeting, and the old constitution was openly back on the table for the first time since Augustus.

The dream lasted only as long as it took the Guard to act. The Senate could debate the Republic, but the Senate had no army, and the Praetorians did. The same soldiers who had killed one emperor immediately set about finding the next, because an empire without an emperor left them without a paymaster and a purpose. Caligula's murder proved that an emperor who dropped the mask could be killed. It did not prove that the position could be abolished. Why the restoration failed, and why the next ruler was made by the Guard rather than chosen by the Senate, is the subject of what follows.

IX

Claudius and the Secret of the Guard

The Praetorian Guard killed one emperor and made the next one in the same day. On 24 January AD 41 the officer Cassius Chaerea and his fellow conspirators stabbed Caligula to death in a passage beneath the Palatine. For a few hours Rome had no ruler at all. In the Senate house, senators met and talked openly about abolishing the Principate and bringing back the Republic. They were free, in principle, to do it. The office of emperor had no legal existence, so there was nothing on the books to stop them voting it away. What stopped them was simpler than law. They had no soldiers, and the soldiers had already decided.

While the senators debated, guardsmen ransacking the palace found a frightened middle-aged man hiding behind a curtain. He was Claudius, the brother of the beloved general Germanicus and the uncle of the murdered Caligula. A limp and a stammer had kept him out of public life for fifty years, which is precisely why he was still alive when most of his relatives were not. The Guard did not find a candidate they admired. They found a member of the imperial family they could use, and they needed one, because a Guard with no emperor to protect was a Guard with no reason to be paid. They carried him to their camp and proclaimed him emperor. The Senate's talk of a republic ended the moment it became clear the army had chosen.

THE PRICE OF A THRONE

Claudius understood what had happened to him, and he paid for it. To secure the loyalty of the men who had made him, he promised each guardsman 15,000 sesterces. This payment, called a donative, was a gift from a new emperor to his soldiers. The sum was enormous: more than ten years' wages for an ordinary legionary, handed to each man at once. It bought the Guard's allegiance, and it announced, without anyone saying so, that the throne now had a cash price and the soldiers were the ones who collected it.

The size of the change shows best in a single comparison. When Augustus died in AD 14, he left the Praetorians a bequest in his will of 1,000 sesterces a man — a founder's parting gift to the bodyguard he had created, given from above as a favour. Twenty-seven years later, Claudius paid 15,000 a man, not as a gift from a ruler to his troops but as a fee owed by a candidate to the men who could make or unmake him. The direction of the money had reversed. Under Augustus the emperor rewarded the Guard. Under Claudius the Guard set the emperor's price, and he met it.

THE COMPETENT MAN BEHIND THE CARICATURE

The hostile sources made Claudius a drooling fool dominated by his servants and his wives. The record of what he actually did contradicts them. The man was a serious administrator, well-read in history, and far more capable than the men who proclaimed him had any reason to expect. His central problem was the one his accidental accession had created: he had been made by soldiers, not chosen for any achievement, and he needed a victory the Senate could not deny him.

He found it across the sea. In AD 43 Claudius launched the conquest of Britain, sending some 40,000 troops and travelling to the front himself to take the surrender of the British town of Camulodunum. Britain had defeated Julius Caesar's attempts a century before, which made the conquest a prize Augustus and his successors had never claimed. It gave Claudius the military glory his unexpected rise had lacked, and it converted a man hauled out from behind a curtain into a conqueror with a triumph to his name. The arch raised in Rome to mark the victory appears on his coins, a piece of legitimacy struck in gold.

At home he governed actively. He extended Roman citizenship outward and admitted leading men from Gaul into the Senate, arguing that Rome had always grown stronger by absorbing outsiders. Part of the speech he gave defending this survives, carved on a bronze tablet found at Lyon — the words of a reflective, historically literate ruler, not the idiot of the gossip. He built on a scale his predecessors had avoided, draining marshland, raising aqueducts to water the city, and constructing a new harbour at Ostia so that grain ships could supply Rome more safely. These were the works of a man running an empire, and he ran it for thirteen years.

GOVERNMENT MOVES INTO THE HOUSE

To do all this, Claudius relied on a circle of powerful freedmen — former slaves of the imperial household, now its trusted managers. Men such as Narcissus, who handled the emperor's correspondence, and Pallas, who oversaw his finances, ran whole departments of state from inside the palace. The Senate found this intolerable and said so loudly, because it meant that ex-slaves were exercising authority that senators believed belonged to them. To them it was proof that Claudius was a weakling ruled by his servants.

Read without the senatorial outrage, it is something more important. The empire had grown too large to be run by the old Republican magistracies, which were designed to govern a city and its neighbours, not a territory ringing the Mediterranean. Someone had to manage the correspondence, the treasury, the petitions, and the records of a world-spanning state. Claudius built that machinery out of his own household, because the household was the only structure loyal enough and flexible enough to do the job. This was monarchy growing the organs a monarchy needs. Government was migrating out of the Senate and into the emperor's home, and once there it would stay.

As emperor he proved an energetic builder, judge and reformer.

THE WIVES AND THE ENGINEERED HEIR

The deadliest politics of the reign happened inside that same household. Claudius's wife Messalina was accused of conspiracy and executed in AD 48. He then married his own niece, Agrippina the Younger — a marriage so close in blood that the law had to be adjusted to permit it. Agrippina had a son from an earlier marriage, and she had a plan for him. She persuaded Claudius to adopt the boy, giving him the name Nero, and to advance him ahead of Claudius's own natural son, Britannicus. By adoption, an outsider's child had been placed first in line over the emperor's flesh and blood.

The succession was engineered again, by the same method Augustus had used — but now in the open, inside the palace, by a wife rearranging the line of inheritance to seat her own son.

This is the old Augustan recipe running once more: marry into the family, adopt the chosen heir, raise him above rivals. Augustus had performed it discreetly, presenting each step as a private family matter. Agrippina performed it as palace politics in plain sight, and the goal was nakedly dynastic. When Claudius died on 13 October AD 54, the Roman tradition was nearly unanimous that she had poisoned him with a dish of mushrooms to clear her son's path before Claudius could change his mind and favour Britannicus. Whether or not the mushrooms were real, the succession they secured was exactly the one Agrippina had built.

The reign of Claudius taught the empire two lessons it would not forget. The first was announced in the camp of the Praetorians in AD 41: emperors are made by soldiers, and soldiers expect to be paid for it. Tacitus would later call this the secret of empire, and AD 41 is where it first showed itself in plain view, with a price attached. The second lesson was quieter but just as lasting. While the Guard decided who would rule, Claudius built the machinery that actually did the ruling, lodged inside the emperor's own house. The monarchy now had both a method of selection and an apparatus of government, neither of which the Republic had ever authorised. What it still lacked was any guarantee that the man selected would be fit to rule. That question now passed to the sixteen-year-old his mother had killed to crown.

X

Nero: The Last of the Blood

The dynasty that began with Augustus ended with a sixteen-year-old put on the throne by his mother. Claudius died on 13 October AD 54, almost certainly poisoned by his wife Agrippina, and her son Nero stepped into a position that no law defined and no constitution acknowledged. He was Claudius's great-nephew and adopted son, lifted over Claudius's own boy Britannicus by adoption and marriage rather than by any rule of inheritance. The succession that the Republic had never authorised now ran entirely on family engineering, and the family engineer was a woman who had killed her husband to seat her child. Nero held the powers Augustus had assembled. He did not yet hold any idea of what to do with them, so for a while other people decided for him.

THE FIVE GOOD YEARS

Three people ran the empire in Nero's name at first. His mother Agrippina expected to govern through him and put her own face on the early coinage beside his. His old tutor, the philosopher Seneca, shaped his speeches and his policy. The prefect of the Praetorian Guard, Burrus, controlled the soldiers. Between them these three managed the state competently, and the result was remembered well enough that later writers gave it a name: the quinquennium Neronis, the five good years. Taxes were handled with restraint, the courts worked, the provinces were governed with some care. The young emperor was steered, and while he was steered the machinery Claudius had built ran smoothly.

The arrangement depended on Nero staying steered, which is the one thing a man who holds absolute power cannot be made to do for long. The powers were his, not his advisers'. The whole point of the position Augustus had created was that it concentrated authority in a single person, and that person was now a teenager discovering that nobody could legally command him. The good years lasted as long as Nero chose to be guided. They ended when he chose to stop.

THE FAMILY CONSUMES ITSELF

The first to die was the rival heir. Britannicus, Claudius's natural son, was a living reminder that Nero's claim came through adoption rather than blood, and that made him dangerous simply by existing. In AD 55 he died at dinner, poisoned, and the court understood at once what had happened. The murder was succession management of the kind the dynasty had practised since Augustus, except now it was done by killing a child at the table rather than by quietly arranging adoptions. The method had grown blunter because the stakes were nakedly clear to everyone involved.

Then Nero turned on the woman who had made him. Agrippina expected to rule through her son and would not accept being pushed aside, so Nero decided to be rid of her. The attempt was grotesque. A collapsible boat was built to drown her at sea; it failed, and she swam ashore. Nero then simply sent men to kill her outright in AD 59. A son had murdered his mother to free himself from her control. The dynasty that adoption and marriage had built was now eating its own members, because the position at its centre could be held by only one person and tolerated no rival close to it. Every relative was a potential claimant, and a potential claimant was a threat.

THE FIRE AND THE SCAPEGOATS

In July AD 64 a fire broke out in Rome and burned for days, destroying or damaging most of the city's fourteen districts. The most famous image attached to it is false. Nero did not fiddle while Rome burned: the fiddle is a bowed instrument that did not exist in the ancient world, and Nero was at Antium, some thirty-five miles away, when the fire began. The rumour Tacitus actually reports is that Nero sang of the fall of Troy to the lyre while the city burned, and Tacitus adds that even this was unconfirmed. On returning to Rome, Nero opened public buildings and his own gardens to the homeless and organised relief. The legend of the indifferent emperor strumming over the flames is a later invention.

What Nero did do was find someone to blame. A rumour spread that he had started the fire himself, and to deflect it he fastened the guilt on a small and unpopular religious sect. The Christians were rounded up and put to death with deliberate cruelty. Tacitus, no friend of the sect, recorded the episode in a passage that is also the earliest substantial non-Christian reference to the execution of Jesus.

Nero fastened the guilt and inflicted the most exquisite tortures on a class hated for their abominations, called Christians by the populace. Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus.

— Tacitus, Annals 15.44

The scapegoating shows the position at work in a new way. An emperor who feared the city's anger could redirect it onto a defenceless group, using the apparatus of the state to manufacture guilt and stage punishment. No senator authorised it and no law required it. The power to do it came from the same concentrated authority that had once been disguised as the modest service of a first citizen.

THE PERFORMER ON THE THRONE

After the fire Nero rebuilt, and he rebuilt for himself. Across the scorched centre of Rome he laid out the Domus Aurea, the Golden House, an enormous palace and parkland that fed the suspicion he had let the city burn to clear ground for it. The suspicion was probably unfair, but the scale of the project made it believable, and that perception mattered as much as the truth.

The deeper offence to the Roman elite was not the building but the man's idea of himself. Nero wanted to be an artist. He sang in public, played the lyre, drove chariots in races, and competed for prizes as if he were a hired performer rather than the ruler of the world. To the senators this was an emperor degrading his office, doing in public the things a Roman of rank paid slaves and foreigners to do. To the crowds of the city it was something else entirely: spectacle, generosity, an emperor who entertained them. This split in how Nero was received outlived him. He was genuinely popular with the urban poor and the soldiers, even as the elite came to loathe him. The hostile sources speak for the elite, which is why the monster they describe is so hard to square with the man the people mourned.

NOTE

The British Museum's 2021 exhibition, "Nero: the man behind the myth", argued that the hostile senatorial tradition badly distorts him. Nero was popular with the common people and the army, and for decades after his death "false Neros" appeared in the eastern provinces claiming he was still alive — a reception impossible to explain if everyone had hated him.

THE ELITE TURNS

The senatorial class eventually tried to kill him. In AD 65 a broad conspiracy formed around a nobleman named Gaius Calpurnius Piso, drawing in senators, officers and at least one poet. It was betrayed before it could strike, and the reprisals were savage. Suspects were forced to open their veins or were executed outright, and the net pulled in men far beyond the actual plotters. Among the dead was Seneca, the philosopher who had guided the five good years, ordered to take his own life by the pupil he had once steered. The conspiracy and its aftermath finished whatever loyalty the political class still felt. The men who staffed the government and sat in the Senate now had personal reasons to want Nero gone, and they remembered who among them he had killed.

ABANDONED

The end came from the provinces, where the real power had always lived. In AD 68 Vindex, the governor of a region of Gaul, revolted. Galba, the governor of a Spanish province, was proclaimed emperor by his own troops. The revolt spread because the armies that actually held the empire no longer saw any reason to hold it for Nero. The Senate, sensing the shift, declared Nero a public enemy, the first reigning emperor to be branded hostis by the body that had once pretended to grant him his powers. Then the decisive blow fell: the Praetorian Guard, the soldiers who made emperors and were paid to protect them, abandoned him. Without the Guard he had nothing. The position rested on armed loyalty, and the armed men had left.

Nero fled the city and hid in a villa outside Rome, and there, with a freedman's help, he killed himself in June AD 68. He was about thirty. Suetonius reports the words he repeated as he prepared to die, the lament not of a fallen ruler but of a lost performer.

What an artist dies in me!

— Nero, in Suetonius, Nero 49

With Nero died the last of Augustus's blood. The line that ran back through Claudius and Tiberius to the founder, and behind him to Julius Caesar, had no member left to take the throne. This was the unsolved problem detonating at last. Augustus had built a position that needed an heir while pretending it was not an office at all, and for a century the family had patched the gap with adoptions, marriages and murders, finding a new heir each time the bloodline failed. Now there was no one left to adopt and no one left to marry into the line. The dynasty had spent its members eliminating rivals until it had eliminated itself. The question the system had been designed to pretend away — who makes an emperor when the family is gone — could no longer be deferred. The armies that had quietly decided every succession would now decide the next one in the open, with no candidate of the old blood to disguise the fact. Within a year there would be four emperors, and not one of them descended from Augustus.

XI

The Secret Gets Out

When Nero killed himself in June AD 68, the bloodline that ran back to Augustus and to Caesar ended with him. He left no son, no adopted heir, no cousin waiting to step forward. For the first time since Actium there was no member of the founding family alive to take the powers. The question Augustus had spent his whole career arranging never to face came due all at once. Who rules now, and by what right? The system he built had no answer, because the office that needed one had never officially existed. A private citizen holding a stack of Republican powers cannot pass them to an heir when there is no heir by blood and no law that names a successor.

So the only force that could answer did. The armies decided. This is the proof the whole century had been building toward, and it arrived as a single year of civil war.

THE YEAR OF THE FOUR EMPERORS

AD 69 is the answer the system gave to the question it could not handle. Four men held the imperial powers in turn, and what settled each contest was not the Senate and not any rule of descent. It was which legions backed which general. Galba, the governor of Nearer Spain, was hailed first, then murdered by the Praetorian Guard in January when he refused to pay the donative he had promised them. Otho, who had bribed the Guard to do the killing, took his place and lasted three months. Vitellius, carried by the legions on the Rhine, beat Otho in battle in April; Otho killed himself. Then Vespasian, backed by the legions in the east and on the Danube, beat Vitellius by December and kept the throne. The names matter less than the machinery they expose. An emperor was now whoever the soldiers said he was, and the soldiers were on the frontiers, not in the city.

AD 69

The fighting that settled it was Roman legions against Roman legions. Vespasian's forces took Rome after the Second Battle of Bedriacum in October — and his win founded the Flavian dynasty, Rome's second.

Tacitus, writing about all this a generation later, put the lesson of the year into one sentence that has been quoted ever since.

A well-hidden secret of the principate had been revealed: it was possible, it seemed, for an emperor to be made elsewhere than at Rome.

— Tacitus, Histories 1.4

This is what the issue has been calling the secret of empire. For ninety years the Principate had insisted that power flowed from Rome, from the Senate and people, from the offices of a restored Republic. The year of four emperors showed that power flowed from wherever an army happened to stand. A general in Spain, a general on the Rhine, a general in Syria could each be made emperor by the men he commanded. Rome did not confer the throne. Rome ratified whoever won. The disguise Augustus had stitched together so carefully did not so much fall as get shouted down by soldiers.

FIVE REIGNS, ONE FLAW

The five Julio-Claudian rulers each answered the succession question a different way, and the differences trace the mask slipping. Set them side by side and the pattern is plain: every transfer of power was an improvisation, and the route to the throne drifted steadily away from the Senate and toward the army.

Read down the columns and the drift is unmistakable. The route to power moves from winning a war, to inheriting by adoption, to being chosen by the Guard, to being installed by a mother's palace plotting. The relationship with the Senate sours from deference to contempt. And the way power was actually held narrows, reign by reign, to one thing: soldiers, and the money paid to keep them loyal. By Claudius, the army was openly making the emperor and naming its price. By 69, with the family extinct, there was nothing left to disguise the transaction at all.

WHAT VESPASIAN ADMITTED

Vespasian won, and the Principate survived. That is the part worth holding onto. The system did not collapse in 69; it absorbed the shock and kept going. But it kept going as something it had never before allowed itself to be called. Vespasian had no blood link to Augustus and no Republican pretext for his power. He was a soldier whom other soldiers had made emperor, and everyone knew it. So when the powers were formally conferred on him, they were written down.

The law that did this is known as the lex de imperio Vespasiani, and a bronze copy of part of it survives. It is an ordinary-looking statute that gathers the emperor's powers into a single grant: the right to make treaties, to convene the Senate, to recommend candidates, to do what the previous emperors had done. For ninety years those powers had been a quiet accumulation of separate Republican offices, never named as a whole, because naming them would have admitted that a monarchy existed. Vespasian's law named them. The office Augustus had pretended did not exist was now set down in writing as a thing that could be handed to a man who had no claim except that he had won.

This is where the issue's argument lands. Augustus's genius and his fatal flaw were the same act. The mask, the insistence that he was only the first citizen of a free Republic, was exactly what let one-man rule survive its inventor, because a naked monarchy in 27 BC would have ended the way Caesar's did. But the same mask made succession impossible to legislate, because you cannot write rules for inheriting an office you swear is not there. The Julio-Claudian century is that single contradiction working itself out. Each reign improvised a transfer of powers that were, on paper, personal to one dead man, and each improvisation let a little more of the monarchy show. By the time the family ran out, the soldiers simply finished the sentence the system had been refusing to speak. Vespasian's bronze law is the empire at last saying the word aloud. Rome had been a monarchy for a century, and now it knew it, and now it had written it down.

A fact to leave you with. The Roman emperors never held an office called "emperor." Augustus ruled as princeps — "first citizen" — on an accumulation of ordinary Republican powers: a tribune's veto, a proconsul's command, a high priest's robes. That is exactly why nobody could ever say in law how the job should be inherited: officially, there was no job. The word "emperor" itself comes from imperator, which began as nothing grander than the cheer a victorious general's soldiers gave him on the battlefield.
