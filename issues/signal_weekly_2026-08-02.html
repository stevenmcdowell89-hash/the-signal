BAND 02 — THE LETTER FROM THE EDITOR

The Games that nearly weren't.

Glasgow closes the Commonwealth Games this afternoon, Norris ends a McLaren drought in Budapest, and the Bank sits tight — a week of endings before August starts everything over.

The week's biggest story finishes as you read this. The Commonwealth Games close in Glasgow this afternoon — ten sports, four venues, a fortnight of it — and the result that matters most isn't on the medal table. It's that the Games happened at all.

These are the friendliest and most fragile event in world sport: an idea from 1930 meant to be merrier and less stern than the Olympics, an empire's worth of history behind it, and, three years ago, a host city that walked away from the bill. How a stripped-back Glasgow rescued a Games nobody else would touch — and whether saving it this cheaply saves the whole movement — is the Long Read.

Elsewhere, more endings. Lando Norris took his first win of the year in Hungary, McLaren's first of 2026, before Formula 1 clocked off for August; the Bank of England held rates at 3.75% for a fifth straight time, still waiting on inflation to behave. Everything's wrapping up at once — just as the leagues, the grid and the August meteor sky all line up to switch back on. Pour the coffee.

— The Editor

MERRIER AND LESS STERN
NOT THIS WEEK · A STANDING STORY · 1930–2026 · LATEST DEVELOPMENT AUG 2026

The Friendly Games that nearly died

A festival invented in 1930 to be smaller and gentler than the Olympics almost had no 2026 at all — until a host pulled out over money and another agreed to stage it on the cheap. What Glasgow just closed is either the model that saves the Commonwealth Games or the shape they take on the way down.

BY THE EDITOR · THE LONG READ · A STANDING STORY

In August 1930, in Hamilton, Ontario, about four hundred athletes from eleven nations marched into a stadium for the first British Empire Games. There were seven sports and a crowd of roughly twenty thousand at the opening ceremony, and behind the whole thing sat an idea that reads oddly against everything international sport later became: this meeting was meant to be smaller, friendlier and less serious than the Olympics, not a rival to them. It was the invention of a Canadian sportswriter, Melville Marks Robinson — "Bobby" to everyone who knew him — who came home from the 1928 Amsterdam Olympics convinced that the countries of the Empire wanted a gentler festival of their own.

That phrase — "merrier and less stern" — has clung to the Games for the better part of a century. The label that stuck was the Friendly Games, and it was never entirely a joke: the event was built to prize the taking part over the winning, to feel like a reunion of a scattered family rather than a proving ground. It is worth holding onto that founding intent, because the argument now consuming the Games is, at bottom, an argument about whether being merrier and less stern is a thing a modern sporting event can survive on.

FIG. 01 Hamilton, Ontario, 1930 — the first British Empire Games, the founding edition of the movement that runs, unbroken, to Glasgow. Four hundred athletes, eleven nations, seven sports.Laura Moreland · CC BY-SA 4.0 · via Wikimedia Commons

From that start the Games grew, and their name kept catching up with a shrinking Empire. The British Empire Games became the British Empire and Commonwealth Games, then the British Commonwealth Games, and finally, from 1978, simply the Commonwealth Games. The renamings were not cosmetic. They tracked the awkward fact at the centre of the event: it is a sporting festival organised around the map of a former empire, held together now by a voluntary association of mostly independent countries rather than by any bond of rule. In 1930 the eleven competing nations traced almost exactly the reach of British power — Canada and Newfoundland, a scatter of Caribbean and Atlantic territories, the British Isles, South Africa, Australia and New Zealand.

FIG. 02 The eleven nations of the first Games, mapped — a footprint that traced the Empire almost exactly. This is a modern rendering, drawn in 2011, of the 1930 field: the present-day competition spans roughly seventy-four nations and territories, but the geography of who turns up still carries the founding shape.Wikimedia Commons contributor · CC BY-SA 3.0 · modern cartographic rendering

The field widened as the Empire dissolved into a Commonwealth of seventy-odd members, and for decades the Games simply persisted — every four years, staged by whichever member could afford the stadiums and the athletes' village, a mid-tier multi-sport event that never threatened the Olympics and never tried to. The question that trailed it was always the same one, asked more sharply each cycle: what is this for now? A festival named for a vanished empire, in a century that had stopped being sure it wanted to celebrate the fact, needed a reason to exist that was not simply that it always had. For a long time the answer was habit, and habit is cheap only until someone sends the bill.

FIG. 03 Lord Burghley — Olympic hurdles champion — at the inaugural 1930 Hamilton Games, a gentleman-amateur of exactly the type the founders had in mind when they built an event to prize the taking part over the winning.Public domain · Wikimedia Commons

The bill arrived for the 2026 edition. The Games had been awarded to the Australian state of Victoria, and on 18 July 2023 Victoria walked away, withdrawing as host as its projected costs climbed to somewhere between six and seven billion Australian dollars. A regional government looked at that figure — for a two-week event it had agreed to stage largely across smaller cities — and decided the sums did not work at any price it was willing to defend. That left the movement in the position it had spent decades insisting it would never reach: a Commonwealth Games with a date, a history and no host, three years out and facing the plain possibility that there would be no 2026 at all.

Glasgow took it. In September 2024 the city was confirmed as a replacement, on terms that were frank about what had nearly killed the event. Glasgow 2026 was designed to be small on purpose: ten sports rather than the usual twenty-odd, staged across four venues the city already owned, about seventy-four nations and territories and roughly three thousand athletes. The budget came in at around £150–160m. The number matters most next to the ones beside it — Birmingham had spent about £778m staging the 2026 Games' immediate predecessor in 2022, and Victoria had fled a bill of six to seven billion. Glasgow's rescue was not a grand statement of confidence. It was a decision to make the Games cost roughly a fifth of the last edition, and to find out whether anyone minded.

On the sport, the compact model held. Scotland, as host, finished fifth on the table with thirteen gold medals — a national best for a Commonwealth Games, ahead of the eleven it won at Melbourne in 2006. Australia ran away with the meeting on sixty-two golds and England took second on twenty-six, the familiar order at the top of the table undisturbed by the smaller frame around it. A stripped-back Games staged in four existing venues still produced a home team's finest medal haul on record, and it did so with para events fully integrated across six of the ten disciplines — a Games record for para medal events, and the clearest sign that shrinking the programme need not mean narrowing who it is for. Duncan Scott and a home breaststroke double from Angharad Evans gave the host its headline swims, the crowds turned up, and the fortnight looked, from the stands, like a Commonwealth Games. That is the single fact the model's defenders reach for first: whatever Glasgow gave up in scale, it did not give up on being a real competition. But a good fortnight of results settles only the immediate question — can you stage the thing cheaply and have it still count? — and leaves untouched the larger one, which is genuinely contested. The president of Commonwealth Sport, Dr Donald Rukare, answers it by refusing the premise that a smaller Games is a diminished one.

The trouble with rebirth as a framing is that the arithmetic which made Glasgow possible is the same arithmetic that nearly buried the Games. The £150–160m that reads as discipline reads equally as retreat: it is what is left after you strip out the venues nobody could pay for, the sports nobody could fund and the ambition that ran Victoria's bill to somewhere between six and seven billion Australian dollars. Set that figure beside Birmingham, which spent about £778m staging the 2022 Games only four years earlier, and Glasgow is not so much a new model as a controlled demolition of the old one — the same event delivered for roughly a fifth of the previous cost by delivering conspicuously less of it. A model built by subtraction proves you can stage the Games for less. It does not prove that a city will still bid for them once the novelty of the rescue has worn off, or that the athletes and broadcasters and sponsors who make the fortnight worth staging will follow the event down in scale rather than drifting toward the Olympics and the world championships that never shrank. Rebirth assumes a living thing; the numbers describe, at best, a convalescence. Academics who studied the Glasgow edition landed on exactly that hinge — that the survival of the Games now rests on something less certain than spectacle.

Their verdict is quieter than Rukare's and does more work. A compact Games buys time, not a guaranteed future, and the bill for that time falls due long after the closing ceremony, in whatever a host can show for the money once the medals are packed away. Affordable hosting, strong stories, inclusion, benefits that outlast the fortnight — none of those is a thing a good closing ceremony can settle, and none of them is visible on the medal table Scotland just set its own record on. They are the terms on which the next host, and the one after that, will decide whether to raise a hand at all. That is where the question stops being about Glasgow and starts being about geography. The analysts tracking the movement now frame its next chapter bluntly as reinvention or decline, with Africa and Asia rising as the regions that will decide whether the model travels — a genuinely reimagined Games would have to be one a host in that half of the map could both afford and want, not merely a cheaper restaging of a British one. Whether Glasgow's template survives contact with that ambition is the open question, and the people watching from outside are careful not to mistake one city's tidy fortnight for a formula anyone can simply inherit.

Line those four voices up and the disagreement is not about what happened in Glasgow — everyone agrees the Games were staged, cheaply, and came off. It is about what the staging proves. Rukare hears a rebirth; the academics hear a stay of execution whose conditions have not been met yet; Simpson hears a single data point that a future host in Africa or Asia will have to reinvent again, not inherit. The same fortnight of sport, read as a beginning, a maybe and a one-off, depending on where you are standing when you read it.

Bobby Robinson wanted an event that was merrier and less stern, a novel adventure in place of the grind of international rivalry. Ninety-six years on, the Games are still here, and the founding idea has quietly become the survival plan: be small, be cheap, be friendly, and hope that is enough to want. What nobody in the movement can yet say is whether Glasgow is the solid floor of a new and sustainable model, or the last comfortable rung on a longer way down — whether "merrier and less stern" is what a festival is built on, or what it says on the way out. The 2026 Games did not answer that. They only bought the movement the time in which the answer will have to arrive.

BAND 07 — PIXEL & BYTE GAMING

Master Chief on a PlayStation — landmark and franchise low in one breath — a mainline Halo running on a Sony console, after 25 years of Xbox exclusivity, is the sort of sentence that used to read like a typo. This month it stopped being one. The catch is that the same release is, by the aggregate, the lowest-scoring Halo ever shipped. Both facts sit inside a single launch, and the gaming month more or less turns on them.

FIG.Halo: Campaign Evolved in motion — the Unreal Engine 5 remake of 2001's Combat Evolved, now the first mainline Halo campaign on PlayStation.Xbox Game Studios / Halo Studios
  • 104.7

    Halo lands on PlayStation — and posts the lowest score in the canon

    Halo: Campaign Evolved launched 28 July on PS5, Xbox Series X|S and PC — the first time a mainline Halo campaign has appeared on a PlayStation console in the series' 25 years. It is a ground-up Unreal Engine 5 remake of Bungie's 2001 Halo: Combat Evolved: the original ten-mission campaign plus three new missions under the Operation: METEORITE banner, standard edition $49.99. Critics settled on "Strong" — a top-critic average of 83 on OpenCritic, with 89% recommending. Read the other way, that same 83 is the lowest aggregate any Halo has managed in a quarter-century, dragged down by PC performance problems and base-PS5 frame-rate caveats. Both readings are true of the one game: a real milestone that also happens to be the weakest-reviewed entry the franchise has ever put out. The landmark is the platform, not the polish. 28 July, everywhere.

BAND 09 — BOOKMARK BOOKS · ON THE NIGHTSTAND
  • JACKET · JAMES

    Beyond a Boundary

    Sport history · cricket & empire · 1963

    C.L.R. James's classic asks the question the week keeps circling: what does an empire leave behind once the flags come down, and how much of it survives as sport? Part memoir, part cricket writing, part political history, it reads the game he grew up with in colonial Trinidad as the place where the amateur ideal and imperial rule met and argued. As Glasgow closes a Commonwealth Games rebuilt on a smaller, cheaper model — ten sports, four venues, some seventy nations still turning up — James is the book that explains why a festival born of empire still means something to the countries that inherited it, and why "just a game" was never the whole story. The famous line is his: what do they know of cricket who only cricket know.

  • JACKET · SAGAN

    Comet

    Popular science · Sagan & Druyan · 1985

    The Perseids that peak on the night of 12–13 August are debris shed by Comet 109P/Swift-Tuttle, a 26-kilometre lump of ice on a 133-year orbit that last swung past in 1992 and won't return until 2126. Carl Sagan and Ann Druyan's book is the long story behind that one line — where comets come from, why they frightened people for millennia, and how a trail of grit left in a comet's wake becomes a shower of light in the upper atmosphere. Written for the return of Halley's Comet, it moves easily between hard science and the folklore that grew up around these visitors, and it holds up decades on. Read before a dark-sky night, it turns a streak of fire into a fact you can name.

  • JACKET · SERINA

    An Impossible Dream

    History · Cold War · Guillaume Serina · 2019

    The 1986 Reykjavik Summit reaches cinemas this month as The Brink of War, with Jeff Daniels as Reagan and Jared Harris as Gorbachev; this is the book behind the summit behind the film. Working from newly opened Kremlin and White House archives, Guillaume Serina reconstructs the two days in Iceland when two leaders came startlingly close to abolishing nuclear weapons — and then walked away over missile defence. It is the account of how much can hinge on a single room, useful context for anyone who wants the history before the dramatisation shapes it.

The appeal for a newcomer is not homework; it's immediacy. This is music engineered for warmth and motion, the sort that can rewire your idea of a summer record inside the first two tracks, all bright top-lines and rhythms that refuse to sit still. Treat it as the discovery slot on the shelf: the one furthest from the usual playlist, and the one most likely to widen it. Out 14 August.

After a training break — a holiday, an illness, a run of missed weeks — the instinct on the way back is to pick up exactly where the last block left off. In a hypertrophy phase, that instinct costs more than it buys, and the reason isn't the one most people fear. Muscle doesn't detrain fast: strength and size hold across one to two weeks off with very little loss, and both come back quickly once the load returns. What actually punishes the comeback is soreness.

The mechanism is the repeated-bout effect. A muscle trained regularly builds a protection against the micro-damage that produces delayed-onset soreness, and that protection decays over a couple of weeks away from load. So the first hard session after a layoff lands harder than the same session did a month earlier — not because the weight is heavier, but because the muscle has lost its callus against it. Return to full pre-break volume in week one and the bill arrives two days later as the soreness that flattens the next two sessions. The layoff doesn't cost muscle; the over-eager restart costs training days.

The evidence-based fix is to treat the first week or two as re-acclimation rather than a relaunch. Load can come back fast — the nervous system remembers heavy weight — but volume, the total count of hard sets, is what drives soreness, so bring it back in steps. The standard guideline across hypertrophy programming is to raise weekly volume in modest increments, roughly 10–20% a week, letting each level be tolerated before the next goes on top. The smaller the unit you add back, the easier it is to absorb and the easier it is to walk back.

DO THIS WEEK · THE SESSION

Coming off a break, rebuild volume one set at a time — add a single set to each main lift rather than a whole extra session, and cap the weekly jump at around 20%.

Muscle holds through a short layoff; soreness is what costs training days. A graded ramp lets the repeated-bout protection rebuild before the volume that would otherwise wreck the following week.

This week's Long Read turns on an ideal older than any of its stadiums: the amateur, the competitor who shows up for love of the sport rather than a living. Follow that word back and it keeps the promise for a surprisingly long way. Amateur comes into English through French, from the Latin amator — a lover — and behind that stands the flat, ordinary verb amare, to love. For its first stretch in the language the word meant precisely what its root says: someone who does a thing for love, not money, and was respected for it. It sits in a whole family of amare words — amorous, amiable, enamoured, paramour — all grown from the same three-letter seed.

English borrowed it in 1784 from a French amateur, "one who loves," itself restored in the 16th century from an older French form. The Latin ancestor amator carried no faint praise at all: it meant a lover, a friend, a devoted admirer, an agent noun off amatus, "having been loved." Early English used it that way. The gentleman amateur — the naturalist, the astronomer, the collector who pursued a study for the pleasure of it and wanted no fee — was a figure of standing. To be an amateur was to be free of the corrupting tug of payment, to love the work for itself.

Then the word turned on itself. By 1786, two years after it landed, English was already using amateur to mean a dabbler — someone who plays at a thing without mastering it. Through the 19th century that second sense set hard. Loving a pursuit came to imply not being good enough to be paid for it; the devotee curdled into the amateur we now reach for as a mild insult, the person whose work gives away that they never turned professional. One word held both readings — the one who loves, and the one who falls short — and slowly the compliment lost. It even acquired an opposite it had never needed before: the professional.

Sport is where the two senses collided hardest. Nineteenth-century athletics built a whole creed of amateurism — compete for love of the game, never for money — and made it the badge of a true sportsman. But the rule did a second, quieter job: banning pay banned anyone who could not afford to compete for free, which in practice meant the working man who needed the wage. The love-not-money principle that sounded like virtue also worked as a fence. That is the tension the Friendly Games inherited in 1930 and have been negotiating ever since — the same fall the word itself took, from a term of honour to a line deciding who is let in. Amare, to love, is still down there under all of it, doing quiet work.
