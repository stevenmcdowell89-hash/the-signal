BAND 02 — THE LETTER FROM THE EDITOR

A week of narrow passages.

One strait, three stories: Tehran says it stays shut, Muscat calls the talks constructive, Washington won't pay a toll — and a ship is still burning.

There is a stretch of sea twenty-one miles wide at its neck that a fifth of the world's shipped oil must thread, and this week it did the thing narrow places do under pressure. A missile hit an Abu Dhabi tanker on Saturday; Iran set terms for reopening the waterway that read like a ransom note; Oman, stationed in the middle of it, described the same water as "positive and constructive". Brent climbed back above $83. Three accounts of one passage, and no two of them the same passage.

The rest of the week was thresholds. Europe cooked — all twenty-seven of Italy's heat-alert cities went red at once, a first — and a Guatemalan mountain blew its top. On the calendar, meanwhile, everything is crouched just before the gun: two football seasons, Formula 1's return, a new Pixel, and, best of all, the Perseids, peaking Wednesday night under a new moon with nothing to wash them out. Nothing has quite started yet. That is the whole feeling of the week — pressure in the neck of things, and the wide part still to come.

THE LONG READ · THE GULF

The strait and the toll

A missile hit an ADNOC tanker in the Strait of Hormuz on Saturday. Iran says the waterway stays shut until Washington backs down, Oman calls its own talks constructive, and a US official rules out any toll — three descriptions of the same narrow sea, produced in the same week.

BY THE EDITOR · THE GULF · 9 AUGUST 2026

On Friday 8 August a missile struck a tanker owned by ADNOC, Abu Dhabi's state oil company, as it transited the Strait of Hormuz. The United Arab Emirates said Iran had fired it, one of a run of attacks on commercial shipping, and that no one was hurt in that particular hit. The same week, over the same water, Iran set out the terms on which it would allow the strait to reopen, Oman said the talks to end the standoff were going well, and a US official said there was nothing to negotiate. War and diplomacy, the same seven days, the same fifty-mile channel. The daily news carried each of these as a separate bulletin. Read together, they describe a strait that four governments cannot agree is open, closed, or for sale.

FIG. 01 The choke point, mapped. Iran holds the northern shore; the shipping lanes run past Oman's Musandam peninsula to the south. This reference map dates from 1980, but the geography that makes the strait unavoidable has not moved.Perry-Castañeda Library map collection · US Government · public domain · via Wikimedia Commons

Start with why the strait matters, because everything else hangs off it. Hormuz is the sea gate of the Persian Gulf: at its narrowest about 33 kilometres of water, with the usable shipping lanes narrower still. In the first half of 2025, roughly 20.9 million barrels of crude and petroleum liquids passed through it every day — somewhere between a fifth and a quarter of all the oil that moves by sea anywhere in the world. There is no overland pipeline network that can carry that volume if the channel closes. That is the whole reason a single contested strip of sea can raise the price of fuel in countries thousands of miles away that have never heard a shot fired. And the flows are already thinning. Through the first quarter of 2026 they fell almost 30 percent year on year, to 14.6 million barrels a day, and in early August, as the strait flared again, Brent crude pushed back above $83 a barrel.

Put those figures side by side and the shape of the crisis comes into focus. In the first half of 2025 the strait carried 20.9 million barrels a day; by the first quarter of 2026 that had fallen to 14.6 million. Roughly six million barrels a day have already gone missing from the world's most important oil artery — not through a single dramatic closure, but through a slow strangulation as buyers reroute, insurers price in the risk, and some cargoes simply do not sail. The waterway is not shut. It is being throttled, and Brent's move back above $83 is the market reading the throttle rather than any headline.

This week produced four official descriptions of that channel, and they do not fit together. The first came from Tehran. Iran's Supreme National Security Council laid out what it would take to reopen the strait, and the list was not a negotiating position so much as a demand for surrender: the United States must permanently end the war it and Israel launched in February, lift its naval blockade, withdraw its military, drop sanctions, and compensate Iran for war damage. The council's secretary, Mohammad Bagher Zolghadr, an IRGC commander, put the whole posture into a single sentence.

Tehran put machinery behind the words. On 6 August the Iranian parliament reviewed a plan to bar ships linked to the United States, Israel and other "hostile" states from the strait altogether, and to charge every other commercial vessel a transit fee of up to 7 percent of its cargo's value, with a 20 percent fine for violations. That is the toll of the piece's title: not a metaphor but a draft tariff, a proposal to turn the world's most important oil route into a manned tollgate.

The second description came from Muscat, and it was almost the opposite in tone. Oman has been mediating between Iran and the United States, and on 8 August its foreign ministry said the discussions were continuing "in a positive and constructive atmosphere," while condemning the attacks on shipping in the strait. There was more than mood behind the optimism. A day earlier, Iran said it had finalised a shipping-route arrangement with Oman — a northern corridor near the Iranian coast for vessels entering the Gulf, and a southern corridor near the Omani coast for those leaving it. President Trump had said on 5 August that the strait would reopen "soon." From Muscat, the story of the week was a deal taking shape.

The third description came from the ships themselves, and it was neither hopeful nor conditional. ADNOC reported that more than a dozen of its vessels had been attacked while trying to get through — a running tally, not a single headline.

FIG. 02 The pressure at sea. An IRGC fast-attack boat runs close to US vessels in the Gulf in this 2022 image; it is craft like these, alongside missiles and drones, that have made every transit of the strait a gamble this year.NAVCENT Public Affairs / US Navy · public domain · via Wikimedia Commons

The company said its ships had been hit repeatedly since the war began: "over a dozen of its vessels have been attacked by missiles and drones while transiting the strait since the U.S. and Israel launched the war on Iran in February." Across those attacks, one crew member has been killed and twenty wounded. Friday's strike, the one with no casualties, was simply the latest entry in that ledger. It was not an isolated event; it was the cost of doing business in a strait that neither side controls outright. Two days earlier, on 5 August, Yemen's Houthis had claimed an attack on a Saudi oil tanker — on the same day Trump was promising the waterway would open soon. The tanker war does not pause for the diplomacy; it runs underneath it.

The fourth description came from Washington, and it did not match any of the other three. A US official flatly rejected Iran's toll, insisting that any temporary routes through the strait would carry no charges at all: "Any temporary routes will be without any impediments — meaning no approvals or permissions and no tolls or charges." Set that beside Tehran's draft tariff and the problem is plain. Iran's parliament is debating a 7 percent levy; Oman is brokering corridors ship by ship; and the United States is saying there is no fee to broker, because acknowledging one would concede that Iran has the standing to charge it. The two sides are not haggling over the price. They are not even negotiating the same document.

Around the table, the pressure kept building off it. On 7 August Saudi Arabia braced for a threatened coordinated assault — Iran-backed Iraqi militias and the Houthis, with IRGC support — against civilian and economic sites, energy infrastructure among the targets. The strait is the crisis's front page, but the same war is being fought at the kingdom's oil terminals and across the wider Gulf, and a strike on Saudi energy would move the oil price as surely as a mine in the channel.

THE CASE AGAINST · THE STRAIT NEVER ACTUALLY CLOSED

The toll and the terms may be theatre over a strait that is quietly staying open.

There is a colder reading of the same week. For all the talk of closure, the oil kept moving: 14.6 million barrels a day still flowed through Hormuz in the first quarter, and ADNOC's tankers are being hit precisely because they are still sailing through, not because they have stopped. Meanwhile Iran and Oman have finalised a working corridor — a northern lane in, a southern lane out — which looks less like a blockade than a managed reopening dressed as a standoff. On this account the toll bill and the maximalist list are leverage, not policy: an argument over who gets to claim credit for a strait that both sides have a powerful interest in keeping passable. Critical Threats' read of the week points the same way — when the Islamic Resistance in Iraq postponed its threatened response after mediation by Badr's Hadi al-Ameri on 7 August, that looked like a tactical pause, not the opening move of a wider war. If markets are pricing bluff rather than blockade, the diplomacy at cross-purposes is the point: it lets everyone posture while the tankers, battered but moving, get through.

The trouble with that reading is that a strait held open by improvisation is one bad day from being held shut by accident. Every transit is now a calculated risk, and it only takes one strike on a fully laden ship, or one salvo at a Saudi terminal, to turn a managed standoff into a genuine closure. The corridor Iran and Oman have drawn is a courtesy, not a treaty, and courtesies do not survive the first serious escalation. Brent above $83 is the market's way of saying it believes the oil will keep moving — but is charging a premium against the chance that it won't.

So the week ends with four governments describing one channel of water and agreeing on almost nothing about it. Tehran says it is closed until America capitulates. Muscat says a deal is close. ADNOC says its ships are being shelled every time they try to pass. Washington says there is no toll to pay. Each statement is true to the people who issued it, and no two of them can be fully true at once. The strait itself is indifferent to all of it — 33 kilometres of sea that a fifth of the world's shipped oil has to cross whatever the politics. What to watch now is not the rhetoric but the corridor: whether the quiet Iran-Oman arrangement becomes the real reopening while both capitals keep arguing over the toll, or whether one more missile settles the question the way none of the four statements could.

Sport spent this week between seasons — the high summer lull, with no verdict of record to hand over. No league match kicked off in earnest, no final was played, no trophy changed hands; the title races are all paused for August, and the pre-season friendlies count for fitness and little else. What moves in a week like this is not the scoreline but the squad sheet. The transfer window is where the drama sits, and with Serie A due to open on 23 August, the most consequential rebuild of the summer is the one taking shape in Turin — so that is where an honest Touchline starts, with the deals rather than the games.

Jonathan David joined on a free transfer on 5 August, signing a five-year deal after his Lille contract expired — a proven Ligue 1 goalscorer added without a fee. BAND 09 — BOOKMARK BOOKS · ON THE NIGHTSTAND

  • Prisoners of Geography

    Politics · ten maps · modern primer

    Tim Marshall's 2015 bestseller makes a simple, durable argument: the biggest forces in geopolitics are physical ones — mountains, rivers, coastlines and choke points — and it gives a chapter apiece to ten maps that explain why nations behave as they do. One of those choke points is the Strait of Hormuz, the exact stretch of water this issue's Long Read spends its length on. Read alongside it, the book widens a single flashpoint into a whole grammar of why geography still writes the news. It is brisk, clear and structured for dipping — a map at a time — and it has aged into the standard first book on the subject. It is also the odd one out on a nightstand that leans fantasy and SF: a slim work of non-fiction that reframes the week's real headlines rather than escaping them, which is exactly what earns it the spot.

  • The Tainted Cup

    Fantasy · murder mystery · Shadow of the Leviathan #1

    The first in Robert Jackson Bennett's Shadow of the Leviathan sequence is a Holmesian murder mystery grafted onto a gloriously strange secondary world. It runs on the classic detective pairing — a formidable investigator and the assistant sent out to do the legwork and narrate it — and pairs a genuinely fair-play whodunit, its clues laid down honestly for the reader to catch, with the kind of dense, thought-through worldbuilding that rewards a reader who likes their fantasy to repay close attention. The timing helps, too: the third book in the series, A Trade of Blood, landed on 4 August to best-of-the-year notices, which makes book one the natural on-ramp to a series with plenty of road still ahead of it.

  • Inverting the Pyramid

    Football · tactics history · pre-season reading

    Jonathan Wilson's 2008 book is the definitive history of football tactics — how the game travelled from the attacking pyramids of the 1900s to the pressing systems that shape it now, read as a century-long argument between formations, each one answering the last. It is the rare tactics book that non-coaches actually finish. With the Premier League opening on 21 August and Serie A two days behind it, this is the pre-season read that makes the coming season's tactical shifts legible before a ball is kicked. It is written as narrative history rather than coaching manual, which is why it lands for anyone who follows the game closely without ever having drawn a formation on a whiteboard.

BAND 10 — THIS WEEK IN HISTORY HISTORY · DATED

On the night of Saturday 13 August 1961 — 65 years ago this week — East German soldiers and police strung barbed wire across the heart of Berlin, and a city that had gone to bed as one place woke up cut in two. It happened in the small hours, when the streets were empty. Work gangs tore up the cobbles, sank posts, and unrolled coil after coil of wire along the line between the Soviet sector and the three Western ones. Tram tracks were severed mid-street. Underground trains stopped serving stations on the wrong side. By dawn, Berliners who had crossed for work, for family, for a drink the evening before found the crossing gone — a threshold sealed overnight.

FIG.The barrier going up in Berlin, November 1961 — barbed wire giving way to concrete block, guard by guard, street by street.US National Archives · public domain · Wikimedia Commons

The reason was an open door. Since the founding of the two German states in 1949, some two and a half million people had left the East for the West — and Berlin was the loophole. The long inner-German border was already fenced and mined, but inside the city the sectors were still porous; you could simply ride the U-Bahn from one system to the other. By the summer of 1961 that trickle had become a flood, thousands a day, and it was carrying off exactly the people the young republic could least spare: engineers, doctors, teachers, the skilled and the young. The state was bleeding through the one wound it had never managed to close. Just weeks earlier, the East German leader Walter Ulbricht had told a press conference, "No one has any intention of building a wall." On 13 August, no one needed to say it any more.

Wire came first, then permanence. Over the following weeks and months the improvised fence hardened into the thing the world would recognise — concrete slabs, watchtowers, a raked "death strip" of sand between two walls, floodlit and mined and swept by guns. The regime called it the Antifaschistischer Schutzwall, the Anti-Fascist Protection Rampart, as though the danger stood outside. In truth the guns pointed inward. In its final form the barrier ran some 155 kilometres around West Berlin, turning half a city into an island inside another country.

What it cost is measured in families as much as in miles. Streets were bricked up with people's front doors still facing the West they could no longer reach; parents and children ended the day of 13 August on opposite sides of a line that would hold for a generation. At least 140 people died trying to cross it in the years that followed — shot, drowned, or falling. The Wall stood for 28 years, until the night of 9 November 1989, when a bungled press briefing and a crowd that would not go home simply overwhelmed it. But the thing to hold onto is the speed of the beginning: a city drawn tight in a single night, a chokepoint improvised in the dark and then set in concrete, because a state had decided the cheapest border to defend was the one that ran straight through the middle of people's lives.

BAND 11 — THE DESK SERVICE

The Session

FITNESS · BLOCK 3, HYPERTROPHY VOLUME

How many hard sets is enough? For building muscle, the answer landed this month with unusual confidence: roughly 10 to 20 sets per muscle per week, and if you can recover from more, more still tends to help. A 2026 meta-regression by Pelland and colleagues pooled 67 studies and 2,058 participants and found a 100% posterior probability that the relationship between weekly set volume and growth is positive — the line keeps climbing well past where most lifters stop. So take the answer first, plainly: on the volume question there is no cliff you fall off, only a slope that gets gentler.

The word doing the work is diminishing. The returns are real but they flatten — the jump from 6 sets to 12 buys far more than the jump from 18 to 24, and past a point the extra volume mostly buys extra fatigue. That is the useful shape for where Block 3 sits: hypertrophy at a maintenance-to-slight-surplus intake, rebuilding after the holiday. You are not chasing a ceiling; you are climbing back onto the slope. Add volume where a lift can clearly take it, bank the easy early gains, and stop feeding sets to a muscle that has stopped answering.

DO THIS WEEK · THE SESSION

Add one hard set to each of your two biggest lifts this week — a single top-of-range set, kept at 1–2 reps in reserve, not to failure.

The meta-regression says the marginal set still pays on the way up the slope, and a post-holiday re-ramp is exactly where that cheap volume goes furthest — one set is enough to progress the stimulus without swamping recovery.

The Word, Drawn Tight · One Root, Many Knots
*streig-the reconstructed root — to press, to draw tightPIE

All week the news has turned on a narrow passage of water, and the name for that kind of place is quietly one of the most loaded words in English. A strait is not just a gap between two seas. Pull the word apart and you find the exact idea that the whole Hormuz crisis is built on: something drawn tight until it strains. It comes to us through Old French estreit — tight, narrow, constricted — from the Latin strictus, the past participle of the verb stringere, "to bind or draw tight." The word does not describe the water so much as what the water does to everything trying to pass through it.

That Latin root is astonishingly fertile. From strictus and stringere English inherited a whole family of words about tightness and constraint: strict (bound by rules), stringent (drawn tight), constrict and restrict (tightened, held in), and even strain, the sense of something pulled to its limit. Follow the same root sideways through Old French and it surfaces again in stress and distress — pressure, and the anguish of being pressed. Every one of these is, at bottom, the same gesture: a hand closing tight around a thing. The strait is the geography of that gesture — a place where the map itself is clenched. That the same root gives us both stringent rules and personal distress is the whole point: the cluster keeps circling back to the feeling of being pressed with no room to move.

English reached for the tightness before the geography. The adjective, meaning simply "narrow," is in use by around 1300 — the "strait gate" of scripture is a narrow one, not a straight one. Only in the 14th century does the noun settle onto its most familiar job: a narrow sea passage squeezed between two larger bodies of water, a chokepoint drawn tight by the land on either side. And by the 16th century the word slips into metaphor and never really leaves — to be in dire straits is to be pressed into a tight place with no easy way out, the plural doing exactly what the geography does.

One warning, because the two are eternally muddled: strait is not straight. The look-alike is Germanic, descended from "stretch," and means unbent — a different word with a different history that only happens to sound the same. Which leaves strait pristine in its meaning. When a tanker burns in the Strait of Hormuz and three governments each insist the passage is about to reopen, they are arguing, etymologically, over "the narrow, drawn-tight place" — and the week's whole story is really a single question the word has been carrying for two thousand years: how much pressure can a narrow thing bear before something has to give.
