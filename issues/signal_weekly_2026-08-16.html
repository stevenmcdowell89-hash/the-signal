A NUMBER FOR HOW YOU FEEL

Can a watch call your recovery?

On 12 August, Google gave the Pixel Watch 5 a daily Readiness Score — a single figure that tells you how recovered you are before you have decided anything yourself. It is the latest wrist to make a medical-grade promise. The science underneath it is less settled than the number pretends.

BY THE EDITOR · THE LONG READ · 16 AUG 2026

On 12 August, Google unveiled the Pixel Watch 5 with a feature it wants you to check before you decide how hard to train: a daily Readiness Score. The number weighs your recent sleep and exertion and hands back one verdict on how recovered your body is. It arrives on a watch that starts at $399 for the 41mm case and $429 for the 45mm, reaches shelves on 20 August, and does not travel alone — the same device now tracks blood-pressure patterns and insulin-resistance trends, watches for a breathing emergency, and can flag a loss of pulse from its first day on the wrist. The pitch is no longer about counting steps. It is a sensor that reads your physiology and tells you what to do about it, and the Readiness Score is the sharpest form of that claim: not data, but a decision.

Google is not first to make it. Whoop built its entire brand on a morning Recovery percentage; Garmin ships a Training Readiness figure on its watches; Oura sells a Readiness Score on a ring. Every one of them rests on the same promise and, underneath, the same biological signal. A launch this loud is worth using to ask the question the whole category depends on and rarely answers plainly: can a device strapped to your wrist overnight really tell you, the next morning, that you are recovered?

The signal all of these scores are built from is heart-rate variability, or HRV — the small, constant fluctuation in the time between one heartbeat and the next. A resting heart does not tick like a metronome; the gap between beats stretches and shrinks from one beat to the next, and that variation is governed by the autonomic nervous system, the same machinery that runs the balance between stress and recovery. More variability, broadly, means a body in a rested, parasympathetic state; a flattened, more regular rhythm can mark strain, illness or poor sleep. A readiness score is a proprietary transform of that overnight variability, together with resting heart rate, sleep and recent training load, compressed into one figure the marketing invites you to obey.

The gold standard for measuring that variation is the electrocardiogram — the chest electrodes that read the heart's electrical activity directly, the trace in the figure above. A watch cannot do that. It measures blood flow optically instead: green LEDs shine into the skin and a photodetector infers each pulse from the light that bounces back, a technique called photoplethysmography, or PPG. The readiness number is therefore an estimate built on an estimate — a wrist sensor standing in for the ECG, and a closed formula standing in for the clinical judgement it claims to make. Two things have to hold for the verdict to be trustworthy: the sensor has to measure the signal accurately, and the formula has to interpret it correctly. The evidence treats those as separate questions, and it answers them differently.

The manufacturers' case is the confident one. A low reading in the morning is a signal to ease off; a high one is a green light to push. Google's own framing for the Pixel Watch 5 puts the Readiness Score at the centre of the day's training decision, and the whole consumer-wearable market has spent years teaching people to check the number first and consult how they feel second. Cast that way, the watch becomes a coach that never sleeps and never flatters you.

That confidence used to be low-stakes. A miscounted step, an over-generous calorie estimate — nobody was hurt by a step tracker that flattered them. The Pixel Watch 5 launch marks a category that has stopped being low-stakes. The same watch that hands you a Readiness Score now claims to surface blood-pressure patterns, flag insulin-resistance trends and detect a loss of pulse, and the Readiness Score itself is pitched as guidance you act on — resting a body it says is strained, driving one it says is fresh. Once a wrist device is trusted to make calls with physiological consequences, the accuracy of the reading behind the call stops being an academic point. A number that is wrong in the direction of "you're recovered, push harder" is a different kind of error from a badly counted step.

The validation literature is more careful, and it starts with the measurement. When researchers put consumer devices next to a clinical ECG, the agreement varies a great deal by brand. In one comparison, Oura's fourth-generation ring reached near-reference agreement with the ECG, at a concordance of 0.99; Whoop landed at a moderate 0.94; Garmin came in at 0.87 and Polar at 0.82 — a spread wide enough that "a wearable measured my HRV" means materially different things depending on which wearable you strapped on. More important still, that accuracy is conditional: it holds up overnight, when the wearer is still and at rest, and degrades sharply the moment there is motion. The sensor is at its best in exactly the conditions a readiness score is captured — asleep, unmoving — and at its worst everywhere else.

The researchers who run these comparisons put the trade-off plainly.

A second strand of research questions the number from the other end — not whether the watch measures HRV accurately, but whether the resulting verdict corresponds to anything the wearer experiences. A 2026 study in the journal Sensors reported a disconnection between wearable-derived HRV and users' own self-reported wellbeing: subjective feelings of readiness frequently did not match the tracker's biometric verdict, and the authors urged caution before acting on HRV-based recommendations to guide health decisions. That finding cuts at the heart of the product, because the entire proposition is that the number knows something about your state that you do not. If the figure and the feeling routinely part ways, at least one of them is not measuring readiness in any sense that a person can use.

The people who work with HRV professionally tend to converge on a narrower claim than the watches make. Marco Altini, an HRV researcher whose widely-read essay on the subject is a standard reference, has argued that HRV is meaningful as a trend followed over weeks and read against your own established baseline, and close to useless as a single-morning verdict. Day-to-day HRV is noisy; one night tells you very little, while the direction of travel across many nights tells you a great deal. On that reading, the failure is not the sensor but the presentation: the opaque, proprietary scores that crush a trend into one confident figure overclaim what a single figure can honestly carry. The number is real; the certainty it is displayed with is manufactured.

Those four positions are not really arguing about the same thing, which is why they can all be right at once. The manufacturers are selling an interpretation. The validation studies are testing a measurement. The Sensors study is testing whether the interpretation matches lived experience. And the researchers are describing the timescale on which the whole exercise makes sense. Stack them and the disagreement resolves into a division of labour: the hardware has largely earned its claims, the software layered on top has not, and the honest use of the device sits somewhere between what the box promises and what the sceptics allow.

A watch can measure the beat-to-beat flicker of your heart with clinical accuracy and still be wrong about whether you are recovered. Those are two different problems, and only the first one is solved. TRANSMISSION №021 · THE LONG READ

So can a watch call your recovery? The four positions do not cancel out; laid end to end, they point at a single, unhedged answer. A good wearable can measure the raw signal well — for the best devices, in the conditions they are validated in — and it cannot reliably deliver the verdict it prints on the screen each morning. The measurement is largely solved for a top-tier device worn overnight; the interpretation is not. A readiness score is a real physiological measurement wearing the costume of a diagnosis, and the gap between those two things is exactly where the overclaim lives.

THE CASE AGAINST · THE OTHER READING

Do not throw the number out with the verdict.

The sceptical reading can be pushed too far. At rest and overnight — the conditions a readiness score is actually captured in — the best devices reach near-reference agreement with a clinical ECG; Oura's 0.99 is not noise, and Whoop's 0.94 is genuinely useful. For someone who wears the same device every night and reads the trend rather than the daily verdict, that number tracks something real: accumulating training load, a poor night's sleep, the early hours of an infection before it announces itself. The measurement is sound where the marketing says it is. The failure mode is over-reading one morning's figure, not the technology that produced it.

What the evidence supports, then, is a plain and slightly deflating conclusion. Device agreement with the clinical standard is device-dependent, running from 0.82 to 0.99, so the brand on the strap changes how much the reading is worth. That accuracy is conditional on stillness and rest, and falls away in motion. The resulting score frequently disconnects from how the wearer actually feels. And across all four findings, the number is most trustworthy read as a trend against your own baseline and least trustworthy read as a one-morning oracle. The Pixel Watch 5's Readiness Score, like Whoop's Recovery and Garmin's Training Readiness before it, is a real measurement dressed as a decision. Followed as a trend line over weeks, it earns its place on the wrist. Obeyed as a verdict handed down each morning, it will tell you, with total confidence, things it does not actually know.

The Session

FITNESS · BLOCK 3, VOLUME

A hypertrophy block lives or dies on accumulated hard sets — the working target is roughly 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week, and the muscle is built by adding that work back consistently, not by winning any single morning. The temptation with a wrist-worn readiness or recovery score — the daily number a Whoop, a Garmin, or the new Pixel Watch 5 all now hand out, weighing recent sleep and exertion into one figure — is to let a red reading cancel the day's planned volume. Land the caveat plainly: a 2026 study found that wearable-derived HRV and how people actually report feeling often disconnect, and warned against acting on HRV-based recommendations to steer health decisions. The single-morning number and a lifter's own sense of readiness frequently disagree — and on the evidence, neither is a reliable veto on the week's planned load.

So use the score the way the research supports: as a trend, not a traffic light. One low reading inside a volume block is noise; a run of them across several days, lining up with genuinely heavy legs or broken sleep, is signal worth a small deload — trimming a set or two per exercise rather than binning the session. Because the adaptation is driven by the total work across the week, protecting the weekly set count matters more than hitting every session on a green light. And when the metric and the body disagree, the body's read on whether a working set moved cleanly is the one to weight; the wrist score is context around that decision, not the instruction that makes it.

DO THIS WEEK · THE SESSION

Set the week's hard-set target first — around 10–20 per muscle — and read the morning readiness score as a trend across several days, never as a single-day verdict that cancels the plan.

Volume drives the hypertrophy adaptation, and the science shows one morning's HRV reading and how recovered a body actually is often disagree — so a run of low readings earns a small deload, a single one earns nothing.

Under the Burnham government's cost-of-living package, VAT on domestic electricity falls from 5% to 0% on 1 October, a straight cut to the unit price every home pays, and it lands automatically — nothing needs to be claimed.

That distinction sets the timing for any fixed-rate decision. With no cut expected before mid-September, the pricing on mortgages and savings today isn't waiting on an easing the Committee is tipped to withhold — there is no imminent Bank move to hold out for. A held rate is the one the market has already priced in, so a deal quoted today reflects that expectation rather than betting against it — waiting on the 17 September meeting to change the maths only makes sense if the Committee surprises, which it is not expected to. The two dates that actually matter for household money this autumn are the 17 September decision, when the Bank reports, and 1 October, when the electricity bill drops; everything else is a wait for a cut the market doesn't expect.

Every August the television studios refill with pundits — the ex-professionals and touchline theorists who hand down verdicts on a season that has barely kicked off. The word arrives so worn by the sports desk that it feels as though it were coined for it. It wasn't. Strip pundit back to its root and you find not a retired centre-half but a Sanskrit scholar, and a word that once carried real authority rather than the confident guesswork we hang on it now.

It began as paṇḍita, a Sanskrit word for a learned man — a scholar, someone who had genuinely mastered a subject. Sanskrit passed it down into Hindi and Urdu as paṇḍit, and it was in that form that English reached out and took it in the late 1600s. The first English pundit was no broadcaster. He was a scholar of Sanskrit law, brought into the colonial courtroom to advise English judges who could not read the texts they were expected to rule on. Their whole proceeding leaned on him: the pundit was the man who actually knew, the indispensable expert without whom the bench was blind.

That is the quiet irony now fossilised in everyday use. A pundit today is someone who talks with authority whether or not the knowledge is there to back it up; the original pundit was defined by exactly the depth of learning everyone around him lacked. And the slide happened surprisingly fast. By 1816 the word had already broken loose from the courtroom and the law, broadening to mean any learned expert or commentator — anyone who might be called on for a view. Once a word means "a person whose opinion is worth hearing," it is only a short walk to the person with an opinion, full stop.

So when the panel settles onto the sofa this weekend to pronounce on who will go up and who will go down, the label they wear is a fossil of somewhere much further east. It travelled from a Sanskrit manuscript to a Hindi honorific to a colonial law bench and, three centuries on, to a studio under stage lights — shedding its scholarship somewhere along the road, but keeping the one thing that always made it useful: the promise, sincere or not, that the person speaking knows more than the rest of us do.
