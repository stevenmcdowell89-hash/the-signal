A MONARCHY BY CONSENT
NOT THIS WEEK · A STANDING STORY · 1814–2026 · LATEST DEVELOPMENT SEP 2026

The kingdom that chose its king

On the first of September, in the assembly hall of the Storting, a man swears a single sentence of oath while his father lies in state. He becomes King Haakon VIII of Norway. He inherits almost no power at all — and a throne that exists for one strange reason: the Norwegian people once voted to have it.

BY THE EDITOR · OSLO · A STANDING STORY

The oath is short, and the Constitution fixes its words: in section nine the king promises to govern by the Constitution and its Laws. The ceremony is only that — no coronation, no crown, no anointing. What remains is a sentence, sworn in a working parliament by the least powerful head of state in Europe and one of the most secure. This king was not inherited but chosen: in 1905, Norwegians were asked whether they wanted a king, and said yes.

FIG. 01 The Storting, Oslo, pictured in 2016 — the working parliament where the king swears the Constitution's §9 oath, and where sovereignty has plainly rested for more than a century.Photo: Drlady · CC BY-SA 4.0 · Wikimedia Commons

That choice is the thread running through everything the monarchy has done since. A crown handed down by blood assumes its legitimacy; one handed over by a national vote must keep earning it. The result is a royal house that has spent 120 years being deliberately ordinary — a king who took the tram, a king who married a commoner, a king who cycled to his own sailboat. The ordinariness is not temperament. It is the job: a monarchy the people installed can be removed by the same people, and the family has governed itself accordingly.

The story begins earlier, in 1814, with a document. That spring, as the Napoleonic settlement carved up Europe, Norway seized a narrow gap between empires and wrote itself a constitution. Delegates gathered at Eidsvoll and produced one of the most democratic charters of its time — separation of powers, a broad franchise, sovereignty grounded in the people rather than the crown. Then the gap closed: the great powers forced Norway into union with Sweden under a shared king. The Norwegians kept their constitution and parliament but lost their independence, living for ninety-one years under a monarch in Stockholm they had not chosen. The 17th of May, the day the charter was signed, is still the national day — the country dates itself not from a king but from a text.

FIG. 02 Oscar Wergeland's painting of the 1814 constituent assembly at Eidsvoll, completed in 1885 and hung in the parliament. The charter came first; the king came ninety-one years later.Oscar Wergeland, 1885 · public domain · Wikimedia Commons

The union with Sweden dissolved in 1905, and it dissolved cleanly. The Storting declared it at an end, and when the question was put to the country in August, the vote was 368,208 for separation against 184 opposed — a result so lopsided it reads like a misprint. Norway was independent for the first time in centuries — and then did the surprising thing. Having freed itself from a foreign king, it went looking for one of its own, offering the throne to Prince Carl of Denmark: a grandson of the Danish monarch, married to a British princess, calculated to reassure the powers watching from outside.

Carl's answer set the institution's tone. He would not take a throne handed to him by politicians; if Norwegians were to have a king, they should first be asked whether they wanted one. So a second referendum was held that November — not on independence, but on the state's form. Around 79 percent chose monarchy over a republic. Only then did Carl accept. He landed in Oslo with his young son and, in a gesture that told the country what he understood, set aside his Danish name for an ancient Norse one: Haakon, the seventh of that name, reaching back to the last Norwegian king five centuries before. His son he named Olav — a dynasty Norwegian on arrival, because Norwegians had voted it into being.

FIG. 03 King Haakon VII, photographed in 1946, the year after he returned from exile. Born a Danish prince, he took an old Norse name and became the resistance's living symbol.National Library of Norway, 1946 · Wikimedia Commons

For three decades the chosen king was a quiet one. Haakon VII settled into the narrow constitutional role the 1814 charter left him, signing what his elected governments put before him and staying clear of politics. He learned the limits of the office and respected them, keeping to the margins through the constitutional crisis of 1928 and the lean interwar years. Whether the arrangement meant anything beyond ceremony — whether a king chosen to be decorative could ever be anything else — had never really been tested. Then, on an April morning in 1940, the test arrived in the shape of an invasion, and the answer he gave turned a constitutional formality into the founding legend of the modern crown.

German forces struck that spring, and within days their envoy delivered an ultimatum: the king was to appoint a collaborationist government under Vidkun Quisling and lend it the throne's authority. Haakon put the demand to his cabinet plainly. He would not do it. If the government felt bound to comply he would not stand in its way, but he would abdicate rather than sign — he could not, he said, place the crown behind a regime imposed by a foreign army. The stand is remembered ever since as "the King's No," and the ministers, who had come into the room uncertain, left having refused with him.

He fled north with his son and his ministers, was bombed by German aircraft at Nybergsund, and finally crossed to Britain, from where he broadcast to occupied Norway over the BBC through five years of war. His monogram, a simple H with a 7 threaded through it, was scrawled on walls and worn on lapels as a quiet signal of loyalty. A king the nation had elected turned out to be a king the nation would not give up. When Haakon returned to Oslo on 7 June 1945 — five years to the day after he had been forced to leave Norwegian soil — the reception was less about monarchy than about the man who had said no.

The lesson banked in those years was that the crown drew its authority from consent and conduct, not ceremony, and was worth most when it stood for the country rather than over it. His son Olav V, who succeeded in 1957, drew the same lesson and made it almost homely. During the 1973 oil crisis, with petrol rationed and Norwegians told to leave their cars home on Sundays, the king did exactly that. He was photographed boarding an ordinary Oslo tram, ski gear in hand, paying his fare like any other passenger. The image became one of the most beloved in Norwegian public life. Asked how he dared move around without bodyguards, he gave the answer that made him "folkekongen," the people's king.

The tram ride was less a stunt than a doctrine: a monarch installed by the people, and dependent on their goodwill, has every reason to share their inconveniences rather than escape them. When Olav died in 1991 the country mourned in a way that startled even Norwegians — thousands lit candles in the snow outside the palace, a spontaneous vigil for a king who had spent his reign proving how little distance he wanted from them. His son Harald V inherited the doctrine and pushed it further, having already spent nine years bending it into existence.

As a young crown prince Harald had fallen in love with Sonja Haraldsen, a commoner and a clothier's daughter, and he told his father and the government he would marry her or marry no one, holding the line until they relented in 1968 — a small revolution in a royal house that could easily have ended the line instead, and one the public backed more readily than the palace did. As king he presided over what Norwegians came to call the "bicycle monarchy": deliberately un-grand, low on pomp, a family that turned up to things and went home again. In 2016, at a summer gathering in the garden of his Skaugum estate, he set aside the usual diplomacy of royal speeches and said something plain about who his subjects were, and the words travelled far beyond the country.

Which brings the line to the man who swore the oath this month. Crown Prince Haakon had, in 2001, married Mette-Marit — not only a commoner but a single mother with a young son and a past the tabloids picked over. The marriage tested how far the bicycle monarchy could stretch, and it held; the couple stayed, worked, and outlasted the scandal until the country stopped counting it as one, and her son grew up inside the royal household without ever standing in the succession. A quarter of a century on, the frenzy of 2001 had curdled into something closer to affection, and the marriage that was supposed to be the monarchy's gravest modern risk had become another proof of the family's knack for absorbing the unroyal and carrying on.

So the accession, when it came, carried no drama of its own. Harald's death after a long reign was mourned quietly; the machinery of succession is written into the same Constitution the king swears to uphold, and it turned without a hitch. As his father lay in state, Haakon walked into the Storting, stood before the assembled representatives of the people who are, in the constitutional sense, the real sovereign, and repeated the sentence the charter requires — the same words his great-grandfather, grandfather and father had each spoken in that chamber before him.

The oath holds the arrangement: the king governs by instruments the Storting makes, not he. He signs what his government sends him, but decides nothing in his own name and can neither make law nor rule. The monarchy is a ceremony wrapped around a democracy — which is what lets it survive in a country this egalitarian: it asks no real authority, so threatens no principle.

And it survives for the reason it began: Norway voted for a king, twice in 1905, and has never voted to stop. A throne this unremarkable, kept by a democracy that goes on choosing it, is not a relic but a deliberate invention of modern European statecraft — and this month it changed hands exactly as designed.

THE CASE AGAINST · THE REPUBLICAN READING

An inherited office in a country that believes in earning things.

The counter-case is no caricature: polling puts republican support at a quarter to a third of the country. In one of the world's most egalitarian societies, where merit should outrank birth, a hereditary head of state is a standing exception. The 1905 vote bound people now long dead; no living Norwegian was asked, and consent that cannot be withdrawn is thin. That the king has almost no power is not a defence but the indictment: if the office does nothing, why keep an accident of ancestry over a president the country could choose? The modesty that makes the monarchy easy to love makes it just as easy to do without.

BAND 09 — BOOKMARK BOOKS · ON THE NIGHTSTAND

  • The Almost Nearly Perfect People

    Non-fiction · travel & society · sceptical tour

    Michael Booth spent years living among the Nordics, and this is his witty, clear-eyed account of what the happiness surveys leave out. He works country by country — Denmark's private debt and heavy drinking, Norway's oil money, Sweden's fierce conformity, Finland's guns and silence — and asks what the model actually costs the people inside it. The tone is sceptical rather than sour: he admires the trust and the safety net while refusing to swallow the brochure. It reads as the argument behind this issue's Long Read, told from the ground rather than the podium.

  • Chip War

    Non-fiction · history · semiconductors

    Chris Miller makes the case that the semiconductor is the single most important object in the modern economy, then proves it with history — the physicists, the fabs, and the trade routes that decide who holds power. He traces the industry from Cold War labs and the first integrated circuits to the choke points that now run through a handful of firms in Taiwan and the Netherlands. Along the way it explains, in plain terms, why a chip shortage can stall car factories and why governments treat foundries as strategic ground. Catnip for anyone who follows the tech that everything else quietly sits on top of.

  • Inverting the Pyramid

    Non-fiction · football · tactical history

    Jonathan Wilson's history of football tactics is the evergreen the depth readers keep coming back to. He tracks the game's shape from the early attacking pyramids through Italian catenaccio and Dutch total football to the high-pressing sides of today, and shows why each answer bred the next one to beat it. It is a genuine history — clubs, coaches, and the national styles that carried ideas across borders — not a coaching manual. Read it once and the Serie A and Premier League matches on the screen stop looking like chaos and start looking like arguments between systems.

BAND 10 — THIS WEEK IN HISTORY HISTORY · DATED

Shortly after midnight on Sunday 2 September 1666, a fire started in Thomas Farriner's bakery in Pudding Lane, near the north end of London Bridge. Farriner was baker to the King's navy, and his household escaped over the rooftops — all but a maid, who was too frightened to climb and became one of the fire's first victims. It was a small blaze in a narrow lane. Within four days it had destroyed most of the medieval City of London.

The city was built to burn. Houses were timber-framed and pitch-coated, jettied out so that upper storeys nearly touched across the street. That summer had been long and dry, the wooden buildings tinder-ready, and a strong east wind pushed the flames from house to house faster than anyone could pull them down. The standard defence against fire was to demolish buildings and starve it of fuel, but the Lord Mayor, Sir Thomas Bloodworth, hesitated at the cost and the property owners' objections. By the time King Charles II ordered wholesale demolition, the fire had outrun the firebreaks.

FIG. 01A contemporary Dutch-School painting of the Great Fire, c.1675, the burning city seen across the Thames.Dutch School, c.1675 · public domain · Wikimedia Commons

It burned for four days, from Sunday to Wednesday. It took roughly 13,200 houses, 87 parish churches, the halls of the livery companies, and the great Gothic cathedral of Old St Paul's, whose lead roof ran molten into the streets and whose stones cracked like gunfire. Around 70,000 of the City's 80,000 residents were left homeless, camped in the fields at Moorfields and Islington. Panic turned outward: with England at war with the Dutch and French, rumours spread that the fire was foreign sabotage, and a simple-minded French watchmaker named Robert Hubert falsely confessed and was hanged for it.

The strangest fact is the death toll. Only a handful of deaths were ever recorded — single figures — though historians suspect the poor and uncounted pushed the real number higher. What the fire truly destroyed was the old wooden city. Rebuilding was ordered in brick and stone, along widened streets; Christopher Wren designed 51 new churches and a new St Paul's, and his Monument, a 202-foot Doric column raised between 1671 and 1676, still stands exactly that distance from where Farriner's oven first caught.

The same early-September days carry another kind of fire. On 1–2 September 1859, Earth was hit by the most intense geomagnetic storm on record, now called the Carrington Event. The day before, the English astronomer Richard Carrington had watched a brilliant white flare erupt across a sunspot group — the first solar flare ever observed. The charged particles it flung out reached Earth in under a day. Auroras normally confined to the poles were seen as far south as the Caribbean, bright enough to read a newspaper by. Telegraph systems, the era's cutting-edge network, sparked and failed; some operators got shocks, and a few sent messages with their batteries disconnected, running on the current the storm itself induced in the wires.

FIG. 02A solar flare imaged by NASA in 2014 — a modern echo of the 1859 storm Carrington witnessed.NASA/SDO · public domain

A Carrington-scale storm today would find far more to break. The same induced currents that fried telegraph lines could overload high-voltage grid transformers, knocking out power for weeks; GPS and communications satellites could be scrambled or lost; and aircraft and pipelines would feel it too. Space-weather agencies now watch the Sun precisely because 1859 is not a one-off — it is a warning.

BAND 11 — THE DESK SERVICE

The Session

TRAINING · HYPERTROPHY BLOCK 3

Block 3 is a growth block, and the question every growth block drags back in is how hard each set has to hurt. The gym folklore says grind to failure or the set doesn't count. The evidence says otherwise, and it says it plainly: you do not have to train to failure to build muscle. A 2023 Sports Medicine review pooled the failure-versus-stopping-short trials and found only a trivial edge for going all the way — an effect size of roughly 0.19, small enough that it disappears under normal training noise. A 2024 meta-regression pushed the same way: hypertrophy is fairly forgiving of leaving 1–2 reps in reserve, so a set that stops just shy of failure grows nearly as much muscle as one taken to the bitter end.

The qualifiers are worth stating, because they're where the judgement lives. Maximal strength looks less forgiving — the closer a heavy set gets to failure, the better it seems to serve a one-rep max, so a strength phase reads differently from a growth one. And "stop short" only works if the reserve is honest; two reps in reserve means two clean reps genuinely left, not a guess made to duck the hard part. Recovery hardware — a Whoop recovery score, a Garmin readiness figure — is useful context for how hard a given day can be pushed, a reason to sit at the cautious end of the range when the number is red. It is a reading, not an instruction; the set and the rep target still come from the plan.

DO THIS WEEK · THE SESSION

Cap your compound sets at 1–2 reps in reserve this week; save true failure for the last set of an isolation move.

That's the criterion the research supports — most of the growth for a fraction of the fatigue and joint cost, with failure spent only where it's cheap.

Everything on the money page this month points at one date: Thursday 17 September, when the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee next rules on the base rate. The market lean is toward easing, and that expectation is already doing work before the vote. For savers, best-buy fixed rates tend to soften ahead of an anticipated cut — banks price the future, not the present — so a fix locked today can beat one waited for, because the headline rates on offer are more likely to slip than climb. For borrowers, the meeting matters most to anyone on a tracker or standard variable rate, where a cut feeds through to the monthly payment almost at once; those on a fix feel nothing until it ends.

Two cost-of-living measures sit alongside the decision. From 1 October, VAT comes off domestic electricity, dropping the 5% charge to zero and shaving a slice off every power bill. And the £2 bus fare cap holds the price of a single journey down. Neither turns on the rate vote, but both land in the same autumn budgeting window, and both are worth folding into the sums before any fixing decision — the electricity change in particular quietly loosens the monthly squeeze that makes a fix feel urgent.
