A TELESCOPE MADE OF ICE

Catching ghost particles at the South Pole

This week the Nobel Prize in Physics went to Francis Halzen for an instrument that sounds like a joke: a cubic kilometre of Antarctic glacier, wired with light sensors, built to catch a particle that almost nothing can catch. It has given astronomy a new way to see.

BY THE EDITOR · THE LONG READ · 11 OCT 2026

On 6 October the Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to Francis Halzen for the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy astrophysical neutrinos. The device Halzen spent decades arguing for is a cubic kilometre of clear ice beneath the Amundsen-Scott South Pole Station, turned into the largest particle detector ever built. The idea behind it — bury a telescope in a glacier and aim it down, through the planet — was once close to unfundable, because the thing it hunts is the most elusive particle physicists know of. The prize is confirmation that the gamble worked: the ice now catches messengers from the most violent engines in the universe, and they arrive carrying information nothing else can deliver.

The particle is the neutrino, and its defining quality is that it barely engages with matter at all. Neutrinos are near-massless and carry no electric charge, so the forces that stop ordinary particles — the push and pull of charge, collisions with atoms — mostly do not apply to them. They pass through solid matter as if it were empty. The scale of this is hard to hold in the head: roughly 100 trillion neutrinos from the Sun stream through every human body every second, day and night, arriving from overhead by day and straight up through the whole Earth by night, and not one of them leaves a trace you could feel. They are not rare. They are simply, almost perfectly, indifferent to everything they touch.

That indifference is exactly what makes them precious to astronomers. The two messengers we have used to study the distant universe are both compromised in transit. Light — across every wavelength — is absorbed and scattered by the gas and dust between the stars, so the interiors of the objects that interest us most are hidden behind their own debris. Charged cosmic rays are worse: because they carry charge, the magnetic fields threaded through the galaxy bend their paths, and by the time they reach us they have forgotten where they came from. A neutrino has neither problem. It flies in a dead straight line from its source, passes through gas, dust and stars without deflection, and arrives pointing back exactly the way it came. Each one is an undistorted address. The only difficulty — and it is the entire difficulty — is catching it.

FIG. 01 The observatory as a block of instrumented ice: 86 vertical strings hung with light sensors turn a cubic kilometre of glacier beneath the South Pole station into a single detector, with the array as completed in 2010.IceCube/NSF · CC BY 4.0 · via Wikimedia Commons

IceCube solves the catching problem by brute scale, and by a trick of geometry. If a neutrino interacts only vanishingly rarely, then the way to see any interactions at all is to watch an enormous quantity of matter and wait. The instrument turns one cubic kilometre of deep Antarctic ice into that watched matter — clear, dark, and old, the cleanest large volume of transparent solid available anywhere on Earth. The planet itself does the second job. Neutrinos pass through the Earth freely, while almost every other particle from space is stopped by thousands of kilometres of rock. So the detector can look downward, using the whole globe as a filter that screens out the noise and lets only neutrinos through from below. The Earth is the filter; the ice is the camera.

What the camera actually records is a flash of light. On the rare occasion that a neutrino does strike a nucleus in the ice, the collision produces a fast-moving charged particle travelling, briefly, faster than light itself moves through ice. That particle drags a faint cone of blue light behind it — Cherenkov radiation, the optical equivalent of a sonic boom. In the clear deep glacier that flash can travel a long way before fading, and the sensors buried in the ice read its timing and its brightness. From the pattern — which sensors lit first, how bright, in what order — the collaboration reconstructs the direction the neutrino came from and the energy it was carrying. A particle that touches nothing is caught by the single photon-scale bruise it leaves on its way through.

The engineering that makes this readable is as austere as the physics. Beneath the South Pole station, 86 cables — "strings" — were sunk up to 2.5 kilometres straight down into the ice. Strung along them, spaced through the deepest and clearest ice, hang roughly 5,160 light sensors, the digital optical modules, each a glass sphere about the size of a basketball housing a photomultiplier sensitive enough to register individual photons. Together those 5,160 eyes instrument the full cubic kilometre, so that a flash anywhere inside the block is seen by enough of them, from enough angles, to be located in three dimensions. There is no lens and no mirror. The telescope is a grid of sensors frozen into a glacier, and its optics are the ice.

FIG. 02 Looking straight down on the array in this 2013 layout: each marker is one of the 86 in-ice strings, with the IceTop surface stations paired above them, together covering roughly a square kilometre at the surface.IceCube Collaboration · CC BY 4.0 · via Wikimedia Commons

Building it took the better part of a decade and a run of Antarctic summers, the only season in which the Pole can be worked at all. Construction ran from 2005 to 2010, string by string, until the full kilometre was wired and sealed into the ice where it will sit, undisturbed, for as long as the glacier holds. The timeline of what followed is short enough to set out in three dates, and each one is a step up in ambition — from switching the instrument on, to proving it could see past the solar system, to reading a single particle back to the object that fired it.

The first of those dates is the one that justified the whole enterprise. In 2013 IceCube reported the detection of high-energy neutrinos arriving from beyond the solar system — particles carrying far more energy than the Sun or the Earth's own atmosphere can produce, which meant they had been made somewhere out in the wider universe, by processes violent enough to fling matter around at close to the speed of light. Until then, astrophysical neutrinos at these energies were a theoretical expectation. After 2013 they were an observed population. The cosmos, it turned out, really does send neutrinos, and the ice really could catch them. What it could not yet do was say where any single one had come from.

That came in 2018, and it is the result the Nobel citation rests on. IceCube caught a high-energy neutrino and, working with telescopes watching the same patch of sky, traced it back to a specific object: a blazar catalogued as TXS 0506+056 — a distant galaxy with a supermassive black hole at its centre, firing a jet of matter and radiation almost straight at Earth. It was the first time a neutrino had ever given a cosmic source an address. A single near-massless particle, caught by a flash of blue light in the ice, had been followed home to the engine that made it, and other instruments then found that engine flaring in light at the same time. The messenger and the picture agreed.

That agreement is the real prize, and it has a name: multi-messenger astronomy. For most of its history, astronomy had one messenger, light, and read the universe entirely by the radiation that reached our eyes and telescopes. In the last decade it has gained two more. Gravitational waves — ripples in spacetime — were caught for the first time in 2015, giving a way to feel violent events rather than see them. Neutrinos are the third messenger, and the one that reaches us from places the other two cannot describe: out of the dense, opaque cores of the objects doing the work, where light is trapped and only a particle that ignores matter can escape. When the same event is caught in light, in gravitational waves and in neutrinos at once, each fills in what the others miss, and the picture is more complete than any single sense could build.

A particle that passes through the whole Earth without noticing it is, once a year or so, caught by the faint blue bruise it leaves in a glacier — and followed home. TRANSMISSION №029 · THE LONG READ

Set the pieces side by side and the absurdity resolves into a method. Neutrinos are useless to catch and perfect to follow — almost nothing stops them, which is why they arrive honest and undeflected from the far side of the universe, and why you need a cubic kilometre of the clearest ice on Earth and five thousand frozen eyes to register the handful that interact. Bury the detector in a glacier and aim it down, and the Earth becomes a filter that throws away everything but neutrinos, while the ice becomes a camera that turns each rare strike into a flash of light you can time and trace. That is what the 2026 Physics prize honours: not a bigger telescope pointed at the sky, but a new sense altogether, with the planet for a lens and a glacier for a film, reading the universe in the one particle that refuses to be stopped.

BAND 10 — THIS WEEK IN HISTORY HISTORY · DATED

On 14 October 1066 — 960 years ago this week — a Norman duke and an English king drew up their armies on a ridge near Hastings and fought from mid-morning until dusk for the crown of England. It was an unusually long battle for the age, when most were settled inside an hour or two; this one ground on through the whole of a short autumn day. By nightfall the English line had broken, King Harold Godwinson lay dead on the field, and William, Duke of Normandy, had a kingdom. Few single days reach so far forward. The shape of who ruled England, who held its land, and even which words its people spoke all turned on this one.

FIG.Scene 57 of the Bayeux Tapestry — Harold rex interfectus est, "King Harold is killed". The "Tapestry" is not a tapestry at all but a wool embroidery some 70 metres long, stitched onto linen; it is the campaign's primary visual source, a near-contemporary account rather than a record made on the day. The image here is a 2007 photograph of the surviving embroidery.Bayeux Museum · public domain · Wikimedia Commons

The throne had been contested all year, and Harold's grip on it was never secure. The strange thing about the autumn of 1066 is that he had to defend it at both ends of his kingdom almost at once. On 25 September, in the north at Stamford Bridge, he destroyed a Norwegian invasion in a battle hard enough to be remembered on its own account. Then, with the victory barely won, word reached him that William had landed on the south coast. Harold turned his tired army around and marched it back down the length of England. Two full-scale battles at opposite ends of the country inside three weeks is a punishing demand to put on one army, and the sheer pace of it is part of why the second went the way it did: the men who formed up near Hastings had already fought and walked a very long way to get there.

The armies that met on 14 October were not vast — estimates run to something like 7,000 to 12,000 on the Norman side against perhaps 5,000 to 13,000 English — but they were matched closely enough to make for a grinding contest. Harold took the high ground on Senlac ridge, his men packed shoulder to shoulder behind a wall of shields; William's Normans, with their archers and their mounted knights, had to come at that wall uphill. For most of the day it held. What finally undid it, as the Bayeux embroidery tells the story, was discipline breaking — stretches of the English line spilling forward to chase a Norman retreat and losing the shape that had protected them. At the last, Harold himself was struck down, and with the king dead the English resistance on the field collapsed into rout.

FIG.The dispositions at Hastings, 14 October 1066 — Harold's line along the ridge at Senlac, William's Normans attacking uphill from the south, drawn in this 2008 battle map.Wikimedia Commons · CC BY-SA 4.0

William did not so much become king that afternoon as spend the following weeks making himself one; he was crowned on Christmas Day 1066. What came after was less a change of monarch than a change of country. The old English aristocracy was swept aside and replaced, almost wholesale, by a Norman ruling class. Landholding was rewired from the top down, with the realm's estates parcelled out afresh to the men who had won them. And the language of power turned to French, bending English itself toward it for centuries, so that the tongue read a thousand years later still carries the join. The ground itself kept the mark too: the place is remembered so completely by the day fought on it that the town which grew there is called, simply, Battle. One long day on a Sussex hill, and England wore the marks of it ever after — in who ruled, in who held the land, and in the words its people spoke.

FITNESS · REST INTERVALS

How long should you rest between hypertrophy sets? About two minutes is a sensible default — but the more useful finding is that you don't need to watch the clock at all. A 2024 Bayesian meta-analysis (Singer et al., pooling nine randomised trials) compared short rests of 60 seconds or less against longer rests above 60 seconds, and found the muscle-growth gap near-trivial: a standardised effect of roughly 0.48 for short rests against 0.56 for longer ones, with credible intervals that overlap almost completely. There's a small edge once you clear the 60-to-90-second mark, and essentially nothing more to gain by stretching rests out further.

FIG. The pooled effect sizes for short and longer rests sit side by side with overlapping intervals — the difference is inside the noise, in this figure from the 2024 meta-analysis.Singer et al. · Frontiers in Sports and Active Living · CC BY 4.0

The honest read is that rest length is a weak lever for size, not the hidden variable it's often sold as. Where longer rests earn their keep is the set itself: given more recovery you complete the planned reps with cleaner technique and a touch more load, and on the compound lifts — squats, rows, presses — that preserved quality is worth the extra half-minute. On isolation and accessory work the cost of a short rest is smaller still, so there's no reason to pad it out. It's worth being clear about what the trials measured, too: these were direct hypertrophy comparisons — muscle thickness and cross-sectional area over blocks of several weeks — so the finding is specifically that total muscle grown barely moves with rest length once training is otherwise matched. The old "short rests for size" advice leaned on an acute hormonal spike that never translated into more tissue. Volume and effort do the work; the clock is a rounding error.

DO THIS WEEK · THE SESSION

On your next two accessory sessions, rest around 90 seconds to two minutes and stop timing it to the second — go by whether your breathing has settled and the next set feels genuinely repeatable.

The meta-analysis puts the hypertrophy gap between short and longer rests inside the noise once you clear ~60–90s, so the attention you'd spend counting is better spent on the reps that actually drive the adaptation.
