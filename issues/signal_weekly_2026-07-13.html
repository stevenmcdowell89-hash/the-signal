SEVEN MONTHS THAT NEVER ENDED

The night the world went live

Sixty-four years ago this week, a beach-ball of transistors was flung into orbit and, for a few minutes, let two continents look each other in the eye. It died before Christmas. The idea it carried has not switched off since.

BY THE EDITOR · THE LONG READ · 13 JUL 2026

On the night of 10 July 1962, in a windowless control room in Andover, Maine, a handful of engineers watched a screen fill with the Statue of Liberty and understood, before anyone said it aloud, that distance had just changed shape. The image had crossed the Atlantic not by cable and not by post but through the sky, bounced off a machine that had been in space for a matter of hours. In Pleumeur-Bodou, on the coast of Brittany, a giant horn antenna under a canvas dome swung to catch the same faint signal, and France saw America live. It was the first time a moving picture had made that crossing in real time. The whole exchange lasted only as long as the satellite stayed in view of both shores — a window of minutes — and then it was gone, over the horizon, and the world went dark again until the next orbit brought it back.

The machine was called Telstar 1, and it is worth describing plainly, because the plainness is the point. It was a sphere thirty-four and a half inches across, weighing a hundred and seventy-one pounds, its aluminium skin studded with three thousand six hundred solar cells and its insides packed with the still-exotic technology of the transistor. Bell Labs built it. AT&T paid for it, reimbursing NASA for the launch under a formal agreement — an arrangement that made Telstar the first privately financed satellite, a piece of the sky bought and flown by a telephone company. It went up on a Delta rocket from Cape Canaveral, and within the same working day it was already doing the thing it had been made to do, relaying television, telephone calls and fax between two continents that had never before spoken face to face at the speed of light.

What it relayed that first week reads now like a mixtape of a civilisation introducing itself. The Eiffel Tower. A reference to President Kennedy. A clip of a baseball game between the Phillies and the Cubs, sent east so that Europe could watch Americans watch America. Singers, officials, the small ceremonial noises a species makes when it is nervous and proud at once. None of it mattered as content. All of it mattered as proof. The signal was the message: here we are, and now you can see us while it happens.

FIG. 01 Telstar 1, the 1962 sphere that relayed the first live transatlantic television signals.NASA / AT&T · public domain · Wikimedia Commons

To feel why a nation held its breath over a telephone company's science project, you have to stand in 1962. The Cuban missile crisis was three months away. Kennedy had promised the country the Moon the previous September, and the space race was less a race than a public referendum on which system — American or Soviet — actually worked. The Soviets had reached space first with Sputnik in 1957 and put the first man into orbit in 1961; every American launch since had carried the weight of catching up. Telstar arrived into that mood as a rare piece of good news that anyone could understand. It did not carry a warhead or a cosmonaut. It carried the evening's television, and it let ordinary people on both sides of an ocean watch the same live moment at the same instant. In a decade organised around the fear that the sky had become a place bombs came from, here was a thing in the sky that did the opposite — it made strangers visible to one another. The newspapers of that fortnight caught the shift in tone precisely. Where a satellite launch had until then been read as a move in a military game, Telstar was written up as an event the whole family could gather round, a picture from across the sea rather than a threat from above it. For a few weeks the same technology that everyone feared could end the world was recast as the technology that might, finally, let the world talk to itself.

The signal was the message. Not what crossed the Atlantic, but that anything could — live, while it happened, with the horizon as the only clock. TRANSMISSION №016 · THE LONG READ

It is easy, from here, to miss how strange and unfinished this first version of the live planet was. Telstar did not hang motionless over the ocean the way the satellites that carry our lives now do. It orbited low and fast, circling the Earth roughly every two and a half hours, and it could only pass a signal between Maine and Brittany during the brief stretch of each orbit when it happened to be above the curve of the world for both at once. The always-on connection we treat as a law of nature — the permanent, invisible switchboard in the sky — did not yet exist. In 1962 the world could watch itself live only in glimpses, on a timetable set by orbital mechanics, in windows you had to book and wait for. The miracle came with a stopwatch attached.

The answer to that problem had already been written down, seventeen years earlier, by a man who had no way to build it. In 1945 the engineer and writer Arthur C. Clarke published a short paper arguing that a satellite placed far enough out — roughly twenty-two thousand miles up — would circle the Earth in exactly one day, and so appear to hang still over a single spot on the ground. Three such stations, he reasoned, could blanket the whole planet in permanent radio contact. It was a beautiful idea with no hardware attached, and it stayed that way for a generation. Telstar was not that idea. Telstar was the cheaper, nearer, low-flying version you could actually launch in 1962 with the rockets that existed — a satellite you had to chase across the sky rather than one that waited patiently overhead. Clarke's vision would be realised only the following year, when a far smaller satellite reached the high orbit he had described and, for the first time, simply stayed put.

Around all of this ran a quieter contest over who should own the sky once it worked. Telstar was AT&T's, and a private telephone monopoly holding the keys to global broadcasting made Washington uneasy. The argument came to a head the same summer the satellite flew. On 31 August 1962, six weeks after that first live night, Kennedy signed the Communications Satellite Act, which created a new, government-chartered company — Comsat — to own America's share of any international satellite system, deliberately preventing AT&T from turning its head start into a permanent grip. The politics mattered as much as the physics. The question of 1962 was not only whether a planet could watch itself live, but who would hold the switch, and on what terms the rest of the world would be allowed to plug in. The machine settled the first question in an afternoon. The second is, in a sense, still open.

Then there is the detail that turns the whole story from triumph into something stranger and sadder. The day before Telstar launched, on 9 July 1962, the United States detonated a nuclear warhead high above the Pacific — the Starfish Prime test, a 1.4-megaton bomb set off some two hundred and fifty miles up, at the edge of space, to see what would happen. What happened was that it pumped the upper atmosphere full of charged particles, strengthening a belt of radiation that had not been at that intensity when Telstar's design was signed off. The satellite flew, the very next day, straight into a hazard its own government had helped create hours earlier. Over the following months that radiation gnawed at the delicate transistors Telstar depended on. Its command system began to fail in November; engineers coaxed it back to partial life for a few more weeks, and then it went silent for good in February 1963, roughly seven months after that first live night. One of the most consequential broadcasting devices ever built was, in effect, poisoned by a bomb fired the day before it was born.

What killed Telstar could not touch the idea, because the idea had already escaped the hardware, and the hardware behind it improved with startling speed. Telstar 2, launched in May 1963, flew in a higher orbit that dodged the worst of the radiation. NASA's own Relay satellites were doing similar work. Then, in July 1963, a small satellite called Syncom 2 reached the high, slow orbit Clarke had described and became the first to hover in step with the Earth's rotation; its successor, Syncom 3, went fully geostationary the following year and carried the 1964 Tokyo Olympics live across the Pacific. By 1965 the first commercial version — Early Bird, the founding satellite of what became the Intelsat network — was parked permanently over the Atlantic, doing continuously what Telstar had only managed in stolen minutes. In the space of three years the racing, short-lived sphere had been made obsolete by machines that simply stayed where you pointed them. Telstar was the proof of concept that its own children rendered quaint.

FIG. 02 A later Telstar deploys from Space Shuttle Discovery in 1984 — the programme, and the name, long outlived the original.NASA · Johnson Space Center · public domain

The name outlived the machine in more ways than one. There are still satellites called Telstar in orbit today, sixty years on, run by a company that traces its lineage back to that first sphere; the word became a brand that kept flying long after the original had gone dark. It also became a hit record within weeks of the launch, a British instrumental that turned the sound of the space age into something you could dance to — but that tune has a story worth telling properly, and it waits at the end of this issue. What matters here is that the name attached itself to a feeling as much as to a thing, and the feeling was the sense that the sky had quietly become an address you could reach.

That is the layer time gives this story. We tell the history of technology as a history of objects that work, and Telstar barely did — a photogenic failure, dead by spring. But the thing it switched on was never the machine. It was the habit of a planet watching itself in real time, and that habit has only deepened until it disappeared into the ordinary. Look up now and there are thousands of satellites overhead, whole constellations of them, holding a live connection so constant that no one thinks to call it live at all. We watch a match played on another continent, join a face on the far side of the world, carry the whole switchboard in a pocket, and none of it registers as a marvel. That flatness, that total lack of wonder, is Telstar's real monument. The permanent, effortless, everywhere connection we now barely notice began as a window of minutes, watched by men in a shed in Maine who knew they were seeing the future flicker past before it dropped below the horizon. They had to wait for the next orbit. We never have to wait at all, and that is exactly how you know they won.

THE CASE AGAINST · THE OTHER READING

Maybe the myth outran the hardware.

Telstar makes a lovely turning point, but history is rarely so tidy, and the case against the legend is strong. Echo 1, a passive reflecting balloon, had already bounced signals across the sky two years earlier. The transatlantic telephone cable had been carrying voices under the ocean since 1956. And Telstar's real successor — the geostationary satellite that actually built the always-on world, hanging fixed above one spot rather than racing round in low orbit — arrived within a year, in the shape of Syncom, and was itself outrun by Early Bird inside three. On this reading, Telstar was not the breakthrough but the photograph of one: the charismatic, well-financed, well-publicised object that a telephone company was delighted to put on the news, standing in for a change that was coming anyway from a dozen directions. The first live picture is a better story than the first workable architecture, and stories are what survive. The honest verdict may be that Telstar mattered most for what it looked like — proof you could see — rather than for what it built. Which, for a device whose entire purpose was to let the world watch itself, is perhaps not a demotion at all.

98.4 · WIMBLEDON · THE FINAL

Sinner keeps the crown

Jannik Sinner beat Alexander Zverev 6-7(7), 7-6(2), 6-3, 6-4 to defend his title — a fifth Grand Slam, and only the tenth man in the Open Era to retain the gentlemen's crown. It was not a rout. Zverev took a tight opening set on the tie-break and pushed the second to the same place before Sinner steadied, reeled off the breaker, and never trailed again; from two sets to one up the outcome felt settled long before it was. Zverev handed him the compliment straight afterwards: "He's still the best player in the world, I do believe that… probably three guys who can challenge him." Sinner returned it, calling Zverev's game one that's "growing and growing." The day before, Linda Nosková came through an all-Czech women's final over Karolína Muchová 6-2, 5-7, 6-3 — a first Slam for a player who had never previously gone past a major quarter-final. Two champions in two days, one of them defending, one of them arriving. SW19, retained.

BAND 10 — THIS WEEK IN HISTORY HISTORY · DATED

It began, as so many great institutions do, with a broken bit of garden machinery. In the summer of 1877 the All England Croquet Club — a genteel outfit on Worple Road in Wimbledon, its lawns kept smooth by a pony-drawn roller — found itself short of the money to mend that roller when it broke. The committee's solution was to add three words to its name, "and Lawn Tennis", and to stage a tournament. Entry was a guinea. Twenty-two men signed up. Nobody in the room can have imagined they were founding anything at all, let alone the oldest tournament in the sport.

FIG.Spencer Gore — surveyor, racquets man, and in July 1877 the first Wimbledon champion. He remained convinced lawn tennis had no future.Wikimedia Commons · public domain

The championship opened on 9 July and then, in a fixture of the English summer that has never quite gone away, it rained. The final was pushed back a few days — partly by the weather, partly by the small matter of Eton playing Harrow at cricket over at Lord's, which no self-respecting gentleman of the period would have dreamt of missing. When play finally resumed on the 19th, the man who walked off with the title was Spencer Gore: twenty-seven years old, a surveyor by trade and a racquets player by instinct, who beat William Marshall in straight sets. Around two hundred spectators paid a shilling each to watch. The winner took twelve guineas and a silver cup; the roller, one assumes, got its repair.

What matters more than the result is the quiet committee work behind it. A group of enthusiasts, Henry Jones among them, spent that summer fixing the things we now take entirely for granted — the rectangular court, the height of the net, the way the service works. They were, in effect, writing the rules of a game as they went, and they got them close enough to right that we are still playing inside those lines a century and a half later. The game itself moved on faster than its first champion expected. Gore had won by rushing the net and volleying everything back before it could bounce; within a season or two, opponents had worked out the lob, and his brisk style was undone. He was, by all accounts, thoroughly unconvinced that lawn tennis had any future at all. Few men have been so successfully and so publicly wrong.

Which is the part I keep turning over. The same fortnight of July, the same stubborn grass, the same drizzle interrupting play — and this week Jannik Sinner defended his crown on that very lawn in front of a Centre Court full to the rafters and a broadcast audience scattered clean across the planet. What began as a whip-round to fix a pony roller is now beamed live to every continent at once, the kind of reach that would have seemed pure sorcery to Henry Jones and his net measurements. There is a neat rhyme in it: the reach we now take entirely for granted started here too — with two hundred people, a shilling apiece, and a game its own winner thought would never catch on.

The marathon is done, the holiday's over, and Block 3 opened on 8 July with a different job entirely: build muscle, eating at maintenance to a slight surplus. The temptation in week one is to come out swinging with maximum sets. The volume-landmark model says the opposite — start near your minimum effective volume and climb toward maximum adaptive volume across the block, adding sets week by week. A 2024 Journal of Applied Physiology trial backed this in trained men: progressively increasing weekly sets beat holding volume fixed for hypertrophy.

Muscle is hard to build in a deficit, so eat at maintenance to a slight surplus — roughly 5–10% over — with 1.6–2.2 g of protein per kilo. And coming off a week of active-recovery holiday, the controlled ramp does double duty: it lets the tissue re-adapt before the hard weeks arrive.

TUMBLE INTO Telstar, the No.1 that outlived it

Within weeks of Telstar 1 going up, the eccentric British producer Joe Meek released an instrumental called "Telstar" — credited to The Tornados — built to sound like a satellite singing. It became the first record by a British group to top the US Billboard Hot 100. He made it not in a plush studio but in a rented flat above a leather-goods shop on Holloway Road, layering tape loops, spring reverb, distortion and a clavioline, an early electronic keyboard. He was a bedroom producer decades before the term existed. Follow his Wikipedia entry and the thread runs straight through the clavioline and the Mellotron, the BBC Radiophonic Workshop, Delia Derbyshire and the Doctor Who theme, the early Moog — right down to the neon-and-Carpenter synthwave still being made today. Open the one tab. Surface an hour later, three tabs deep, with a new favourite dead genius.
