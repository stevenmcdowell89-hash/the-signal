Foreword

Efteling & Beekse Bergen, Again

Sixteen sleeps to a trip taken once before — and the whole point this time is the room to do it properly.

Efteling & Beekse Bergen is not a first visit. The floating castle in Kaatsheuvel and the savannah park at Hilvarenbeek were both seen once already, on a clock that ran too fast. The castle got a single day. The savannah got four nights. Both ended with a list of things left undone. This is the trip that goes back to finish them.

The difference is measured in nights. Two of them now sit inside Efteling's gates rather than one rushed daytrip, which turns the park from a sprint into a place worth slowing down in — the evening half of it reachable at last, the early-morning half waiting before the queues arrive. Beekse Bergen gains a night too, five instead of four, the kind of margin that lets a wildlife park stop being a checklist and start being somewhere lived in for a week.

Returning visitors tend to find Efteling exactly where they left it. The Efteling still breathes that same sense of beautiful, whimsical design as it did when I was little, writes Esther van de Paal — a continuity that rewards a second look rather than punishing it. And the park has not stood entirely still: Hooghmoed, a gold-smeltery drop-tower complex, opened beside Baron 1898 this May, new ground for anyone who was last here before it existed.

Seven nights, two estates, 30 June to 7 July. The first visit was a reconnaissance. This one is the return — slower, fuller, and finally given the time it always wanted.

Part One

Efteling

the floating castle · opened 1952 · two nights

Two nights, not one. That is the change the first half of this trip turns on. The last visit gave Efteling a single day, gates to closing, and ran out of daylight before it ran out of park. This time the indigo storybook stays open into the dark.

Efteling opened in 1952 and is one of the oldest theme parks in the world, and it has never read like its American cousins. The register here is celestial rather than corporate: a moon with a face, a castle that seems to float, fairy tales walked rather than queued. First-timers tend to reach for the same two words. Lydia Storks of the Disney Food Blog landed on "weird and wonderful" and meant both as the highest praise.

What follows in this half is the case for the overnight, the rides worth the queue and the early access, the living world of the Sprookjesbos, and the Wonder Hotel that keeps you inside the gates. Sixteen sleeps out, the floating castle is the first place this countdown lands.

The case for the overnight

Why Two Nights Changes Efteling

The park a single day runs out of daylight for.

Last time, Efteling was one day. Gates at opening, the long walk back to the car park before the lake had even gone dark. A single day is a sprint: you ride what you can, eat on your feet, and leave before the park becomes itself. Two nights at the Wonder Hotel buy back the hours a day-trip never sees — dusk in the Fairytale Forest, the late show on the water, and two slow mornings inside the gates with the crowds still queuing outside.

The headline is the water. Aquanura, opened in 2012 and one of the largest fountain-and-light shows in Europe, plays out on the Vijver lake at the very end of the day — jets, colour and the occasional blast of fire scored to music. It is staged after closing time for most day visitors, which is exactly why it slips off a single-day plan. Stay over and it becomes the obvious last act, watched from the lakeside with nowhere to rush off to afterward.

The Park After Dark

The lit dark rides are a different ride at night. Droomvlucht, a suspended flight through fairy-tale forests since 1993, is reckoned one of the prettiest dark rides anywhere — and prettier still once the daylight has gone out of the park around it.

The overnight also reshapes the mornings. Wonder Hotel guests get a half-hour head start into the park before the public each day, so two nights means two unhurried openings — Symbolica or Baron 1898 walked straight onto while the turnstiles outside are still filling. The evenings belong to guests too: when the day crowds drain out, the Sprookjesbos goes quiet and the lit storybook scenes hold the light. A single day spends its dusk in traffic; an overnight spends it inside the gates.

None of this needs a schedule. The point of going back with more time is precisely that there is no rush to make: a second morning to revisit a favourite, a late hour to wander the lit forest, and a fountain show that no longer has to be missed for the drive home.

Lore & Legend

A Universe You Can Step Into

Most theme parks build rides and hang a theme on them afterwards. Efteling did it the other way round. Before there was a single coaster, there was a wood — the Sprookjesbos, the Fairytale Forest — and everything the park has become since has grown out of those trees. Walking in is less like arriving at an attraction than stepping inside a book someone has been writing for seventy years.

The Forest Came First

The Sprookjesbos has been the heart of the park since 1952, holding around thirty walk-through fairy tales tucked among the trees — and it still sets the rule the whole place obeys.

Where it begins Sprookjesbos, est. 1952 What’s in there ~30 walk-through tales
step inside
the forest sets the rule

The look is not an accident, and it is not borrowed. It belongs almost entirely to one man. The illustrator Anton Pieck drew the forest's crooked roofs, its gnarled timbers and its impossible little cottages, and the park has spent decades refusing to let go of that hand. Sleeping Beauty lies behind a hedge of real thorns. A red-cloaked girl meets a wolf in a clearing. The Six Servants and the Talking Parrot mutter to passers-by. Nothing is sleek; everything looks slightly older than it can possibly be, as if the buildings had been weathering there since long before anyone bought a ticket. One returning visitor sums up the trick neatly — that it is "a beautiful large area with magical fairy tales character and mini buildings complete with animatronics around every corner."

What keeps it from feeling like a museum is that the world talks back. Holle Bolle Gijs sits where the paths meet — a round, ever-hungry figure who happens to be a working litter bin. Drop a wrapper in and a voice booms up from inside: "Papier hier!" — paper here, more please. Children spend whole afternoons feeding him, and that is the point. The bins are not bins disguised as a character; they are a character who is genuinely glad of your rubbish. The estate is dense with these small living encounters, each one a tiny piece of the same fiction, none of them a queue.

Threading the whole thing together is Pardoes, the park's wizard-jester, a purple sprite who turns up on signage, in shows and in the lore as a kind of master of ceremonies for the magic. He belongs to Efteling's wider invented mythology, the Fabula universe, in which the rides are not standalone thrills but chapters of one long story — the dreamflight, the flying Dutchman, the dragon-slaying Joris — each set somewhere on the same map. You do not need to know any of it to enjoy a day here. But the longer you stay, and the more of the park you read, the more it resolves into a single coherent place rather than a collection of machines.

That is the difference a return trip exposes. A single day buys the headline rides. A second day, with the evening still ahead, lets the world settle into focus — the lit cottages, the muttering animatronics, the hungry bin, the sense that you have walked into something that was complete before you got there and will carry on long after you leave.

Accommodation

A Castle You Sleep Inside

There is a hotel that looks like it floated up out of the lake and decided to stay. The Efteling Wonder Hotel opened in 1992 as the park’s first place to sleep over, closed in January 2025 for a ground-floor overhaul, and reopened that April rebranded as the Wonder Hotel — 124 rooms over eight floors, and a new identity built around one nightly piece of theatre.

The Floating Castle, Rebranded

The Wonder Hotel reads as a castle set down on the water — the floating-castle exterior issued with the 2025 rebrand.

Photo Efteling press kit
sleep inside the gates
DroomStoom in the lobby — the bedtime story you check in for

Each evening the lobby gathers for DroomStoom, a "dream steam" story told under the hotel's central tower as the day winds down. It is the cue that the theming does not stop at the park gates; here it follows you to the pillow. Staying inside the estate means the fairytale is the building, not just the day out.

Rooms, and a category kept secret

Two kinds of room cover most of the floors. Premium Rooms sleep three, four or five at around 29 square metres — the practical, well-themed default. Above them sit the non-themed Luxury Suites, roomier at about 53 square metres for five, with a separate sitting area. Then there is the part everyone books for: the themed suites, a category of around twenty individually decorated rooms, each dressed after an Efteling ride or fairytale. The range runs from Droomvlucht and Fata Morgana through Pardoes, Raveleijn and Roodkapje to Sprookjesboom and the Koningssuite, with newer themes added through 2025 and 2026. Which one you wake up in is a surprise — part of the booking, and not for spoiling here.

Restaurant Saga, and what the stay includes

Dining now centres on Restaurant Saga — the Saga Restaurant & Bar that anchors the renovated ground floor, themed to the park's fairytales and reading its menu out "by chapters". Breakfast there is included with the room, which matters more than it sounds on a morning when the gates are about to open. There is one thing the hotel deliberately does not have: a pool. Guests who want a swim use the Badhuys at neighbouring Bosrijk rather than anything on-site, so the Wonder Hotel keeps its focus on the castle, the story and the dining.

Why the inside wins

The real case for sleeping inside the gates is time. Hotel guests get a half-hour head start into the park before public opening, through an exclusive side entrance that empties straight into the park near Vogel Rok — what one Efteling guide calls simply "the shortest and easiest path to get into the park at the start of the day". Thirty minutes ahead of the crowd at Baron 1898 or Symbolica is thirty minutes a day-tripper never gets. The other half of the case is the evening: when the day-visitors file out, the Sprookjesbos lanterns are still lit, Aquanura plays out over the lake, and the walk back to the room runs through the park rather than away from it. Two nights buys both ends of the day a single ticket leaves on the table.

Part Two

Beekse Bergen

the open savannah · largest wildlife park in the Benelux · five nights

Five nights, not four. That is the change the second half of this trip turns on. Last time the savannah got four nights; this time it gets a fifth, and the difference is whole mornings spent doing nothing but watching. Where Efteling crowds its wonders together, Beekse Bergen lets the days spread out.

The park opened on 1 June 1968 as the drive-through Lion Park Beekse Bergen and took its present name in 1974. It is now the largest wildlife park in the Benelux, and the register could not be further from a theme park's. The pace is slower, the horizons longer, the animals close enough to set the clock by. Chloe Gallagher of The Travel Mum put the contrast plainly.

What follows in this half is the whole estate, all of it car-free: the safari walked and sailed, the conservation stories behind the names on the savannah, and the Safari Resort that puts the grass plain outside the window. Sixteen sleeps out, the wild is where this countdown ends.

More Than Driving Round the Animals

One estate, four parts, five nights — and not a car needed for any of it.

Far More Than the Drive

Four parts ring Lake Beekse Bergen, and the Attractions Pass that comes with the stay throws every gate open, unlimited, for the whole five nights.

Photo Beekse Bergen / Libema press
five nights, one pass
the ranger-driven bus, winding through summer woodland

The shorthand for Beekse Bergen is a car crawling past lions, but that is the one mode this trip skips. The estate is a great deal larger than its drive-through reputation — four parts gathered around one lake, the Victoriameer, and a Safari Resort stay hands you the lot. Resort guests get unlimited free access to the Safaripark and to Speelland for the entire stay, with further day attractions folded into the Attractions Pass. Five nights turns that from a ticket into a rhythm.

Start with the safari, because it does not need a car to work. The walking safari runs roughly 3.5 km past lions, elephants, giraffes and pelicans, moats standing in for fences wherever the sightlines allow. The boat safari sails the two ranger-narrated vessels, Livingstone and Stanley, between the restaurants on a thirty-minute loop past the rhino plain. The bus safari adds the guided, hands-on version — furs and horns passed down the aisle. Walked and sailed, the whole park is reachable on foot and on water, no key required.

Then there is the half that has nothing to do with animals at all. Speelland sits across the lake — a family adventure and water-play park aimed at children up to around twelve, all slides, splash decks and climbing. Its newest piece, Speelland Indoor, opened in December 2024: a 2,000 m² tropical indoor play world with room for up to 600, and the wet-weather card the estate never used to hold. A rained-off afternoon now has somewhere warm to go.

Lastly the lake and the Resort itself, which is no mere place to sleep between outings. The Victoriameer carries beaches; the lodges look out over the grass plain. Across five nights the four parts stop being a list to tick and start being a place to settle into — a savannah to walk one morning, water to splash through the next, a boat at dusk, a beach in between.

The Safari, Ranked

Six encounters worth the trip, built on the three car-free ways round the park — walking, boat and bus. No engine, no windscreen, just the moat between you and the lions. Last time was four nights; five gives the savannah room to slow down, and the ranking below leans on the modes that never need a car key.

The walking safari

“Trenches or moats are used in place of fences where possible, so you get a direct, uninterrupted view of the animals — they seem really close.” — Natalie Smith, Frugal Mum

Where
A self-guided 3.5 km route past lions, elephants, giraffes, penguins, ring-tailed lemurs and pelicans — the dependable, do-it-on-your-own-clock backbone of a car-free visit.
Why

Born on the savannah

The line that keeps going

Behind the grass plains and the boat that drifts past them, Beekse Bergen does the unglamorous, long-game work of keeping rare animals alive as a population, not a single lucky birth. The park coordinates two European breeding programmes — the EEP for the Southern African cheetah and the one for the Southern white rhino — and runs recovery work for species including Pere David's deer and the Przewalski's horse inside the wider EAZA framework. The names that come out of that work are the point. So are the dates.

The cheetah line

A female brought from France in late 2023 gave birth on 11 February 2024. Three cubs; two survived. That is how a healthy European population is built — one careful pairing at a time.

Programme EEP — Southern African cheetah Born 11 February 2024 Photo Beekse Bergen via DutchNews.nl
welpjes
one careful pairing at a time

Cheetahs are notoriously hard to breed in human care, which is why the matching matters more than the mating. The curator who oversees the programme, Lars Versteege, frames it as genetics first and sentiment never.

The rhino plain tells the same story in a heavier animal. A Southern white rhino calf — a male named Ravy, forty kilos at birth — arrived to a first-time mother, Sofie. White rhinos are not a tidy success anywhere; a calf raised well by an inexperienced mother is its own small triumph, and the park holds the studbook responsibility for the species across European collections. Ravy is the kind of birth that justifies the moats and the patience.

The longest thread runs through the African painted dogs — Lycaon pictus, down to a few thousand across the wild. In January 2022 a litter of pups was born to mother Imani and father Julius. Imani was no newcomer to the place: she was herself born here in 2013, in a litter of twelve, to a grandmother named Tuli. Three generations of one of the planet's most endangered carnivores, raised on the same patch of Brabant. Her keeper, Mariska Vermij-Van Dijk, kept the report plain.

The giraffes keep the ledger ticking over too — three Nubian calves born in January 2025, a species the wider programme is quietly trying to claw back. None of this is staged for the boat. The cheetahs the rangers point out as you sail past the rhino plain are the same animals the studbooks track by name and number, and the cubs of February 2024 are the reason the pointing still has something to point at.

Read together, the cheetah cubs, Ravy, Imani's pups and the giraffe calves are not exhibits that happen to breed. They are the breeding programme, walking around. It is the serious counterweight to a week of fairy tales next door — a place where the storytelling is true, the dates are real, and the animals have names.

Five nights

Safari Resort — Sleeping over the savannah

Most wildlife parks send you home at closing time. Beekse Bergen lets you stay the night inside the fence line, and that single fact reorganises the whole trip. Five nights at the Safari Resort means the savannah is not a day out but a view from the bed — and the animals that took an hour to reach by boat are grazing on the other side of the glass while the kettle boils. This is the return-trip draw, the reason a second visit runs to five nights instead of four.

The Savanne Lodges

The six-person Savanne Lodge and the larger eight-person Savanne Lodge Plus look straight out over the Serengeti, Masai Mara, Ngorongoro plains or the Simbara lion plain. From a private terrace you watch giraffes, zebras, ostriches, camels, antelope and rhinos go about their day — and some lodges have a giraffe feeding station built in beside them.

The lodge range runs wider than that. There are safari tents and kid lodges, group lodges for a crowd, two treehouses tucked into the planting, and hotel rooms over in Hogon House for anyone who wants a serviced stay rather than self-catering. But it is the lodges with the plain at the foot of the garden that returning guests remember. Reviewers keep landing on the same point.

Dining lives in Karibu Town, the resort's own small village, so a car never enters the picture. Restaurant Moto is the centrepiece — an African-style grill and buffet whose windows look out over the Serengeti, so dinner comes with the same animals you watched from the lodge. Around it sit the Baobab Café for daytime snacks, a Market for groceries and takeaway when a lodge night calls for cooking in, and the Pamoja Lounge, a bar with bowling lanes for the slow end of the evening. Pizza delivery reaches the lodges too. Over in the Safari Hotel's Hogon House, the Nommos and Amma restaurants round out the choice without anyone leaving the estate.

Before You Go

The homework, demoted to a coda — because a Countdown is about wanting to be there, not about spreadsheets. A handful of things worth knowing before the gates open, kept deliberately short. The whole week is onsite and car-free: Schiphol by train, a taxi to the first set of gates, and after that everything lives inside the two estates.

good to know

Five things the brochures bury

  1. Wonder Hotel guests step into the park thirty minutes before the public, through an exclusive side entrance that opens straight in near Vogel Rok — the quietest the park ever gets.
  2. At Beekse Bergen the boat safari is also transport: Livingstone and Stanley run between the Safari Restaurant and the Kongo Restaurant, so the ride to the far end doubles as a thirty-minute cruise past the rhino plain.
  3. A Safari Resort stay includes unlimited free access to both the Safaripark and Speelland for the entire visit — no day-by-day ticketing to think about.
  4. Where it can, the Safaripark replaces fences with moats, which is why the walking route gives lions and giraffes at uninterrupted, close range.
  5. The two boats are named for explorers, never for a bird — Livingstone and Stanley, if you want to know which one you boarded.

One flag for the trip window. The regular bus safari is suspended until 1 October 2026 and is taking group bookings only — which means it may not run for individual visitors across late June and early July. That leaves the walking safari and the boat safari as the dependable car-free ways round the park, and they cover it comfortably between them. Worth confirming the bus status nearer the date in case the schedule shifts.

Otherwise the logistics end here. Two estates, seven nights, no car, and a count still running down. Sixteen sleeps.

The chorus that already went

Two parks, seven nights, one castle and one savannah — and a whole chorus of people who have already made the trip. The Deacon family did both in a single run and called it “magical, quiet (compared to a UK August!) and the kids adored every second.” The countdown, for once, agrees with everyone.
