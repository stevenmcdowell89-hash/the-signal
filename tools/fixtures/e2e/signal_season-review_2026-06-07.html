The Season at a Glance

Nine months, thirty-eight rounds, one long-deferred answer

Some seasons ask a question. This one settled an argument that had been running for twenty-two years. Between the sixteenth of August and the twenty-fourth of May, twenty clubs played three hundred and eighty matches, and by the end of them the oldest open wound in English football had quietly closed. Arsenal are champions of England again, for the first time since the season nobody lost a game, and the strange thing — the thing this file keeps returning to — is how unlike a coronation it felt while it was happening.

It was, for most of its length, a three-team squabble conducted in bad weather. Manchester City led at Christmas. Liverpool led in February. Arsenal led when it mattered, which is the only ledger line anyone will remember in ten years, and led it by holding their nerve through a spring schedule that had broken them in each of the three previous seasons. The title was clinched on a Tuesday night, the nineteenth of May, with a game to spare.

The rest of the table told its own stories. Manchester City finished second and looked mortal for the first time in a decade. Liverpool's first full rebuild year under Arne Slot delivered third and a sense of something gathering. At the other end, two of the promoted three went straight back down — the yo-yo tightens every year — and Wolves fell through the trapdoor with them after eight managers-worth of drama compressed into one. All of it is graded, club by club, in the second half of this file.

Verdict
The wait ended; the order changed. Both halves of that sentence matter.

Grading a season fairly means holding two clocks at once. On the short clock, this was a title race decided by fine margins: fourteen one-goal wins, a single April head-to-head, a final gap that flattered the winners' comfort. On the long clock, it was a landslide two decades in the making — a club that had finished second four times in seven years finally finding the last ten per cent that separates nearly from actually. Both clocks are right. The file that follows tries to keep them both ticking, because a review that only tells you who won is just a table with adjectives.

One reading note before the file opens. Names, grades and verdicts here are committed, not hedged — that is the house style — but they are committed from the sofa, at broadcast distance, with the same evidence any careful viewer had. Where a judgement leans on a number, the number is shown. Where it leans on taste, the taste is signed. You are allowed to disagree; the file only asks that you disagree with something it actually said.

What follows is the season in five movements: how the title was actually won, the five dates it turned on, what happened to everyone chasing, the grades, the prizes, and — because a season is also a thing that happens to the person watching it — the nine months as they crossed your own Sunday mornings. Pour the coffee. This is a long one, and it earned it.

FILE 02

How It Was Won

The arc of Arsenal's title, August to the 19th of May

Every title has an official story and a true one. The official story of Arsenal's 2025-26 is simple: most points wins, they had the most points. The true story is about August, and about what this squad learned from three consecutive springs of watching someone else lift the thing.

Start in August, because that is where the points that won it in May were actually banked. Arsenal opened with seven wins from eight, and the eighth — a flat goalless draw at Goodison-in-exile — was received at the time as evidence that nothing had changed. What had changed was invisible in any single match: they had stopped dropping points to the bottom six. Across the whole season Arsenal took 58 points from the 60 available against the bottom ten. No champion in the three-points era has been so ruthless against the teams you are supposed to beat.

The middle of the season was where the old Arsenal would have wobbled, and the wobble duly arrived on schedule. Two wins from six across December, a home defeat to Manchester City that put City top at Christmas, and a familiar chorus warming up in the press boxes. The response was the season's real hinge, and it wore the captain's armband.

Rice's line about February was not bravado; it was a reference to a specific piece of squad engineering. In each of the three near-miss springs, Arsenal's title challenge had died in the same six-week corridor — the one where European knockouts, cup replays and the league's meanest away grounds arrive together. This year the corridor was planned for like weather. Rotation began in November, before anyone was tired; the medical staff were given veto power over selection for the first time; and two January signings were made not to improve the eleven but to protect it. None of this makes highlight reels. All of it shows up in the only place that matters, which is the fixture list's cruellest stretch turning into eighteen points.

From the second of January to the eleventh of April, Arsenal went fifteen league games unbeaten, and — this is the statistic that should hang in the training ground — won nine of them by a single goal. The transformation was not in the football, which was often no better than functional. It was in the arithmetic of close games. The 2024-25 side turned tight matches into draws; this one turned them into wins, week after grinding week, until the table stopped arguing.

The run-in, when it came, was almost anticlimactic — which is itself the achievement. Arsenal's previous near-misses all ended with April collapses that made May irrelevant. This time April was the month the lead grew. City dropped five points in two away games; Liverpool's young midfield finally hit the wall physics had promised all year; and Arsenal simply kept winning by one, a machine for converting nervous Sundays into three points. When Bukayo Saka opened the scoring against Newcastle on the nineteenth of May, the title had been arithmetically inevitable for a week and emotionally inevitable for a month.

Mikel Arteta's part in this deserves precision, because the easy version — vindicated visionary — misses what actually changed. Tactically the champions were less adventurous than the runners-up: fewer high turnovers, less of the choreographed build-up that made the 2023 side a neutral's delight. What Arteta built instead was a squad with no soft minutes. Nineteen players started five or more league games and the drop-off between first and second choice, position by position, was the smallest in the division.

There is also a tactical footnote that deserves better than a footnote. Arsenal scored nineteen set-piece goals, the most in the division, and conceded three — the fewest. That fourteen-goal swing is, in effect, the title margin arriving from dead balls alone, and it was built by a specialist department the club doubled in size eighteen months ago. When the history of this title is written properly, the corner-kick spreadsheet will get a chapter of its own. The romantics will hate it. The trophy will not care.

Is it a great champion? The points say very good rather than immortal; 89 wins most seasons and walks this one. But greatness in league football was never only about the number. It is about what the number ends. This one ended twenty-two years of a club flinching at its own reflection, and on that measure — the only one N5 was counting — it stands with any title of the era.

FILE 03

Five Moments the Season Turned On

The hinges: five dates the table never recovered from

A league season is eight months of noise wrapped around perhaps five genuine hinges — the dates after which the table stopped being one kind of story and became another. Here are this season's five, filed in order, each with the scoreline that made it and the consequence that followed it. The awards chapter will come back for several of them.

The September win at Anfield mattered less for the points than for the shape of the thing. Arsenal's three near-miss seasons all shared a signature: away games at direct rivals approached like dental appointments and exited pointless. Winning one in September — coming from behind to do it, with the away end still finding its seats — rewrote the internal weather. Players talk about these things obliquely, but the numbers don't: Arsenal took ten points from the away fixtures at last season's top six, up from two.

It is worth pausing on what made the Anfield afternoon different from the fixture's recent history, because the difference was structural rather than spiritual. Arsenal arrived without three first-choice players and simply absorbed it — the bench that afternoon carried more league starts than the bench of any previous away day at a rival in the Arteta era. When the equaliser came it was scored by a rotation player, and when the winner came it was created by another. The depth chart won that game before the teamsheet was printed, which is exactly the pattern the whole season would keep repeating.

Boxing Day was the season's false summit. City were emphatic, Arsenal were bullied in midfield for the first time all year, and every retrospective of the last three springs got another airing. What nobody clocked at the time: it was City's last statement performance of the season, and Arsenal's last league defeat until April. The table on the morning of the twenty-seventh of December — City first, Liverpool second, Arsenal third — would be exactly inverted by May.

Liverpool led the league for eleven weeks in the winter on the strength of the youngest midfield in the division, and the fourteenth of February was where the bill arrived. A goalless draw at Villa Park is nobody's idea of a crisis, but it began a run of two wins in seven in which the press stopped pressing and the sprints stopped arriving. Arne Slot never dressed it up as anything but what it was.

The return fixture against City was the night the season stopped pretending to be open. One-nil, a set-piece goal, twenty minutes of game management so unapologetic that the away end applauded it in the manner of one craftsman acknowledging another. Arsenal went top that evening and were never caught. It was also — the grades chapter will make this point less politely — the night City's season was revealed as a second-place season wearing a contender's coat.

The April head-to-head deserves one more paragraph of anatomy, because single-goal games between elite sides are usually decided by a mistake and this one was not. It was decided by a plan executed at full size: Arsenal conceded possession deliberately for the first half-hour, invited City's fullbacks sixty metres forward, and aimed every regained ball at the corridor they had vacated. The goal came from the game's fourth corner, which was itself the product of that corridor. Guardiola called the approach "brave in the way people never call defending brave," which is as close as that press room gets to a bow. Nothing about the evening was accidental, including the twenty minutes of game management that followed — the champions-elect treating a one-goal lead like a museum piece being carried down stairs.

And then the night itself, already narrated in the last chapter and destined to be narrated at every Arsenal wedding for a generation. What belongs in this file is the small detail the cameras nearly missed: at the whistle, before the fireworks, the entire squad walked to the north end and stood in a line facing the fans in silence for perhaps ten seconds. No badge-thumping, no cupped ears. Just — look. We did it. It was the gesture of a group that understood exactly how long twenty-two years is, because they had spent three of them being reminded weekly.

Five dates, five hinges. Every one of them will reappear in the awards chapter, because this magazine does not hand out prizes for vibes; it hands them out for moments you can point to on a calendar.

FILE 04

The Chasing Pack

City's stall, Liverpool's rebuild, and the race for the last Champions League place

Somebody has to finish second, and this year it was the club that had forgotten how. Manchester City's 84 points would have won four of the last ten titles; the fact that it felt like a collapse tells you what a decade of dominance does to a grading curve. The deeper worry for the Etihad is not the number but its shape — City took fewer points from the last ten games than anyone else in the top six, and for the first time in the Guardiola era the squad's age curve points the wrong way at its spine.

Liverpool's third is the most interesting result in the top half. Eleven weeks on top of the table with the youngest starting eleven in the division is a rebuild ahead of schedule, and the February fade — narrated in the moments chapter — reads less like a flaw than a to-do list: two experienced legs in midfield and this team is the 2026-27 favourite in a lot of sober models. The race behind them, for the last two Champions League places, went to the final Sunday and produced the campaign's best pure theatre.

The final Sunday's fourth-place drama was the fixture computer's accidental masterpiece. Villa, Chelsea and Newcastle began the day separated by two points, playing simultaneously in three different cities, and for one eleven-minute spell in the second halves the live table changed hands four times. Villa's winner at Brighton arrived in the seventy-ninth minute from their captain, at the end of a move their manager later admitted was a training-ground pattern they had not dared use all season. Chelsea did everything asked of them and finished fifth anyway, which is the cruellest sentence the league can write about a young team. Newcastle, playing at the champions' trophy party, got a point nobody will remember and a grievance about the calendar that the grades chapter takes seriously.

Manchester United's eighth needs its own paragraph, if only because eighth with this wage bill is a kind of dark achievement. The mid-season managerial change bought a two-month bounce and then the same team re-emerged, like a lake revealing an old church in a drought. Spurs finished seventh, which is the most Spurs sentence available. And at the bottom, the story was the trapdoor's new spring-loading: Burnley and Sunderland went down with a combined 51 points, and Wolves — eighteen years a Premier League fixture in two spells — followed them on the final day.

Six prizes, each pinned to a dated moment from the file

House rules: every award in this magazine is pinned to a moment with a date on it. No lifetime-achievement fudge, no vibes-based hardware. If a prize cannot point at a calendar square, it does not exist. Six awards this season, each one cross-referenced to the file.

Declan Rice takes the player award for the season the armband grew into a load-bearing structure — but the citation names the fourth of April, the set-piece goal and the seventy minutes of midfield governance that flipped the arithmetic for good. Arteta's citation is a date range rather than a date: from the morning after Boxing Day's beating to the eleventh of April, his team played fifteen league games without losing, nine of them won by a single goal. That run is the title. The young-player award goes, irregularly but unapologetically, to Liverpool's midfield as a collective — no individual among them was the story; the fact of an under-23 engine room leading the league for eleven winter weeks was.

Match of the season was played in March at Villa Park between two clubs chasing the same Champions League place: seven goals, four lead changes, and a winner in the ninety-fifth minute that ultimately decided fourth place by the single point it was worth. Goal of the season is Saka's September equaliser at Anfield — the outside-of-the-boot arc that started the away-day rehabilitation the whole title stood on. And signing of the season is Leeds' eighteen-million-pound holding midfielder, proof that the survival blueprint is a spine purchase, not a squad refresh.

Two awards were harder to place than the citations suggest, and honesty about the runners-up is part of the format. Rice's player-of-the-season case was pushed hard by two others: Villa's captain, whose final-day winner decided a Champions League place and whose season was arguably worth more per pound than anyone's; and Arsenal's own goalkeeper, whose eleven one-goal wins kept required a save-of-the-month in most of them. The file's tiebreaker is the fourth of April, the highest-leverage ninety minutes of the season, and the man who governed it. Manager of the season came down to Arteta against Leeds' survival architect, and reasonable files will split there — a title run of fifteen unbeaten against the best promoted campaign in five years. The citation goes with the run because of what it followed: three consecutive springs of the same collapse, planned against and eliminated. Repairing your own recurring failure is the hardest job in management, and this season's file finally has its proof.

The stranger awards deserve their sentence of defence too. A collective young-player award will annoy purists, but the eleven winter weeks Liverpool's under-23 midfield spent on top of this league were the most remarkable developmental fact of the season, and no single name owns it. And an eighteen-million-pound signing of the season, in a division that spent two billion, is the point rather than the anomaly: the market's best work is almost never its loudest.

Shearer's caveat is fair, and this file's answer is the cross-reference: every one of these six citations points back into the moments chapter, where the group photo is printed uncropped. That is what tying awards to moments is for.

The Season in Numbers

The charts the table was hiding

The table is a summary; the season lives in the distributions. Three exhibits this year, each one a thing the final standings were quietly hiding. Sources for every figure: the final published table and the league's own match data, compiled 24 May 2026.

Exhibit one is the title in a single bar: Arsenal dropped two points all season against the league's bottom half of the bottom half. The champions were not the best team against good teams — City took more points from the top-six mini-league. They were the least charitable team in England, and 89 points is what that buys.

Exhibit two needs reading against its own history. In the last title race this close, the eventual winners took the lead in the table for the final time in March; this year the decisive crossing came on the fourth of April, the latest title-deciding lead change in nine seasons. What the three lines hide is the character of each: City's is a plateau with a late sag, the line of a great team idling; Liverpool's is a steep climb into a February ceiling, the line of youth meeting physics; Arsenal's barely accelerates at all. It is the flattest championship line in the modern era — no surge, no slump, just a club-record run of identical weeks. Championships usually look like sprints somewhere. This one looks like a metronome.

The stat band above holds the season's three loudest single numbers, each with a story attached. Fourteen one-goal wins is a champions' record and the whole title in miniature. Haaland's twenty-seven was somehow both a personal down year and enough to win the golden boot by four — the striker class behind him is in generational transition, and next season's award is the most open in a decade. And fifty-one combined points for two of the relegated three is the number that turns a talking point into a governance question.

Exhibit three is the one the league's owners will be discussing all summer: the combined points of the two promoted clubs that went straight back down. The gap between the divisions has stopped being a slope. What that means for the competition's design — and for next August — is the business of the closing chapters.

A closing word on method, because numbers chapters attract suspicion and should. Everything above comes from two sources — the final published table and the league's own match-by-match data — and nothing above required a model. That is deliberate. The expected-goals conversation has its place, but a season review is a court of record, not of projection: what happened, weighed once, sourced twice. Where this file speculates, it says so out loud; where it grades, the inputs are printed beside the grade. The two artefacts below are for the pub, not the court.

The champions' summer question is succession-proofing a title squad without disturbing it — history says the year after a first title is the dangerous one, and the fixture calculus gets no kinder with a Champions League campaign that now carries a point to prove after Budapest. City's is whether the great squad rebuild happens in one window or two. Liverpool's is two midfield legs, as discussed. And the league's own question, the one the numbers chapter left on the table, is what to do about a promotion gap that has become a storey: the World Cup summer means a compressed window, and the newly promoted will have had the shortest preparation period in Premier League history.

Two structural changes deserve more than a cheat-sheet cell. First, the calendar: a World Cup final on the nineteenth of July followed by a league kick-off on the fifteenth of August gives the tournament's deepest-running internationals barely three weeks between a final and a first fixture. Every club with a large World Cup contingent — which is to say every club near the top of this file — will start the season managing fatigue they did not cause. Expect August tables that lie, and expect the smart Novembers to be built in June. Second, the money: the league's spending rules enter their first full season of the new assessment regime, and at least three of this file's top eight are operating within a margin their owners would once have considered a rounding error. The transfer window that follows a review is usually where the next review is decided. This one more than most.

Put the file away; August is eleven Saturdays off

Close the file gently. A season that ends a twenty-two-year wait does not need a grand summation; it needs the lights turned off respectfully. The table is settled, the grades are committed, the awards are pinned to their dates, and the forward file is open with next August's questions already breathing on it.

What lingers, weeks later, is not the arithmetic. It is the ten seconds after the whistle on the nineteenth of May — a line of players facing a stand in silence, twenty-two years wide. Football spends most of its energy shouting. The moments that survive are almost always the quiet ones.

Every long season leaves one unanswered question on the desk, and this one leaves a good one: was 2025-26 the start of something or the end of something? The case for the end: a decade-defining team finally aged in public, and the club that outlasted it did so with the oldest virtues in the book — depth, nerve, dead balls. The case for the start: the youngest midfield in the league led the table for eleven weeks, the best pound-for-pound season belonged to a club outside the traditional cartel, and the promoted-class results suggest the competition's whole shape is being renegotiated underneath the title race. Next May's file will rule on it. This one is content to have asked.

A last housekeeping line, because a file should say what it is. This review was compiled in the first week of June from the season's published record and the magazine's own weekly coverage; the grades are final, the awards are pinned, and the forward page will be held to account in a year. If any verdict here ages badly, next season's file will quote it back without mercy — that is the deal a review makes with its own future, and the only honest way to run one.
