The Trains That Refused to Die

Fifteen years ago the sleeper was a museum piece with a timetable. Now the couchettes are booked out by March, and the operators can't add carriages fast enough.

The revival has a date. In December 2016, Austria's ÖBB bought the sleeper carriages Deutsche Bahn no longer wanted and rebadged them Nightjet — a bet that a bed on rails could compete with a dawn flight and a hotel bill. The bet paid. ÖBB now runs the largest night-train network in Europe, carries around 1.5 million passengers a year on it, and has spent the decade since ordering entirely new stock rather than inheriting anyone's cast-offs.

The economics are stubborn — a sleeping car hauls a third of the passengers of a seated one and needs staff all night — which is why the routes that survive are the ones with a story as strong as their timetable: Vienna to Venice arriving with the lagoon at breakfast, the Highland sleeper splitting at Edinburgh in the small hours, three Cornish counties passing unseen before Penzance.

Act II

Down to the Sea

by the 10:04 from York

The English seaside resort was invented by a timetable. When the York & North Midland Railway reached Scarborough in 1845, a fishing town with a spa became, within a decade, the place an entire industrial county went to breathe. The Grand Hotel followed in 1867 — for a while the largest brick building in Europe, built to a calendar conceit: four towers for the seasons, twelve floors for the months, a room for every day of the year.

What survives is not nostalgia but rhythm. The tide still empties and refills the South Bay twice a day; the chip shops still calibrate their fryers to the arrival of the 12:52; and the funicular — the oldest in Britain still running, opened in 1875 — still does in ninety seconds what the cliff steps do in a bad fifteen minutes.

Four things the day-trippers walk past

  1. The Central Tramway, 1881 — ninety seconds of Victorian engineering, still on its original route up the cliff.
  2. Anne Brontë's grave in St Mary's churchyard, above the harbour, facing the sea she came here to take.
  3. The Rotunda, 1829 — one of the world's first purpose-built museums, a drum of local geology by William Smith's nephew.
  4. Low tide at Cornelian Bay, twenty minutes' walk south and usually empty even in August.
