SAGA · WESTMINSTER

Seven leaders, no election

Previously: nominations opened in the race to replace Keir Starmer, with Andy Burnham the clear front-runner — 322 of Labour's 403 MPs already behind him and on course to win unopposed.

Nominations closed on 15 July with Burnham on 379, about 94% — the largest share in a modern Labour leadership contest, past Gordon Brown's 88% in 2007 — and on 17 July he was declared leader with no opponent on the ballot. He is due to be appointed Prime Minister on Monday, replacing Starmer without a general election: Britain's seventh leader in a decade, and one more handover of the top job arriving with no public vote. It is an unusual route to Downing Street — the Mayor of Greater Manchester returned to the Commons through a by-election to make himself eligible, an outsider stepping into the job rather than climbing to it. Next: whether an unelected premier faces pressure to go to the country early, and how the "King of the North" holds off the Reform UK challenge.

SAGA · THE GULF

The ceasefire that wouldn't set

Previously: Washington had called the June understanding over, with Qatari mediators shuttling to Tehran to keep a diplomatic channel alive.

The truce is now firmly dead. The US declared it over on 8 July after Iranian attacks on commercial ships, and on 15 July said fresh strikes on Iranian targets were aimed at protecting vessels in the Strait of Hormuz — imposing new sanctions on Tehran alongside them. The 60-day window opened by June's Islamabad Memorandum, meant to settle Hormuz navigation, Iran's nuclear and missile programmes and sanctions, has collapsed, and traffic through the strait stays well down from the roughly 110 ships a day it normally carries. Next: whether any negotiating channel reopens, or the strikes and the shipping squeeze harden into a longer standoff.

SAGA · THE LEVANT

A step back from the border

Previously: cross-border exchanges between Israel and Lebanon had renewed, with strikes running on past the ceasefire's deadline.

The first concrete step down. On 15 July the US State Department reported that Israel had agreed to withdraw from the "pilot zones" it held in southern Lebanon, with the Lebanese army to take exclusive control of the ground it vacates — the clearest de-escalation in the sequence so far, even after Israeli strikes had continued past the earlier deadline. Next: whether the handover to the Lebanese army actually holds, and the quiet with it.
