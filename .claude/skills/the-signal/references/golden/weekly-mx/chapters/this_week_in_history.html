On 14 July 1965 — 61 years ago this week — a spacecraft the size of a small car crept to within 6,118 miles of Mars and did something no machine had ever done: it took a close-up photograph of another planet. Mariner 4 had left Cape Canaveral the previous November and spent nearly eight months crossing the dark between the two worlds. In the space of about 25 minutes over the Martian dayside it captured 21 images, storing them on a four-track tape recorder because the radio link home was far too slow to send pictures live. Then it kept going, past Mars and out into an orbit around the Sun, its job done.

What came back rewrote the planet. For centuries Mars had been the sky's great blank canvas — home in the popular imagination to canals, engineers, dying civilisations rationing their water across a drying globe. Mariner 4's pictures showed none of it. They showed craters: a cratered, cold, Moon-like surface, pocked and ancient. The onboard instruments were just as blunt about it. Surface atmospheric pressure came in below 1% of Earth's, far too thin for liquid water or for the lush Mars that novelists and a good many scientists had half-expected. In a single afternoon of data, the romantic Mars quietly died.

The most human moment came before the computers had even finished. The picture data arrived as a long strip of numbers, each one describing how bright a point on Mars should be. Rather than wait for the processing to render it as a proper image, impatient staff at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory pinned up a printed strip of those raw numbers, bought pastel crayons from an art shop, and hand-coloured each value like a paint-by-numbers — yellow for the brightest, browns and reds for the dark. The result, framed and hung at JPL, was the true first picture of Mars: not a photograph at all, but a colouring made by hand from telemetry, because the people who built the machine could not bear to wait for it.

The same mid-July week carries a run of other beginnings. On 16 July 1969, Apollo 11 lifted off from Florida to carry the first humans to the Moon. On 17 July 1955, Disneyland opened its gates in Anaheim, California, on a chaotic first day its own staff later nicknamed "Black Sunday". And on 19 July 1848 — 178 years ago today — the Seneca Falls Convention opened in upstate New York, the first women's rights convention in the United States, where the Declaration of Sentiments demanded, among much else, the vote. But it is Mariner 4 that lingers. It flew past Mars once, sent home two dozen grainy frames, and in doing so turned a planet of stories into a place — cratered, thin-aired, real — that every rover since has been sent to read more closely.
