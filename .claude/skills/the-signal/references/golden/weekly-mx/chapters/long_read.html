SEVEN LEADERS, NO ELECTION

How Britain hands over power

On Monday, Andy Burnham is expected to walk into Downing Street as prime minister. He won 94 percent of his party's nominations and not a single vote from the public. Nothing about that breaks a rule. The unease comes from how routine it has become — the seventh handover in a decade, and the fourth with no election at all.

BY THE EDITOR · THE LONG READ · 19 JUL 2026

On Friday 17 July, Andy Burnham was declared leader of the Labour Party. He had no opponent. When nominations closed two days earlier, on 15 July, he held 379 of his party's MPs behind him — about 94 percent of the parliamentary party, the largest share of nominations any Labour leadership contest has recorded, ahead of the 88 percent Gordon Brown drew in 2007. On Monday 20 July he is expected to be appointed prime minister, replacing Keir Starmer, whom a party rebellion forced out earlier this month. Between the rebellion and the door of Number 10, the British public was not asked to vote once.

This is worth stating slowly, because it surprises people every time it happens. Britain does not elect its prime minister. Voters elect 650 members of the House of Commons, one for each constituency, and no ballot paper anywhere in the country names the person who will run it. The prime minister is appointed by the monarch, and by long convention the monarch appoints whoever can command the confidence of the Commons — in practice, the leader of the largest party. "Command confidence" has a concrete meaning: it is the ability to win a no-confidence vote and to get a budget through the House. Whoever can reliably do those two things can govern. When the largest party changes its leader, that person inherits the confidence of its MPs, and so inherits the office. No national vote is required, because no national vote handed out the office in the first place.

That is why Starmer's fall and Burnham's rise can play out inside a single fortnight in July, with Parliament doing the choosing and the electorate watching. Labour won the last general election, in 2024, with Starmer at its head. The mandate it won belongs to the party, not to the man — the ballot in each seat named a local candidate under a party banner, not a prime minister. The party is therefore entitled to change the man without returning to the country. King Charles will invite Burnham to form a government on Monday for the same reason he invited Starmer two years ago: because Burnham is now the person who can hold a Commons majority together.

The handover itself is almost startlingly quick. On the day, Starmer travels to the Palace and formally tenders his resignation to the King. Burnham follows, is asked to form a government, and accepts — the old ceremony still described as "kissing hands." He walks out a prime minister the same afternoon. There is no transition period, no confirmation hearing, no interregnum. The country does not spend a day without a head of government, and it does not spend a minute asking the country's permission. The seamlessness is the whole design: the point of the system is that power never drops, that there is always someone who commands the Commons, and that the change of face costs the machinery of state nothing. What the design does not price in is what it costs the public's sense that it chose any of this.

Even inside Labour, the choice was made narrowly. Because Burnham gathered the nominations of 379 MPs, no rival could clear the threshold to force a contest, and the leadership passed to him uncontested. There was no ballot of Labour's wider membership, no hustings, no vote of the hundreds of thousands of people who pay the party's subscriptions. The man about to become prime minister was chosen by 379 colleagues in the House of Commons. The country did not vote. His party's members did not vote. His fellow Labour MPs settled it among themselves inside a week. Burnham plainly commands his party; the point is how few people the rules require to make that judgment.

None of this is new, and that is the part that has begun to trouble people. Burnham will be the seventh person to lead the country in ten years. The line runs David Cameron, Theresa May, Boris Johnson, Liz Truss, Rishi Sunak, Keir Starmer, and now Burnham — seven prime ministers across a decade that ought to hold two or three. By CNN's count, four of the most recent handovers happened with no general election at all: the party in power changed its leader, and the country changed its prime minister as a side effect. Truss lasted weeks rather than years before her own party replaced her with Sunak, again without the public casting a ballot. The machinery worked exactly as designed each time. The frequency is what has changed.

The template is not even unusual for Labour. Gordon Brown walked the same path in 2007, succeeding Tony Blair mid-term, uncontested, on 88 percent of nominations and without a general election — the precedent Burnham's 94 percent has now surpassed. The mechanism is old and settled. The tempo is the new thing: what used to happen to a country once in a decade now happens to Britain most years.

Burnham reaches Number 10 by a crooked path even by these standards. For most of the drama he was not a member of Parliament at all. Since 2017 he has been the mayor of Greater Manchester, a job he left the Commons to take, and one he used to build a national profile as a regional power broker — "the King of the North," as the label stuck. A mayor cannot be prime minister, and a prime minister must sit in the Commons. So to make himself eligible, Burnham first had to get back into Parliament, which he did by winning the Makerfield by-election and reclaiming a seat in the House he had left years earlier. He campaigned for the leadership of the whole country, in effect, before he held the constitutional standing to take it, and acquired the standing on the way.

There are two honest ways to read all of this, and they do not sit comfortably together. The first is the reading CNN's analysis reached for when Burnham was declared: a governing party burning through leaders is a party consuming itself, and a country that keeps acquiring prime ministers it never chose is running a democratic deficit. On this account the serial handover is a symptom. Starmer's government was floundering; his own MPs decided a change of face was quicker than a change of course or a change of government; and so they installed a new leader over the summer rather than face the voters. The people who backed Labour in 2024 voted for one leader and one prospectus, and by the middle of 2026 they have neither, without having been consulted. Repeat that pattern often enough and the thread connecting what the public votes for to who ends up governing them starts to fray. A prime minister governs with enormous latitude — over war, spending, the shape of the state — and the argument runs that such power should trace back, at some visible point, to a decision the electorate actually made. Four mid-term handovers in a decade stretch that line of accountability until it is hard to see where the public's choice ends and the party's begins.

The second reading takes the same facts and finds them reassuring. A parliamentary system is built precisely so that a failing leader can be removed between elections without the state seizing up. When a chief executive loses the confidence of the people around them, the country swaps the driver and keeps moving; it does not have to crash the car or wait years for a scheduled vote to change direction. Al Jazeera reported Burnham's arrival in exactly these flat terms — a leadership contest resolved, a successor confirmed, a government continuing — rather than as a constitutional crisis. Nothing broke. The Commons still holds a Labour majority; that majority still commands confidence; the person it puts forward becomes prime minister. This is the system doing the exact job it exists to do, quickly and without upheaval, which is no small thing in a century that has watched other countries manage the same task far worse.

Both readings describe the same seven prime ministers. They disagree about what to call the pattern, and the bare facts will not settle the argument for you. What the constitution treats as a routine transfer, a decade of repetition turns into a question about legitimacy — and the two do not cancel out, because a system can be working exactly as designed and still be reporting that something has gone wrong upstream, in the politics the design was never built to fix.

What Burnham inherits makes the argument more than academic. He takes over a government that CNN's analysis described as struggling, with the hard-right Reform UK pressing Labour from the flank and the standing question that hangs over every unelected prime minister: how long can you govern on a predecessor's mandate before the public demands its own say? Sunak carried Boris Johnson's 2019 mandate for the better part of two years, then lost the election he eventually called — the vote that put Starmer in and set this whole chain running toward Monday. Burnham's pitch, trailed as a promise to "restore hope," assumes he can do what most of the leaders before Starmer could not: steady a party in office and win the argument with the country before the country insists on being asked. He arrives with the largest nomination mandate in his party's modern history and the smallest democratic mandate a prime minister can hold, which is none. Whether he can reconcile those two things is the story of the government that begins this week.

On Monday, then, a man almost no voter chose will take the most powerful job in the country, entirely lawfully, to run a government elected under someone else. The rules will be followed at every step. The King will act on convention, the Commons will hold its majority, and the machine will turn over smoothly, the way it is built to. The thing Burnham cannot fix by being competent is the question the whole decade keeps raising: at what point does a system that lawfully keeps handing power to people the public did not pick stop looking like democracy working and start looking like democracy being worked around? He has until the next election, whenever he chooses to call it, to argue that the answer is still the first one.
