    Heartstopper bows out with a film, not a fourth season

    Heartstopper Forever, the feature-length send-off to Netflix's teen-romance series, arrived this week and draws a line under Nick and Charlie after three seasons. Rather than stretch to another full run, the show chooses a single film — a decision that lets the ending play as a proper valediction instead of one more cliffhanger. The consensus among reviewers is that it grows up with its cast: the writing follows the characters into thornier, more adult territory, and it reaches for closure rather than another hook. The Guardian's Owen Myers, in a three-star notice picked up by Variety, met the recurring complaint that the series is simply too nice head-on:

    It is a fair defence of a show whose gentleness was always the point — the argument that its warmth is a feature, not a flaw, is roughly the whole case for watching it. That is also the balance the finale has to strike: sending off a young cast the audience has watched grow up without either dodging the harder edges of adulthood or losing the tenderness that made the series matter in the first place. The verdict this week is that it manages it. For anyone who followed the series from its first, quietly radical season, the week of 13-19 July is when it signs off — on its own terms, and on a note the reviews read as a mature swan song rather than a franchise clinging on for one more lap.     A comfort-viewing week around the finale

    The Heartstopper film headlines a busier-than-usual streaming week. The Walking Dead: Daryl Dixon returns for its third season, the spin-off that has quietly become one of the more durable branches of that universe still finding new ground. Alongside it, Netflix brings The Hawk, a Will Ferrell golf comedy, and a third season of Quarterback, the NFL-access docuseries that trails the league's starting passers through a full campaign. It makes for a slate that leans hard toward comfort viewing — an apocalypse drama, a broad sports comedy and a locker-room documentary, three very different registers of undemanding company for the back half of July. None of them is reinventing its form, but that is rather the appeal: on a Sunday when the two biggest live events of the week — the World Cup final and the Belgian Grand Prix — are the appointment viewing, the streaming schedule is stacked with the kind of releases that ask nothing more than an evening on the sofa. The returning genre draw anchors it; the two lighter arrivals round it out. Between them there is enough breadth here that a quiet night in is well covered whichever way the mood runs, from post-apocalyptic tension to broad comedy to sport served as documentary.
