Modern Britain runs on old machinery — a monarch appointing whoever can command the Commons, a mechanism older than Parliament itself. But medieval England survives in stranger places than the constitution. It survives inside the word tawdry. Cheap, gaudy, flashy in a way that gives poor taste away — the word is a fossil, and buried inside it is a real person: a 7th-century Anglo-Saxon princess named Æthelthryth. English tongues softened the name to Etheldreda, then to Audrey, and finally to Saint Audrey. She was the daughter of an East Anglian king, married twice for reasons of state — the second time to Ecgfrith, king of Northumbria — and by tradition kept a vow of chastity through both marriages before leaving the world for religion. Around 673 she founded the abbey at Ely, out on the low island in the Cambridgeshire fens whose name means "eel island," and ruled it as abbess until her death.

According to the account left by the Venerable Bede, Æthelthryth died in 679 of a tumour in her throat, and she met the pain as penance. As a young woman she had loved fine necklaces, and she took the swelling at her neck as fair repayment for the vanity of all those jewels once clasped around her throat. It is the detail that outlived everything else about her. She was made a saint; Ely grew into a cathedral and a place of pilgrimage; and her feast days — 23 June, when she died, and 17 October, when her relics were moved — pulled in crowds year after year. By the Middle Ages the fair held in her honour at Ely had become one of the great trading events of the region.

Where crowds gather, sellers follow. At the annual St Audrey's fair at Ely, traders sold cheap lace and silk neckwear — collars, ribbons, scarves for the throat — pitched at pilgrims who wanted a keepsake and had little to spend. This finery was known as "St Audrey's lace," a nod to the saint and, pointedly, to the very necklaces that had supposedly killed her. Said quickly and carelessly across generations, "Saint Audrey" simply wore down in ordinary speech. The s of "Saint" slid onto "Audrey," the syllables collapsed inward, and "St Audrey's lace" became "tawdry lace."

By the 17th century the erosion was complete and the word had slipped its origins entirely. The lace itself carried a reputation — showy, cheaply made, the kind of thing sold at a fair to people who didn't know better — and "tawdry" broke free of the neckwear to describe anything with that quality. It no longer needed the lace, or the fair, or the saint. Today it means cheap and gaudy, flash without worth, and almost nobody who reaches for it knows they are invoking an Anglo-Saxon abbess who blamed her own jewellery for her death. It is one of the very few English words descended from a saint's name — and the saint is the part that vanished. What survives, lodged inside the word we use to name it, is the vanity she spent her last breath repenting.
