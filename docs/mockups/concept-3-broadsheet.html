The Front Page · Westminster

A Coronation,
No Crowd

Britain is about to change prime minister — and not a single ballot will be cast to do it.

Andy Burnham, Labour’s only declared candidate, is on course to walk into Downing Street entirely uncontested — a handover of the highest office decided not at the ballot box but in the quiet arithmetic of a party with no one left to say no.

It is the sort of transfer of power that constitutions permit and democracies rarely relish: legitimate, orderly, and strangely silent. There will be no rosettes, no count read out after midnight, no losing candidate’s gracious concession. Only a name, and a date, and a car pulling into a familiar black door.

Supporters call it unity. Critics call it a coronation. The country, for now, mostly calls it Tuesday — and turns the page to the tennis.

From the Editor

A quiet week that wasn’t

On coronations without crowds, champions without warning, and the strange comfort of a Sunday that asks nothing of you but attention.

I keep coming back to the silence of it. A country changes its prime minister this week and the loudest sound is a tennis ball. There is something almost restful in that — power moving without a fight — and something that should keep us awake, too.

So I’ve set the two side by side. The coronation with no crowd, and the fortnight of crowds that roared: Wimbledon’s two first-time champions, a World Cup narrowing to four, a red car winning at home. One story is about consent we forgot to give. The others are about the joy of being surprised.

Read them together with your coffee. Notice which one makes you sit up. That, I think, is the week telling you something.

— The Editor
